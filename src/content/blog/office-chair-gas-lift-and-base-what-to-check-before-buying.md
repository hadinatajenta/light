## Quick answer

- Most office chair cylinders sit in the base on a 50 mm tapered fitting, often called a Morse taper, which is why so many cylinders are interchangeable. Fitting is not the same as matching your seat height.
- Stroke is the travel between the lowest and highest seat position. A 100 to 140 mm stroke suits most office environments and users.
- Class 3 under DIN 4550 — the German standard that sorts gas cylinders into four classes — is the stated minimum for commercial chairs used 8 hours or more a day by multiple people.
- Never open, heat, or force a sealed gas lift. Measure the chair as it stands, starting from its model number.

A chair that sinks by mid-morning, or one that will not drop low enough for your legs, usually gets blamed on the gas lift. Seal degradation is the most frequent cause of a sinking chair, the base and the castors can imitate the same symptoms, and often the cylinder is fine while the sizing is wrong. The order that works: identify the failure, measure the assembled chair, then decide whether the money goes on a cylinder, a base, or a different chair.

## Why does your chair sink, wobble, or drop when you sit down?

Separate the symptoms before you shop. A chair that creeps down through the day points at the seals. A chair that flexes, rocks, or tips points at the base: a US Consumer Product Safety Commission recall covers height-adjustable swivel office chairs whose base can bend, creating a fall hazard, with a refund remedy attached. That is structural rather than cosmetic, and not something to live with.

Inside the cylinder, manufacturer documentation puts the nitrogen charge at roughly 100 to 150 bar, sealed at the factory. Nothing in there is serviceable, and forcing the mechanism is how people get hurt.

## How do you measure a gas lift on a chair you have not taken apart?

Manufacturer guidance is blunt: never drill, cut, heat, open, or manually force a sealed gas lift through its travel. Measure the chair as it stands.

1. Find the model number. It can resolve fit details that a ruler cannot see, and it is the first thing a parts supplier asks for.
2. Set the chair to the lowest height you can use. Sit normally to settle it, stand up, then measure the unloaded chair without touching the height control.
3. Raise it to the highest setting you can use and measure the same way.
4. Subtract the lowest figure from the highest. That difference is the stroke your chair needs, and it is the number to quote when you order.

The example below shows a chair measured at both ends of its travel.

| Measurement | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Lowest usable seat height | 260 mm |
| Highest usable seat height | 400 mm |
| Stroke | 140 mm |

Step 2 matters more than it sounds, because the seat has to come down far enough for your feet to rest flat. A cylinder that cannot reach your lowest usable seat height is the wrong cylinder, whatever class it carries.

Wheel size changes every measurement above, so measure with the wheels you intend to keep. Castor supplier documentation gives the added seat height for the two common upgrades.

| Wheel size | Added seat height (mm) |
| --- | --- |
| 65 mm castor | +15 |
| 75 mm rollerblade wheel | +25 |

Those figures are approximate, and they are cumulative with everything else in the stack. A 75 mm wheel swap can lift the seat by about as much as a taller cylinder would, which is why a chair can suddenly feel too high after a wheel upgrade alone.

## What does a gas lift class number actually tell you?

DIN 4550 is the classification the furniture industry works from, and it splits gas cylinders into four classes. The published figures for the two classes most office chairs are sold under are below.

| Class | Wall thickness (mm) | Cycle rating | Stated load (kg) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Class 3 | 2.0 | ~100,000 | |
| Class 4 | 2.5 | ~200,000 | ~150 |

The load column is where sources disagree, and it is worth knowing that before you compare listings. A chair parts supplier states that its Class 4 lift is rated up to 250 kg and describes Class 3 as suitable to 150 kg, figures that conflict with the class table and with each other. That is why the Class 3 load cell above is left blank rather than guessed. Treat load claims as manufacturer statements to verify, not interchangeable facts. What stays consistent is the direction of travel: a higher class means a thicker tube wall and a longer rated cycle life.

For a chair shared by several people for 8 hours a day, Class 3 is the stated minimum for commercial use. A single home user at a few hours a day is buying durability headroom with Class 4 rather than a better fit.

The check with more value is certification: ask whether the cylinder carries SGS or equivalent third-party testing rather than a rating the seller wrote for itself. One chair brand states that documented gas cylinder incidents trace back to cheap, uncertified cylinders built without a recognised safety standard. That is a manufacturer's claim, but it lines up with the general advice to buy certified parts.

One caveat applies to every number in this section. A chair's weight rating is not a sizing guide, as an office furniture dealer has argued: a high rating does not mean the chair fits a person of that weight.

## Will a replacement cylinder fit your chair, and will the seat height stay the same?

Two cylinders can both drop into the same sockets and still leave the chair at noticeably different seat heights. That is the part buyers miss. The taper gets you fit; the overall length and the stroke decide the height.

Most office chair gas lifts connect to the base through a 50 mm tapered fitting, often called a Morse taper, which is why so many cylinders are interchangeable. A chair parts supplier describes universal cylinders as using a 50 mm outer tube and a 28 mm inner tube. Heavy-duty versions use larger internal components and different wall thicknesses, and quoted capacities run from 350 up to 1,000 pounds.

If the chair's maker still supplies the model, a matched part removes most of the guesswork. If it does not, measure overall length as well as stroke, and plan to re-check seat height rather than assuming the taper settles it.

## What should you check in the base and the castors?

A five-star base is a structural part, so inspect the legs for cracks, for flex under load, and for deformation where the cylinder socket meets the centre hub. A bending base is the failure mode the CPSC recall describes, and it calls for replacement rather than repair.

The socket is standard at the 50 mm taper, but the base is built around your chair's hub and leg geometry, so confirm a replacement is listed for your model before ordering.

Castor size deserves the same care for a different reason: because wheel diameter shifts seat height, a wheel change can take you outside the range you measured. Wheel swaps are often the simplest height adjustment available, though. If the chair sits a little high and the cylinder is already at the bottom of its travel, check smaller wheels and different wheel profiles before buying a cylinder.

## How does cylinder choice connect to your desk height and your posture?

Seat height only works if it lines up with the desk above it. Set the seat first so your feet rest flat and your thighs are supported, then bring the desk to your elbows. When a fixed desk forces the chair upward, a footrest is usually a better answer than a taller cylinder, because you otherwise lose floor contact. ## Buying checklist: what to confirm before you order

- Chair model number, written down first.
- Lowest and highest usable seat height, measured on the unloaded chair with the wheels you intend to keep.
- Required stroke, the difference between those two figures, checked against the 100 to 140 mm that suits most users.
- Fitting: 50 mm tapered socket, with universal cylinders at 50 mm outer and 28 mm inner tube.
- Overall length, not just the taper, since two cylinders that both fit can sit at different heights.
- Class and certification: Class 3 as the stated minimum for shared, full-day commercial use, plus SGS or equivalent third-party certification.
- Base condition: no flex, cracks, or deformation, and a model-matched replacement where there is any doubt.
- Weight rating understood as a durability figure rather than a fit guide.

## If the free fixes are not enough

This table maps the problem you can describe to the part that answers it.

| Situation | What answers it |
| --- | --- |
| Chair sinks gradually through the day | Replacement cylinder, third-party certified, matched on stroke and overall length |
| Seat will not drop low enough for your legs | Cylinder with a shorter minimum length, or a smaller wheel set |
| Cylinder fits the socket but seat height shifts | Cylinder matched on overall length rather than taper alone |
| Base flexes, cracks, or tips under load | Model-matched replacement five-star base |

If no matched part is available for the model, re-measure the assembled chair before ordering anything universal, and expect to re-check seat height once it is fitted.
