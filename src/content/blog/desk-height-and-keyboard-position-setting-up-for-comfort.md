## Quick answer

- Set the chair first, then the desk: elbows at worktop level, forearms level or sloping slightly down, wrists straight.
- The keyboard belongs where your elbows land with your upper arms hanging and your elbows bent to about 90 degrees, measured in your own chair.
- A fixed desk at 28 to 30 in (71 to 76 cm) suits most people between about 5 ft 8 in and 6 ft; outside that range you need a keyboard tray, a footrest or an adjustable frame.
- Fit also depends on the gap from the floor to the lowest obstruction under the desk, and on both your seated and your standing elbow heights, not just on the top of the frame's range.

Most desk-height advice stops at "buy an adjustable desk". The real question has two parts: how high your hands need to be, and how much clearance your legs need underneath. Get both right and the keyboard, the monitor and the lamp stop fighting each other for the same few centimetres.

## How high should your desk be for typing?

Take one measurement, in your own chair. Sit with your feet supported, let your upper arms hang, and bend your elbows to roughly 90 degrees. Measure from the floor to the underside of your elbow. That is your seated elbow height, and it is where a keyboard used directly on the worktop belongs.

Three details matter alongside the height: elbows slightly open rather than pinched square to the body, forearms level or angled down a little, and wrists straight. Wrist extension — bending the hand upward at the wrist — is what a worktop a few centimetres too high produces across a working day.

Raising the whole desk to meet your elbows is only half the fix. The other half is whether your legs still fit underneath, which is why the seat comes first.

## Why does your seat height decide the desk height?

Because the worktop sits a fixed distance above your seat, and your seat has to sit at your popliteal height — the distance from the floor to the crease behind your knee, measured with your foot flat and your knee at a right angle.

Set the seat there and your thighs carry your weight without pressure behind the knee. Then measure the distance from the seat surface up to the underside of your elbow and add it to the seat height. That sum is your ideal worktop height, and it rarely matches what is on the shop floor.

Run the arithmetic before you buy. If your popliteal height is 42 cm and your elbow sits 24 cm above the seat, the worktop needs to be about 66 cm. A 29 in desk is 73.7 cm, so the keyboard lands roughly 7.7 cm higher than your forearms want. You have three ways out: drop the chair by 7.7 cm and support your feet at that height, keep the chair and move the keyboard down, or buy a desk that descends far enough.

Dropping the chair is free, but only if your feet still have something to rest on. If the lower seat leaves them dangling, an adjustable under-desk footrest restores support without touching the chair. If you would rather leave the chair alone, a clamp-on tray such as the VIVO KB05E keyboard tray puts the keyboard below the worktop so the desk can stay at a height your legs tolerate. Either route keeps the seat as the reference point, which is also how [sizing an office chair](/blog/chair-seat-depth-and-height-office-chair-sizing/) works.

## How do you compare sit-stand height ranges?

This table shows what the main standards and guidelines allow for worktop height.

| Standard | Setting | Height range (mm) |
|---|---|---|
| EN 527-1 | Fixed, seated | 720 to 760 |
| EN 527-1 | Sit-stand, top of range | Up to 1250 |
| ANSI/BIFMA G1 | Sit-stand | 559 to 1181 |
| ADA 902 | Work surface, covered facilities | 711 to 865 |

The bands do not line up. A frame engineered to the top of the North American range sits 69 mm below the top of the European sit-stand band, so the same desk can pass one specification and fail a tender quoting the other. Two cautions when you use the table. ANSI/BIFMA G1-2013 is a guideline (its front matter says the dimensions are recommendations, not requirements, and BIFMA sells the document rather than publishing it), so that sit-stand figure usually reaches you through a manufacturer's conformance sheet. And ADA 902 (the Americans with Disabilities Act 2010 Standards, section 902) applies to work surfaces in covered facilities and their accessible portions, not to every desk in a private office.

The practical use is simpler than the standards debate. Find the lowest seated height your elbows need and the highest standing height your elbows need, then check both against the frame range. A frame that reaches the top of the European sit-stand band but does not descend far enough to meet a short user's seated elbows is the wrong buy, and the bottom number is the one listings tend to bury. On whether to stand at all: a 2025 scoping review of 17 studies found improved movement patterns in seven and better mental health outcomes in four, but its authors concluded the evidence is too sparse for robust conclusions. Treat the range as a fit question, not a health claim.

## What does the load rating on a height-adjustable desk actually mean?

Height-adjustable desks carry two capacity figures. Static capacity is the load the desk holds while stationary, typically under evenly distributed conditions. Dynamic capacity is the lower load the motors can lift reliably, and it runs 20 to 30 percent below static. Dynamic is the one that applies every time you press the button with monitors, a lamp and a tray already up there.

If a listing claims ANSI/BIFMA testing, ask which test and who ran it. The X5.5 vertical adjustment test for height-adjustable desks runs 5,010 cycles with the load distributed unevenly across the surface, which is a much harder exercise than one clean lift. But BIFMA participation is voluntary, so an untested desk has not failed anything; it was never submitted. BIFMA also treats a first-party conformance claim, supported by the manufacturer's own verification results, as generally adequate proof of compliance, so a compliance claim does not guarantee an independent laboratory was involved. Where a desk contains electrical components, BIFMA additionally requires those parts to be tested to Underwriters Laboratories (UL) standards. For scale, roughly $46 billion of office furniture sells in the US each year and only about 40 percent comes from contract manufacturers whose products are ANSI/BIFMA certified.

If wobbly typing at standing height is your actual complaint, the checks are different and shorter; see [standing desk stability](/blog/standing-desk-stability-what-to-check-before-buying/).

## What under-desk clearance should you ask about before buying?

Knee clearance — the clear space under a work surface for your legs and chair — is where a desk that looks correct on paper fails. The table below gives the minimums under section 306 of the ADA Standards for an accessible work surface.

| Measurement | ADA minimum |
|---|---|
| Clear height to lowest obstruction | 685 mm (27 in) |
| Clear width | 760 mm (30 in) |

The figure that decides whether a desk fits you is the clear height from the floor to the lowest obstruction underneath: the apron, the crossbar or the cable tray. Suppliers rarely publish it. On a height-adjustable frame that obstruction moves with the desk, so ask for the clearance at your seated height, not the clearance at the top of the range.

## How do you keep the keyboard and the screen at the right heights at the same time?

Once the keyboard is at elbow height, the monitor usually is not. A worktop set for your elbows tends to leave the screen low, and a screen at the right height tends to push the worktop too high for your hands. Stop asking one surface to do both jobs.

Screen height is its own adjustment: top of the display at or slightly below eye level, at roughly arm's length. If a keyboard tray or a lowered chair has taken the worktop down with it, a monitor arm such as the HUANUO FlowLift gas-spring monitor arm raises the screen independently of the desk. Check the rated weight and the mounting pattern before ordering: an arm sized for a compact panel will not carry a large ultrawide.
