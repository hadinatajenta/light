## Quick answer

- Weigh the tower as built, then compare that number with the mount's published capacity. Case size tells you little about load.
- Treat the published capacity as a ceiling, not a target, and remember the desktop has its own limit.
- Leave a measured gap on every side of the case so air can move.
- Check that your tower's height matches one of the mount's fixed settings.
- Measure knee clearance at seated height, and floor clearance at the desk's lowest position, before you drill.

An under-desk PC mount gets the tower off the carpet and out of the footwell, which is worth doing. It also hangs a loaded steel box from a panel most people have never thought about as a structural component. The decision comes down to two published numbers that listings tend to bury: the weight the bracket is rated to hold and the space the case needs around it. Padding and finish are secondary.

What follows assumes you are choosing between two mounts, or between a mount and leaving the tower on the floor. The aim is to show which figures actually constrain the purchase and where the desk, not the bracket, is the weak part.

## The numbers to check before drilling

The table below combines the documented specifications for the example steel hanger-style mount with the checks that decide whether it will fit your desk and tower.

| Check | Documented value or target | Pass condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loaded tower weight | Compare with 66 lb capacity | At or under rating |
| Tower height | Fixed settings: 14, 16, 18, 20, 22 in | Matches one setting |
| Side clearance | 2 to 3 in on every side | Gap unobstructed |
| Knee clearance | Measure when seated | Legs swing clear |
| Desk panel | Solid material at bolt points | Bolts fully engaged |
| Case protection | Padding only | No anti-vibration or locking listed |

## How much does your tower actually weigh?

Weigh the machine as configured: case, power supply, graphics card, drives, CPU cooler, fans, and any brackets. A household scale is enough to get a working number. Compare the total with the mount's published capacity, not with its stated case-size range.

The documented example's capacity is a manufacturer figure, not an independently tested limit, so give yourself margin rather than loading to the number. A build with a large air cooler and a full-size graphics card can weigh more than the case's exterior dimensions suggest.

Capacity is only half of the problem. The bracket bolts to the underside of the desktop, and a desktop is designed to carry a load spread across its surface, not a point load hanging from four bolts. ANSI/BIFMA X5.5-2021, a desk and table test standard from the Business and Institutional Furniture Manufacturers Association (BIFMA), includes a concentrated load test on primary surfaces in Section 5.2. That is the test category that speaks to a loaded hanger bolted under a top. Most furniture sold outside the contract furniture market is not tested against that standard at an independent laboratory, and lighter home-office pieces may instead be assessed against the occasional-use standards, ANSI/BIFMA X6.4 and X6.5. An absent standard is not proof of weakness, but it is an absence. Certification to a named standard means passing every test in it; a product described only as 'BIFMA tested' has not made that claim.

One mounting guide states the point bluntly: many standard desks and brackets are not built for heavier towers, high-performance gaming PCs, or desktops with many added components. If your build is heavy, a holder that carries load to the floor is the safer answer than a hanger.

## What clearance does an under-desk PC mount need?

Mounting guidance recommends the side gap shown in the table. That gap has to be measured in the finished position, because desk frame rails, a drawer box, a wall, and the underside of the top all count against it.

The failure mode is specific rather than theoretical. If the bottom of the case sits directly against a surface, intake airflow is severely restricted and the system can overheat. A hanger holds the case off the desk, and that standoff (the gap between the case and whatever it hangs from) matters as much as the load rating. With a shelf-style holder, check the standoff the design leaves; the tower stands on the shelf rather than hanging from it.

Legroom is the second clearance problem. OSHA has no workstation-specific standard, so compliance rests on the general duty clause plus ergonomic guidance, and that guidance says desks should provide enough legroom and allow proper placement of computer components. A tower plus bracket reduces both. Sit with your feet flat and measure the width your knees actually need. Before drilling, test the case offset to one side of the knee well; moving it off centre can preserve legroom. BIFMA's X10.1-2024 ergonomics guideline is a non-mandatory sizing guide, not a certification, so treat it as context rather than a pass mark.

## Will your tower fit the mount's height settings?

The documented example uses fixed stops rather than continuous adjustment, so a tower that lands between them hangs at the next setting or not at all. The surplus becomes clearance you have to find elsewhere. Measure with feet and base rails included.

Placement then decides whether you can live with it. The same supplier notes that a tower set too high or too low makes USB ports, the power button, and internal parts awkward to reach. Before drilling, hold the case at the intended height and test three things: reach the power button, plug into a rear port, and lift the case out. If rear access is tight, front-facing ports are worth more than any other feature on the spec sheet.

## What changes when the mount goes on a standing desk?

A mount that works on a fixed desk can fail on a sit-stand frame for reasons unrelated to weight. Three things change.

First, the tower travels. The cable bundle flexes every time the desk rises, so leave slack loops and route power and display cables through a port rather than over the front edge. Our guide to [desk grommets and cable ports](/blog/desk-grommets-and-cable-ports-sizes-to-check-before-buying/) covers the hole sizes that will actually pass a plug. Then check the bottom of the travel: compare the desk's lowest height minus the mount's drop against whatever sits underneath, including your shins. Our walkthrough of [standing desk height range](/blog/standing-desk-height-range-measuring-for-sit-stand-use/) explains how to establish that lowest figure for your own body.

Second, the frame occupies space you measured as empty. Check for crossbars and motor housings under the desktop; they may occupy the space you measured as empty. Confirm the mount spans the rails rather than competing with them.

Third, the load is on a moving structure. Keep the tower over a leg or near the frame's centre rather than cantilevered at one end, and re-check the fasteners after the first week of raising and lowering.

## Which mount features earn their place?

A steel frame and a stated capacity are the requirements. Padding is worth having, and a snug grip matters more than anything else on this list if your case has a tempered glass side panel.

Separate adjustable width and length from adjustable height. Width and length let the bracket grip different case footprints. Height sets where the case hangs and, as above, can be a short list of fixed stops rather than a sliding range.

Among the extras, a swivel earns its keep when the tower sits behind a desk leg or you need rear ports regularly. Tool-free adjustment matters if you swap cases. Cable clips are a nicety; hook-and-loop tape can do the same job. On anything powered, check for a safety listing: where furniture carries electrical components, the BIFMA regime requires those components to be tested to Underwriters Laboratories (UL) standards, so a powered accessory with no named listing is a fair question to ask before buying.

## Buying checklist

- Weigh the tower as built and write the number down.
- Find the published capacity and the height settings; if they are fixed stops, confirm your case height lands on one.
- Check the free gap on all sides once the mount is in position against the clearance target in the table.
- Inspect the underside of the desktop at the bolt points for solid material, not just thickness.
- Sit at the desk and measure knee clearance before drilling.
- On a sit-stand desk, subtract the mount's drop from the lowest desk height and compare with floor clearance.
- Hold the case at the intended height and test the power button, a rear port, and removal.
- Re-tighten the bolts after a week of use.

## If the free fixes are not enough

When repositioning and re-measuring cannot solve it, the table below maps four specific situations to the kind of product that answers each.

| Situation | Better option |
| --- | --- |
| Tower height falls between fixed stops | Hanger with wider or continuous height range; floor holder on casters |
| Tower heavier than mount rating | Floor cart or load-to-floor shelf |
| Thin, hollow-core, or weak desktop | Frame-rail clamp or floor stand |
| Ports or power button unreachable | Swivel or pull-out mount; side stand at desk height |
