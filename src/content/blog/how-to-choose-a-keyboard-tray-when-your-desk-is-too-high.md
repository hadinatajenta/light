## Why a too-high desk is a measurement problem first

If your keyboard sits on a desk 75 cm off the floor and your seated elbow height is 68 cm, the keyboard is 7 cm above where it should be. OSHA's Computer Workstations eTool guidance recommends the keyboard sit at or slightly below elbow height with the shoulders relaxed. Seven centimetres is well past the point where a reminder to sit up straighter helps. Typing in that position pulls the wrist into extension and the shoulder into a shrug, and no wrist rest corrects a gap that size.

A keyboard tray closes that gap by moving the input surface below the desk. The useful question is narrower than "which tray is best": can a tray deliver the drop your body needs, and will it mount to the desk you actually own.

## Get three numbers before you shop

Buying a tray without these measurements is guesswork, and returns are annoying on hardware that has been drilled, clamped, or assembled.

**1. Floor to desk top**, measured at the front edge where you sit. Desk height often differs from the number in the listing once feet, casters, or a height-adjustable frame are involved.

**2. Floor to seated elbow height**, with the chair at a height where your feet are supported and your thighs are roughly parallel to the floor. Sit relaxed, arms hanging, then measure to the underside of the elbow. This is the target height for the keyboard surface.

**3. Front edge to the first under-desk obstruction.** Drawers, aprons, support beams, power strips, cable trays, and the crossbar of a height-adjustable frame all compete for the same space. Measure the depth from the front edge to whatever comes first.

The difference between desk height and seated elbow height is the total drop you need from the desk top. A tray does not start at the desk top, however; it starts at or just below the underside. So the travel a tray must provide is roughly:

required tray travel = (desk height − desk thickness) − elbow height

If desk height is 75 cm, elbow height is 68 cm, and the desktop is 2.5 cm thick, the underside is about 72.5 cm, and you need about 4.5 cm of downward travel. A tray with 10 cm of adjustment will cover that with room to spare; a 4 cm tray may be marginal or too short, depending on the desk and tray thickness. Check the published travel range and, if possible, the lowest height the keyboard surface reaches, not just the word "adjustable."

There is a second route to the same result: raising the chair reduces the drop, but it also lifts your feet off the floor unless you add a footrest, and it may push your chair arms into the tray. Compare the two costs honestly. A footrest is usually cheaper than a tray; a tray is usually the better fit if the desk is more than a few centimetres out and the chair is already at a comfortable height.

## What the spec sheet has to prove

Five specifications decide whether a tray is usable. If a listing omits any of them, treat the product as unverified rather than assuming it will be fine.

**Vertical travel.** The tray must lower the keyboard from the desk underside to your measured elbow height. Look for a published adjustment range in centimetres or inches. "Adjustable" on its own tells you nothing about how far down the surface goes.

**Tilt range including negative tilt.** A slight negative tilt, with the front edge higher than the back (the keyboard slopes down away from you), helps keep the wrist neutral. ANSI/HFES 100 covers ergonomic principles for keyboard support height, tilt, and adjustability. Check that the tilt range includes negative degrees and that the mechanism locks at the angle you choose, since a tray that drifts under typing tension will not hold position.

**Usable width and depth.** Measure your keyboard and add any wrist rest. If a 44 cm keyboard sits on a tray with a 50 cm wide surface, the margin is small, and a separate mouse platform becomes necessary rather than optional. Wrist rests add depth, so a 25 cm deep tray may not accept both the keyboard and the rest without overhang.

**Extension and swivel.** Track length determines how far the keyboard pulls out, and swivel lets you bring the mouse closer without moving the chair. "Full extension" frequently describes the travel of the track rather than the usable keyboard position, so ask how many lock positions exist and where the surface actually sits when extended.

**Load capacity and stability.** Ask for a load rating, ideally a dynamic one that reflects typing forces rather than a static resting weight. A tray that sags or wobbles will not stay at the height you set, and that is the whole point of the purchase.

The word "ergonomic" on its own is marketing. Compare the five specifications above against your own measurements instead.

## Mounting is where most trays fail

A tray that meets every ergonomic requirement is still useless if it will not attach.

For clamp-on models, the clamp jaw must match the desk thickness at the mounting point. Thin tops and very thick tops both cause problems: shims, a different mount, or reinforcement may be needed. The edge profile matters just as much. Beveled, bullnose, waterfall, and glass edges may prevent the clamp from seating squarely, so check whether the design requires a flat vertical face and how deep the jaw opening is. Manufacturer installation manuals are the reliable source here, not the marketing copy. Claims of "no drilling" or "easy install" assume a flat, accessible edge with clearance behind it, and some desks still end up needing drilling or a reinforcement plate.

Under-desk obstructions cut the available track length and knee clearance. Lowering the keyboard brings the tray closer to your thighs, and a mouse platform can collide with chair arms that previously passed underneath the desk. Sit at your normal working height and check clearance before you commit.

On a height-adjustable desk, work through the full travel path. The tray can strike the frame, a crossbar, or the legs as the desk moves, and cable management that was fine at sitting height may snag on the tray arm. If you are reusing an existing arm or track, verify screw-hole spacing, thread type, and plate dimensions, because mounting patterns vary between manufacturers and are often proprietary. "Universal fit" usually means a limited range plus adapters.

## Materials, wear parts, and what the warranty signals

Steel arms and tracks running on ball-bearing slides resist sag and wear better than all-plastic friction slides. Where a specification allows it, look for material gauge, finish, and roller type, since the slide is the part that determines whether the tray still sits level after a year of daily use.

A long warranty plus availability of replacement rollers, tracks, or knobs is a reasonable durability signal: the manufacturer expects the product to be serviceable rather than disposable. Check whether wear parts are user-replaceable. If a tray is claimed to meet ANSI/BIFMA X5.5, ask for the test report. That standard covers desk and table products and may not directly address keyboard trays or ergonomic fit, so the claim can be true and still tell you nothing about whether the tray suits your body.

## Who should buy a tray, and who should not

Buy one if you have a fixed-height desk that sits several centimetres above your seated elbow height, a flat front edge, and clear space under the desktop for a track. Buy one if you share a desk with someone of a different height and want the keyboard to move rather than the furniture.

Skip it, or solve the problem another way, if the desk is glass or has a curved or heavily profiled edge, if a drawer unit occupies the front span, if the top is too thick or too thin for available clamps, or if the drop you need is only a centimetre or two. In that last case, raising the chair with a footrest, or choosing a thinner keyboard, may be the cheaper fix. If you stand for most of the day, a tray set for seated work can end up in the way; verify clearance through the full standing range before buying.

## Buying checklist

- Measure floor-to-desk, floor-to-elbow, and front edge to first obstruction. Write the numbers down.
- Calculate the drop from the desk top: desk height minus elbow height. Subtract desk thickness (and tray thickness if known) to estimate the tray travel needed. Add a little margin.
- Confirm published vertical travel meets or exceeds that figure.
- Confirm tilt range includes negative degrees and locks.
- Compare tray surface width and depth against your keyboard plus wrist rest.
- Decide whether you need a mouse platform, and check which side it mounts on and whether it adjusts independently.
- Check extension length and swivel against your seated reach and chair arms.
- Ask for a dynamic load rating, not just a static one.
- Verify clamp opening against desk thickness, and jaw depth against your edge profile.
- Measure knee clearance at working height with the chair you own.
- On a sit-stand desk, test the full travel path and cable routing before mounting.
- If reusing an arm or track, confirm hole spacing, thread type, and plate dimensions.
- Check warranty length and whether rollers, tracks, and knobs are sold separately.
