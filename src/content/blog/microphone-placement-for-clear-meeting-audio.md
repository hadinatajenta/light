## Placement decides the audio before the microphone does

Most meeting audio complaints are placement complaints. A microphone sitting 60 cm from a talker in a room with hard walls is being asked to reproduce a large proportion of reflected sound, and gain or software cleanup cannot fully restore direct speech that was never captured cleanly. Before comparing models, settle three facts: how many people talk, where they sit relative to the capsule, and what surface the microphone can be mounted on. Specifications matter, but they matter second.

## Match the polar pattern to who is talking

Polar pattern is the first decision and the one marketing pages bury last. For a single talker at a desk, a cardioid or supercardioid reduces pickup of room noise and of everything behind the capsule. That rear rejection is why a directional desk microphone can sound cleaner than an omnidirectional model in the same untreated room, even when the omnidirectional has better measured self-noise.

The same property works against you with multiple talkers. Seat three people around a single cardioid and the person sitting behind the capsule is in the rejection zone. For a group around a table, an omnidirectional or boundary microphone, or a ceiling array that uses multiple elements for beamforming, usually produces more consistent levels across seats. Count talkers before you count features.

Polar pattern should be specified and measured according to IEC 60268-4. Ask for the polar plots, not the pattern name alone. A polar plot shows how response changes with angle, which is exactly what determines whether the person at the far end of the table sounds quieter than the person beside the microphone. Off-axis behaviour rarely appears in marketing copy because it is hard to make impressive in a sentence.

## Distance, proximity effect, and the tone you actually get

Directional microphones exhibit proximity effect: bass response rises as the capsule moves closer to the mouth. Used close, a cardioid can sound thick and muddy on speech. Used far, it sounds thin and picks up more room. The practical answer is a constant working distance. Manufacturer placement guides commonly suggest something in the 15 to 30 cm range for a directional desk microphone, and the exact figure matters less than holding it steady.

Run the geometry for your own room. If the recommended working distance is 20 cm and your gooseneck can only bring the capsule to within 45 cm of a seated talker's mouth, you are compensating with gain, and gain lifts the room along with the voice. That is arithmetic, not opinion.

Claims of "long pickup range" or "picks up from 20 feet" are meaningless without a stated signal-to-noise ratio and room condition, because direct sound falls with distance while the reverberant field does not. A quiet, treated room supports a longer working distance than a glass-walled one. If your room is hard-surfaced, spend the effort on getting the capsule closer rather than on a more sensitive microphone.

## Connectivity is where "plug and play" breaks down

Check the connector against the equipment you already own, because this is a common and expensive mistake. USB microphones that comply with USB Audio Class 1.0 are often driverless on many operating systems, but Class 2.0 devices may require drivers, and some models need a companion app for anything beyond basic operation. "Plug and play" is a compatibility claim, not a specification. Verify it against your operating system and your conferencing software before buying.

XLR microphones need an interface or mixer with phantom power. P48 phantom power is specified in IEC 61938 and is the common case, but some microphones require 12 to 24 V, and plug-in power lavaliers typically expect 3 to 5 V from a transmitter or recorder. Feeding the wrong voltage can cause poor performance and, in some designs, damage. Confirm the requirement in the manufacturer documentation and confirm your interface meets it.

Dante requires a Dante network and may require licensing, so it is a room infrastructure decision rather than a desk accessory. Bluetooth avoids cables but introduces latency and codec variability that can affect lip sync. For a recurring meeting setup, wired USB or XLR into an interface remains the least fragile path.

## The specifications worth reading

For speech, a frequency response covering at least 100 Hz to 10 kHz is common, but the range alone says little. Check the tolerance. A plus or minus 3 dB figure over that band describes a far more neutral microphone than a curve that wanders by 10 dB. IEC 60268-4 defines the measurement conditions, and a manufacturer publishing the actual curve is showing more than one publishing a single number.

Self-noise, quoted as an equivalent noise level in dB SPL A-weighted, tells you whether the microphone will be audible in a quiet room. Compare it against your room's noise floor rather than against a competitor's marketing. Sensitivity (dBV/Pa or mV/Pa) sets output level for a given sound pressure. Higher sensitivity helps distant placement but also raises the room, which is why a high number is not automatically better.

Maximum SPL and dynamic range matter if the microphone sits close to a loud talker or in a noisy space. Dynamic range is the gap between maximum SPL and self-noise, and both figures should come from the manufacturer's documentation rather than a retailer listing.

## What the labels do not tell you

"Noise-cancelling" on a microphone usually means passive rejection of off-axis sound through the polar pattern, not active cancellation as in headphones. Check the pattern and the off-axis figures instead of the term. "Studio-quality" and "broadcast-quality" have no standard definition and no measurable threshold, so they carry no information you can act on.

"360-degree pickup" deserves the same scepticism. On an array microphone it may imply omnidirectional behaviour, but sensitivity can vary with angle, so a talker at one side of the table may be quieter than one directly in front. Look for beamforming or polar documentation.

Finally, echo cancellation, noise reduction, and automatic gain control may run on the device or inside the conferencing application. Ask which, and whether the on-device processing can be bypassed. Doubling up processing in both places often sounds worse than running it once, in whichever place has the better algorithm.

## Mounting, materials, and shared-room durability

A microphone that drifts out of position stops being a good microphone. For a table setup, a gooseneck or stand with enough length and a shock-isolated mount prevents thumps travelling up the desk. Boundary microphones rely on the mounting surface, and ceiling arrays need hardware that keeps the array aimed at the seating area.

For shared rooms, look for metal housings in zinc alloy or aluminium rather than plastic, a steel mesh grille over the capsule, locking XLR or threaded connectors, and cable strain relief with a replaceable cable. Warranty length is a rough signal of how confident the manufacturer is in the build, though it is no substitute for inspecting the connector and grille yourself.

## Buying checklist

- Count the talkers first. One talker at a desk points to cardioid or supercardioid; a group points to omnidirectional, boundary, or a beamforming array.
- Ask for polar plots and read the off-axis behaviour. A pattern name will not tell you how the far end of the table sounds.
- Measure your mounting geometry before buying. Can the capsule sit at the recommended working distance from the talker's mouth without anyone leaning in?
- Check that the polar pattern is specified and measured to IEC 60268-4 and that the frequency response comes with a tolerance figure and a measured curve.
- Compare self-noise against your room's actual noise floor, not against another product's spec sheet.
- Confirm the connection type and power requirement: USB Audio Class version, phantom power voltage per IEC 61938, plug-in power, Dante, or Bluetooth.
- Verify operating system, driver, and conferencing software compatibility in writing rather than trusting a badge.
- Decide where echo cancellation and noise reduction actually run, and whether you can bypass them.
- Inspect the build: housing material, grille, connector locking, strain relief, replaceable cable, shock isolation.
- Treat a missing specification as absent rather than assuming a good value.

## Sources and further reading

IEC 60268-4 for microphone measurement conditions, including polar pattern and frequency response. IEC 61938 for phantom power, including P48. The USB Audio Class specification from USB-IF for driverless operation claims. AES67 for audio-over-IP interoperability. Manufacturer application notes and specification sheets from Shure, Audio-Technica, Sennheiser, and Beyerdynamic, which often publish frequency response curves, polar plots, and placement guidance in addition to marketing summaries.
