## Quick answer

- Start 3 to 4 inches (7.5 to 10 cm) above the seat surface, then move the pad to match your own spine.
- The target is the inward curve above your pelvis, not a fixed number. Find it with your thumbs.
- A well-set pad keeps your lower back touching the backrest. If it pushes your ribs forward or lifts your pelvis off the seat, it is too deep.

Your chair probably has an adjustable lumbar pad, and it probably came set for someone else. Adjustable lumbar support is one of the few chair features where the factory setting is rarely right, because the correct position depends on the length of your torso, the height of your pelvis and where you actually sit on the seat. This guide covers how to find the number for your body, how to check that it is right, and what to do if your chair cannot get there.

Set seat height first. Every lumbar measurement is taken from the seat surface, so if your seat height is wrong, your lumbar position is wrong too. Seat depth matters for the same reason: if the pad sits right but you are perched forward, your back is no longer against it.

## Where should lumbar support sit on your back?

The lumbar support belongs against the inward curve of your lower spine, above the pelvis. Published retailer guidance puts it at the small of the back, typically 3 to 4 inches (7.5 to 10 cm) above the seat surface. That is the number to start with, not to trust.

A second manufacturer guide places the lumbar zone higher, at roughly the natural waist, about 6 to 8 inches (15 to 20 cm) above the seat. The two figures disagree by a wide margin. That gap is not a reason to pick a side; it is a reason to stop treating any single number as correct for you. The distance from seat surface to lumbar curve varies with torso length, and two people of the same height can differ by several inches.

The fit check is the one to judge by: well-positioned support fills the natural curve above the pelvis and keeps the lower back in contact with the backrest without pushing the back forward. Contact, not pressure.

## How to find your own lumbar curve by hand

You do not need a tape measure for this part. Sit with your back against the backrest, place your hands on your hips with your thumbs pointing toward your back, then slide your thumbs upward until you feel where your spine curves inward. That hollow is the target zone.

A few things to check while your thumbs are there:

- Is the pad centred on the hollow, or is it sitting above it near the ribs?
- With your back relaxed, does your lower back stay in contact with the backrest, or is there a gap you have to hold yourself to close?
- If you slide the pad up an inch, does the contact improve or worsen?

Try one adjustment at a time and sit with it for a few minutes. Lumbar changes are easy to over-correct, because the wrong setting often feels fine for the first thirty seconds.

## Why lumbar depth matters more than height

Height gets the attention, but depth causes more of the discomfort. Too much protrusion forces the lower back into an excessive arch, which creates pressure rather than support, and sitters respond by sliding forward off the pad. That loses the support entirely.

If your chair has a depth adjustment, use it after you set height. The correct depth is the shallowest setting that still keeps your lower back in contact with the backrest. If the pad is fixed and feels too aggressive, you may be able to move or remove it. That is a legitimate fix rather than a defeat.

Cushion and backrest construction change how much depth you need. A [mesh office chair seat and back](/blog/mesh-vs-foam-office-chair-seats-what-to-check-for-long-sitting/) flexes under load, so the effective pad position can shift slightly once you sit back. Set the pad while you are seated and settled, not while you are standing beside the chair.

Where you sit front-to-back changes everything. A seat that is too long for your thighs pushes you forward, and a lumbar pad that was correctly placed when you sat upright ends up behind your shoulder blades. If you keep adjusting the pad and it never feels right, the real problem may be seat depth. Our guide to [sizing an office chair by depth and height](/blog/chair-seat-depth-and-height-office-chair-sizing/) covers the 2 to 3 inches (5 to 7.5 cm) of clearance you want between the seat edge and the back of your knees.

## Starting heights by body height, and why they are only a start

This table shows commonly published starting bands for lumbar pad height above the seat surface. They are a first adjustment, not a fit standard.

| Body height | Starting lumbar height above seat |
| --- | --- |
| Under 5'4" | 2 to 3 in (5 to 7.5 cm) |
| 5'4" to 5'10" | 3 to 4 in (7.5 to 10 cm) |
| Over 5'10" | 4 to 5 in (10 to 12.5 cm) |

Two caveats matter more than the numbers. Height is a rough proxy for torso length, and plenty of people are the exception. The bands also assume you are sitting fully back against the backrest; sit forward and the pad is behind you rather than against you, and no band will work.

Taller readers have a second constraint: many chairs run out of lumbar travel before the pad reaches the right spot, so check the adjustment range in the spec sheet against your measured seat-to-lumbar distance before buying. Our guide to [choosing an office chair for a tall user](/blog/how-to-choose-an-office-chair-for-a-tall-user-measure-first/) walks through the measurements in order.

## Is an adjustable lumbar pad worth paying for?

Yes, but not for the reason the marketing implies. Manufacturer guidance is blunt about this: many ergonomic chairs include adjustable lumbar support, but having the feature does not guarantee it is positioned correctly, and support set too high or too low can feel uncomfortable instead of supportive.

What you are buying with an adjustment mechanism is the ability to correct a fit that is wrong by a couple of inches. If your back lands in the middle of the published bands and the chair is otherwise the right size, a fixed pad in the right place can be perfectly comfortable. If you are at either end of the height range, or your torso is unusually long or short, adjustment is closer to essential.

Under EN 1335, the European standard covering office chair dimensions and test methods, better Type A chairs add 3D lumbar adjustment across height, depth and pressure. A listing that mentions the norm without the paperwork is a marketing word, not a verification: the manufacturer should be able to supply a declaration of conformity referencing the relevant part of the norm plus a lab test report.

The classes map loosely to sitting hours, which is a practical filter. Type A has full adjustment ranges for 8 or more hours of daily use, Type B has reduced adjustment for 4 to 8 hours, and Type C has basic adjustment for up to 4 hours or meeting rooms. If you sit all day, Type A is the band to look in.

## If your chair is too small, too tall, or too firm in the pad

A correct lumbar setting only holds if the rest of the fit is right. This table gives target ranges for the specifications that decide it.

| Fit point | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Seat depth clearance behind the knee | 2 to 3 in (5 to 7.5 cm) |
| Hip clearance each side of the seat | 1 to 2 in (2.5 to 5 cm) |
| Seat width range | 17.5 to 20 in (44 to 51 cm) |
| Seat height range, task chairs (BIFMA X5.1) | 16.5 to 21 in (42 to 53 cm) |

Seat depth is the figure that most often sabotages a good lumbar setting. Published fit guidance calls for 2 to 3 inches of space between the front edge of the seat and the back of your knees when your back is against the backrest, to avoid pressure behind the knee. If the seat is longer than that, you will slide forward to relieve the pressure and lose lumbar contact.

Seat width tolerance is asymmetric: a seat may be somewhat narrower than your seated hip width, but should not be much wider, because an over-wide seat puts the armrests too far out to be useful. Standard seat widths run 17.5 to 20 inches (44 to 51 cm), with at least 1 to 2 inches of space on each side of your hips.

If you are replacing a chair, work in this order: measure the depth from the back of your buttocks to the crease behind the knee, then subtract a typical 50 mm seat slide range to get the minimum acceptable depth. A seat that is slightly too short is far more workable than one that is too long, because you can add a cushion or sit forward without losing support.

## How long should it take to get right?

Give it a week, not an afternoon. Lumbar position changes how you sit, and a setting that is genuinely better can feel worse for the first few days because you are using muscles that have been idle. Adjust one variable per day: height first, then depth, then seat height if the pad still does not land.

If it still does not feel right after a week of deliberate adjustment, the chair may not fit, and no amount of pad tuning will fix a seat depth that is 3 inches too long.

## Buying checklist

- Measure seat-to-lumbar on your own body with your thumbs before you buy anything. Write the number down.
- Confirm the lumbar pad's adjustment range in the spec sheet, not in the marketing copy, and check that it covers your number.
- Check seat depth against your measured thigh length, and treat a seat slide of about 50 mm as the adjustment that buys you tolerance.
- Check seat width against your seated hip width: a narrower seat is workable, an over-wide one is not.
- Verify weight capacity against your body weight with margin. Mechanisms and gas struts are rated by weight for safety and durability, not just comfort.
- For all-day use, look for EN 1335 Type A, or a declaration of conformity and a lab test report, rather than the word "ergonomic".
- Read the return policy. Lumbar fit is one of the few things you genuinely cannot assess in a showroom in ten minutes.

## If the free fixes are not enough

Sometimes the measurement is fine and the chair still cannot get there. These are the situations where a purchase makes sense.

| Your situation | What answers it |
| --- | --- |
| Fixed pad that sits above your curve, no depth adjustment | BestOffice ergonomic mesh office chair |
| Feet left off the floor at the seat height your lumbar position needs | Mind Reader adjustable footrest or adjustable under-desk footrest |
| Lower back fatigue during standing hours | Ergodriven Not Flat anti-fatigue mat |
| Leaning in toward a dim screen and losing back contact | Dimmable clamp desk lamp |

Two of these are replacements rather than fixes. The chair only makes sense if the rest of the chair already fits you, and a lamp does not change your lumbar fit; it removes the reason you lean in and lose back contact in the first place. Footrests matter more than people expect here: if your seat height has to rise to bring the lumbar pad into range, your feet may leave the floor, and a footrest is cheaper than a different chair. An anti-fatigue mat addresses the fatigue that builds in the lower back during standing hours on a hard floor, which is a different problem from seated lumbar support and should not be confused with it.
