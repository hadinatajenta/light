## Quick answer

- 45 cN (centinewtons, often written as '45 g') is CHERRY's lightest standard MX figure; the MX Red actuates after 2 mm of travel.
- 60 cN is the MX Black, which also actuates at 2 mm, so the heavier spring buys force rather than a shorter stroke.
- Choose 45 cN if your fingers ache at the end of a long day. Choose 60 cN if your hands rest on the keys and produce stray letters.
- A 55 cN tactile switch, such as the MX Brown, sits between them if you want a tactile bump at a mid-weight.

Switch weight is the easiest keyboard specification to compare and one of the hardest to interpret. Actuation force is not a performance figure like a refresh rate. It is the push required to register a keystroke, which makes it a comfort variable with a side order of typing style. Buyers who stop asking which weight is better and start asking which weight suits their hands tend to get it right the first time.

## What do 45 g and 60 g actually measure?

Actuation force is measured in centinewtons (cN). CHERRY identifies the coil spring as the part that sets it: the figure varies with stem colour and, according to CHERRY, stays the same for the whole lifespan of the switch. That matters when you compare a well-used board with a new one: the rated spring force is not expected to drift over the switch's life.

The table sets out the reference points the manufacturers publish.

| Switch | Actuation force (cN) | Pre-travel (mm) | Total travel (mm) | Character |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Red | 45 | 2.0 | | Linear |
| Brown | 55 | 2.0 | 4.0 | Tactile |
| Black | 60 | 2.0 | | Linear |
| Silver Speed | 45 | 1.2 | | Linear |

Two things follow. First, 60 cN is not heavy in absolute terms: Das Keyboard puts the typical Cherry MX spread at 45 to 80 cN, so both candidates sit in the lower half of the range, and the 15 cN step between them is a third more force on paper. Second, the unit label on product listings is loose. Das Keyboard gives the conversion of one gram-force to 0.98 cN, so a switch listed at 45 g and one listed at 45 cN are close but not identical quantities.

Ratings also carry tolerance. DigiKey's engineering forum, explaining how operating force is measured, notes that a datasheet figure is nominal with a stated spread, citing a 350 gf switch that may sit anywhere between 250 and 450 gf. That is a different switch family, but the principle travels: two weights quoted 15 cN apart can overlap at the edges of their tolerance bands.

The rated number is not the force that ends the stroke, either. Mark-10's force-versus-distance test on a linear keyswitch recorded 0.305 N at the start of travel and 0.507 N at maximum compression. If you bottom out, your fingertips meet the second number, not the rated actuation force.

## Does a heavier switch make you slower or more accurate?

The clearest description of the trade-off comes from a University of Virginia Scholars' Lab guide to switch basics: light springs are more responsive and allow faster keystrokes but provide less feedback when pressed, while heavy springs give more feedback and more deliberate typing at the cost of faster fatigue and reduced typing speed.

Read that as two different failure modes. On a light switch, the failure is a stray letter from a finger resting too heavily on the home row, and the remedy is technique. On a heavy switch, the failure is hand fatigue that accumulates across a working day and does not clear over a coffee break.

For eight hours of writing I would take the lighter spring, accepting the risk of stray letters over the risk of cumulative fatigue. That is a preference, not a rule. If you routinely rest your palms on the board and find yourself deleting doubled characters, the extra force of a 60 cN switch is doing real work and the trade is worth accepting.

## Why does 45 versus 60 cN change feel more than speed?

Both reference switches actuate at 2 mm of pre-travel, the distance the stem moves before the contact closes. Moving from 45 cN to 60 cN leaves that distance untouched, so the keystroke registers at the same point in the stroke and the difference is confined to how much effort it takes to get there.

That is why weight is the wrong dial if speed is your goal. CHERRY's claim that the MX Silver Speed is up to 40 percent faster than other MX switches rests on two changes together: the 45 cN spring and a shortened 1.2 mm pre-travel. The travel reduction does most of the work, because it moves the actuation point nearer the top of the stroke. A 60 cN switch at 2 mm buys none of that.

Choose weight for how your hands feel at hour six, and read pre-travel figures separately if you genuinely want a shorter path to actuation.

## Is a mid-weight tactile switch the better compromise?

The MX Brown is the mid-weight tactile option in the table. The reason to consider it is the bump, not the number: a tactile switch adds feedback that a linear switch does not. Whether that feedback helps your hands over a long day is personal.

Who should avoid it: anyone who wants an uninterrupted stroke for very fast repeated presses, and anyone who has tried a tactile switch and found the bump distracting under the pinky. Tactile feedback is a preference, not an upgrade, and it does not sit neatly between the two linear weights in feel even though it sits between them in force.

## What else has to match before weight matters?

A switch that does not fit the board you own is an expensive mistake. Das Keyboard documents the pin difference: a PCB-mounted housing base (printed circuit board, the board the switches solder or slot into) has one extra pin, three in total, while a plate-mounted base has three extra pins, five in total. Count before you order loose switches.

Backlighting needs a second check, because physical fit does not guarantee light. The LED cutout has to line up with the board's LEDs, and CHERRY notes that the RGB version of a switch uses a translucent lower housing with a recess for the surface-mounted LED on the circuit board. RGB and non-RGB housings are different parts, not cosmetic variants of the same one.

Durability is rated per model rather than per weight class. In Das Keyboard's table the Red, Speed Silver, Black and Brown are rated above 100 million keystrokes, while the clicky Blue and Green appear at over 50 million. CHERRY separately states that every MX switch is guaranteed for more than 50 million strokes without loss of input quality and that it is the only manufacturer guaranteeing this. Treat the exclusivity as a vendor claim and the per-model ratings as the figures you can actually compare.

## What should you check before you order?

- Decide from your worst typing day, not your best. A weight that only works when you are fresh is the wrong weight.
- Confirm the pin count of your board: three pins for PCB-mount, five for plate-mount.
- Check the LED cutout position, and whether your board's surface-mounted LEDs need the RGB housing.
- Read the lifespan rating for the specific model, since it varies by switch rather than by weight.
- Price the whole change, not just the switches. If the board does not accept loose switches, a different weight means a different keyboard.
- If you cannot decide between 45 and 60, handle both before committing. A tester with one switch of each weight and type answers in a minute what a spec sheet cannot.

## When changing the switch is the answer

The table maps four common situations to the kind of fix that addresses them.

| Situation | What helps |
| --- | --- |
| Fatigue on a 60 cN board | 45 cN linear board or switches |
| Stray letters from resting hands | 60 cN board or 55 cN tactile |
| Board cannot take loose switches | Replacement keyboard at desired weight |
| Patchy backlight after a swap | Correct LED cutout or RGB housing |
