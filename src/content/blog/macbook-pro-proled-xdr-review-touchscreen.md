Apple's first touchscreen Mac went on sale November 6, and the reviews landed a day earlier. This MacBook Pro PROLED XDR review collects the lab numbers and opinions from more than a dozen outlets, so you can decide whether the new OLED MacBook Pro deserves $2,899 of your money. The short version: almost every reviewer loves the screen, and almost none of them think touch is the reason to buy it.

## Quick answer

- The 14-inch starts at $2,899 and the 16-inch at $3,399, both with M5 Pro. That is $400 more than the mini-LED MacBook Pro with the same chip, which Apple still sells.
- The display is the star. Notebookcheck measured about 2,084 nits of peak HDR brightness, and Tom's Guide calls it the brightest laptop screen it has tested.
- Touch is a bonus. There is no Apple Pencil support and no tablet mode, and some reviewers forgot it was there.
- Speed is unchanged: these are the same M5 Pro and M5 Max chips Apple shipped in March.
- It is lighter: 3 pounds for the 14-inch and 4 pounds for the 16-inch, down from 3.5 and 4.7.
- Buy it for photo and video work or daily travel. Skip it if your laptop lives closed on a desk next to a monitor.

## What $2,899 buys, and what the $400 pays for

Apple announced the MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display on October 28 and shipped it November 6, 2026. [Apple's announcement](https://apple.com/newsroom/2026/10/apple-unveils-groundbreaking-macbook-pro-with-proled-xdr-display) lists U.S. prices from $2,899 for the 14-inch M5 Pro and $3,399 for the 16-inch M5 Pro, with M5 Max versions from $4,499 and $4,799. Education pricing drops the M5 Pro models to $2,749 and $3,199.

[MacRumors broke down the base configurations](https://macrumors.com/2026/10/28/new-macbook-pro-maxed-out-cost/). The $2,899 model gets a 15-core CPU, 16-core GPU, 24GB of memory, and a 1TB SSD, while the 16-inch M5 Pro steps up to an 18-core CPU and 20-core GPU. Nano-texture glass adds $150.

The mini-LED MacBook Pro stays on sale at $2,499 for the 14-inch M5 Pro and $2,999 for the 16-inch, so every PROLED configuration costs exactly $400 more than its twin. Engadget frames the 14-inch differently: against the cheapest 14-inch MacBook Pro, the $1,999 M6 model, the PROLED version costs $900 more. Which framing applies depends on whether you need M5 Pro power at all.

Apple's own case, as [9to5Mac reported](https://9to5mac.com/2026/11/03/apple-explains-the-400-premium-for-the-macbook-pro-with-proled-xdr-display/), is that the $400 pays for the display, with touch as a secondary extra. Apple's white paper describes the panel as a smaller version of its Studio Display XDR reference monitor.

## PROLED vs mini-LED: what the new screen changes

The old MacBook Pro screen is an LCD lit from behind by thousands of tiny LEDs grouped into dimming zones. That is mini-LED. Put a bright object on a black background and light leaks around it, a glow called blooming. PROLED is Apple's name for a tandem OLED panel: two stacked layers of pixels that make their own light and switch fully off for black. No backlight means no halos.

On paper the panel peaks at 2,000 nits for HDR and SDR, up from 1,600 nits HDR on mini-LED, with a 10,000,000:1 contrast ratio and ProMotion refresh from 10Hz to 120Hz. The screens grow slightly to 14.2 and 16.3 inches, and an iPhone-style Dynamic Island replaces the notch.

Lab tests fill in what the spec sheet skips. [Notebookcheck measured](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Amazing-OLED-panel-Apple-MacBook-Pro-14-PROLED-XDR-Review.1438048.0.html) about 2,084 nits peak HDR on the 14-inch, 98 percent P3 and 97.8 percent Adobe RGB coverage, and a ColorChecker Delta-E of 1.7 (a color error score where lower is better). Everyday SDR brightness was far lower: about 606 nits with the ambient light sensor off and up to 1,025 nits with it on. Apple's 2,000-nit SDR figure is an outdoor peak, not what you get at a desk. Tom's Hardware measured 542.6 nits SDR and 1,964 nits HDR, per [iThinkDiff's review roundup](https://www.ithinkdiff.com/macbook-pro-proled-xdr-display-review-roundup/), but reported only 83.6 percent DCI-P3, well below Notebookcheck's figure.

Photographers were the most excited. [Fstoppers' Alex Cooke](https://fstoppers.com/gear/review-macbook-pro-proled-xdr-display-and-m5-max-screen-changes-everything-905426) could see detail in the bottom two stops of shadow, praised the new Adobe RGB photography reference mode, and wrote that "the display is the best I have used, on any device." [Engadget's Devindra Hardawar](https://www.engadget.com/2307215/apple-macbook-pro-with-proled-xdr-display-review/) was cooler: in normal use he found the gap to mini-LED hard to spot, though a dark fireworks scene in F1: The Movie looked "revelatory."

If your eyes are sensitive to flicker, pay attention here. Notebookcheck measured PWM dimming at 240Hz at up to 99 percent brightness, against 492.6Hz on the 2024 mini-LED model it compared. Low-frequency flicker gives some people headaches, so try one in a store first and see [our guide to reducing eye strain at a desk](/blog/reducing-eye-strain-at-a-desk-distance-lighting-breaks/).

## How the MacBook Pro touch screen works in macOS

Touch on this touchscreen MacBook is deliberately low-key. The sensor sits inside the panel, and macOS 27 treats your finger mostly like a pointer with extra gestures. [9to5Mac's review](https://9to5mac.com/2026/11/05/macbook-pro-with-proled-xdr-display-touch-review/) lists pinch to zoom, a four-finger swipe for Spaces, swipe up for the Dock, pull to refresh in Safari, double-tap a window to fill the screen, and flick a window sideways to tile it.

macOS also enlarges small targets. Menu items spread apart when you tap them, and window buttons grow on the first tap and fire on the second. Long-pressing a Finder file lifts it for dragging, which [Six Colors' Jason Snell](https://sixcolors.com/post/2026/11/macbook-pro-with-proled-review-you-could-even-say-it-glows/) called "a little awkward." You can switch touch off in System Settings under Accessibility.

What's missing matters more. The Verge's David Pierce found no on-screen keyboard, no copy-and-paste pop-up, and no Apple Pencil support, per [MacRumors' review roundup](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/11/05/macbook-pro-proled-xdr-reviews/), and Engadget confirmed there is no tablet mode. For drawing or couch reading, an iPad with a keyboard case is still the better tool. Where touch did help: zooming during photo edits, showing someone a screen, swiping away notifications, and iPhone Mirroring.

The hinge split reviewers. Apple redesigned it for touch, and MobileSyrup's Dean Daley saw no wobble. TechRadar, Tom's Hardware, Stuff, and YouTuber Tim Pan all saw the screen bounce when tapped, per iThinkDiff.

One desk tip: if your laptop sits high on a stand, reaching up to tap gets tiring fast. [Our laptop stand guide](/blog/how-to-choose-a-laptop-stand-for-daily-use/) covers heights, and a lower angle makes sense if you plan to touch this one.

## Benchmarks: same M5 chips, same speed

There is no speed bump in the MacBook Pro 2026 redesign. Chance Miller at 9to5Mac found results essentially the same as the March M5 Pro and M5 Max models.

The numbers agree. Engadget's 14-inch M5 Pro scored 4,217 in Geekbench 6 single-core. Notebookcheck's 14-inch M5 Pro with an 18-core CPU scored 4,300 single-core and 29,359 multi-core in Geekbench 6.7, and Tom's Guide's 14-inch posted 4,323 and 29,219, per iThinkDiff. Fstoppers' 16-inch M5 Max scored 9,543 in Cinebench 2026 multi-core and 740 single-core, 9.5 percent ahead of the M4 Max, and held about 98 percent of peak speed under sustained load.

Heat and noise produced the loudest disagreement. 9to5Mac never heard the fans on its 16-inch M5 Pro. Notebookcheck measured 44.6 dB(A) under load on the 14-inch and listed louder fans and higher temperatures as cons. MobileSyrup's M5 Max got too warm for a lap after about 10 minutes of the game Control. Tim Pan measured the new M5 Max peaking at 118°F against 133.5°F for the previous M5 Max. If you plan long renders on an M5 Max, the 16-inch's bigger chassis is the safer pick.

## Battery life: a wide spread of results

Apple's ratings match the mini-LED models: 22 hours of video and 14 hours of web for the 14-inch M5 Pro, and 24 and 17 hours for the 16-inch M5 Pro.

Real tests vary with the workload. Notebookcheck's 14-inch M5 Pro ran 16 hours 37 minutes of Wi-Fi browsing at 150 nits, against 15.2 hours for the mini-LED 14-inch M5 Max. At full brightness the gap widened to 9.6 hours against 6, which Notebookcheck credits to the more efficient OLED panel (the M5 Max chip in the comparison model accounts for part of it). Tom's Guide got 21 hours 24 minutes from the 16-inch M5 Max on web browsing, while Tom's Hardware got 15 hours 57 minutes from the same model. TechRadar's looped 1080p video ran 24 hours 21 minutes.

Harder work cuts that down. Engadget got about 12.5 hours on the 14-inch through a mixed day of tabs, Slack, image editing, and video calls. Gizmodo reported about 8 hours of writing and streaming. Even that low figure covers a standard workday.

Notebookcheck also found the 14-inch caps its power draw at 96W, so a 140W brick won't charge it any faster.

## Lighter body, same ports

The weight cut is the upgrade reviewers felt first. The 14-inch drops from 3.5 to 3 pounds (3.1 with M5 Max) and from 0.61 to 0.59 inches thick. The 16-inch drops from 4.7 to 4 pounds (4.1 with M5 Max), per 9to5Mac and Fstoppers. Miller said the lighter 16-inch "feels less like a tank."

Ports match the mini-LED model: three Thunderbolt 5, HDMI, an SDXC slot, MagSafe 3, and a headphone jack, with up to three external displays on M5 Pro and four on M5 Max. For a one-cable desk, [our Thunderbolt 5 dock explainer](/blog/thunderbolt-5-docks-explained-bandwidth-boost-dual-4k-pcie-storage/) covers what that bandwidth gets you. Touch ID stays, and Tom's Hardware and Stuff both faulted the missing Face ID.

Six speakers promise 50 percent deeper bass. Engadget heard no distortion at high volume, while Bloomberg's Chris Welch found music and podcasts boomy, per MacRumors. The glowing Apple logo is back: Six Colors says you can turn it off, but MobileSyrup couldn't fully disable it without dimming the screen.

## Where reviewers disagree

- **Touch.** [Tom's Guide's Tony Polanco](https://www.tomsguide.com/computing/macbooks/macbook-pro-with-proled-xdr-display-review) called it the most responsive laptop touchscreen he has used. MobileSyrup's Daley wrote, "I'm not sure if the MacBook PROLED XDR needed a touchscreen."
- **How visible the screen upgrade is.** Fstoppers and Macworld rave. Engadget says the difference is hard to see in daily use.
- **Value.** Six Colors says the $400 is worth it if you want the display and lighter body. Engadget calls the 16-inch the better deal, since the 14-inch costs $900 more than the M6. Stuff points most buyers to the standard MacBook Pro.

## Who should buy it, and who should skip

Buy it if you grade photos or HDR video. The Adobe RGB reference mode and near-perfect blacks are real working tools, which is why Fstoppers tells M4 Max owners to upgrade for the display, not the chip. It also makes sense if you carry your laptop daily, since half a pound off the 14-inch adds up.

Skip it if your MacBook Pro lives closed next to an external screen. You would pay $400 for a panel you rarely look at, and that money buys a lot of monitor; [our home-office monitor guide](/blog/how-to-choose-a-monitor-for-a-home-office/) shows where to spend it. Skip it too if you own a recent MacBook Pro. Engadget sees no need to upgrade, and Six Colors relays Bloomberg's Mark Gurman reporting that Apple skipped M6 Pro and M6 Max, with M7 chips expected next year. Treat that as a rumor, but it means the chip inside will age faster than the chassis.

If you don't need M5 Pro power, the $1,999 M6 MacBook Pro or a far cheaper machine from [our MacBook Neo review](/blog/macbook-neo-review-home-office/) covers everyday work for much less.

## MacBook Pro PROLED XDR review: the verdict

This is the best laptop screen Apple has built, in a body noticeably lighter than the mini-LED model beside it. Notebookcheck's lab numbers back up the hype on HDR brightness and color. The touchscreen works but changes little, and without Pencil support it can't replace an iPad. The chips are eight months old, and battery and heat results depend heavily on who ran the test. Pay the $400 if the screen is part of your job or the lighter weight is part of your commute. Otherwise the mini-LED MacBook Pro at $2,499, or the M6 at $1,999, does the same work for less.
