## Quick answer

- Assume no factory warranty unless the seller is an authorized dealer.
- Find the manufacturing label under the seat and photograph it before you pay.
- Ask which lift cylinder type the chair uses. Top-activated and cable-actuated are not interchangeable.
- Treat "BIFMA certified" as a claim to verify, not a fact.

A used office chair is one of the few large purchases where the same object can be a bargain or a trap, and the difference is usually paperwork rather than appearance. A cosmetically perfect chair may contain a non-original gas lift, which on its own is enough to end any remaining factory coverage. A scuffed chair with a legible label and a documented history may be the better buy. This guide covers the checks that separate those two cases, and the point at which a used chair stops being worth the risk.

## Who is selling you the chair, and does the warranty follow it?

Herman Miller states that, with limited exceptions, its products carry a 12-year warranty covering parts and labor. The same page states that products bought from a non-authorized reseller are not covered, because the company cannot guarantee the condition or authenticity of the components those resellers used. That single sentence decides most used-chair questions: if the seller is not an authorized dealer, price the chair as though it has no coverage at all, because in most cases it does not.

This is not a reason to walk away. It is a reason to know what you are buying. Used and rebuilt merchandise is an area where the FTC has already established enforcement precedent: the agency has determined that certain practices in the sale of used and/or rebuilt merchandise are unfair or deceptive and violate the FTC Act. That is a reason to ask in writing how old the chair is, whether any parts were replaced, and with what. A message thread is enough.

One more limit worth naming: do not accept a remaining warranty term the seller quotes but cannot document with a purchase date.

## Where is the manufacturing label, and what has to be on it?

Under the seat. Herman Miller's guidance puts the manufacturing label on the underside of a chair's seat, so a listing photo of the top of the chair tells you nothing. For warranty repairs, the manufacturer requires the product number, the FO number (a factory order number printed on that label) and the manufacture date, along with a description and photograph of the fault.

Before paying, get a clear photograph of that label and check that the model it names matches what is advertised. If the label is missing or unreadable, you lose the ability to prove what you own and, in most cases, the service path that depends on it. Once you own the chair, keep that photo somewhere you can find it.

## Which parts can you still buy, and which are dealer-only?

Herman Miller sells only a limited set of spares directly, naming chair casters, Aeron arm pads and Aeron back support options, and directs buyers to an authorized dealer or retailer for everything else. If a used listing shows a cracked frame or torn mesh, that is a dealer conversation, not a parts order. Our guide to [what to check on an office chair gas lift and base](/blog/office-chair-gas-lift-and-base-what-to-check-before-buying/) covers the mechanical inspection in more detail.

Replacement gas lifts are also sold aftermarket, and those listings need the most careful reading. One third-party listing states plainly that its cylinder is designed and manufactured by the seller itself, is not an OEM part (OEM meaning original equipment manufacturer, the company that built the chair), and uses the brand and model names only to identify which chairs it fits. It carries a 2-year warranty against failure, compared with the 12-year parts-and-labor term the manufacturer states for its own products, and is rated up to 450 lb with a five-inch travel range. That 450 lb figure describes the cylinder as a component. It says nothing about what the assembled chair is rated for.

Activation type is the compatibility trap. This table shows which lift type fits which chairs, based on a replacement parts listing.

| Chair | Lift cylinder type |
| --- | --- |
| Aeron Classic and Aeron Remastered | Top-activated |
| Mirra 1 | Cable-actuated |
| Embody | Cable-actuated |
| Counter-height or drafting versions | Stool cylinder |

A top-activated cylinder is pushed directly by the height lever; a cable-actuated one is worked by a cable running to the mechanism. Buy the wrong type and it will not fit. Also worth knowing before you replace anything: the same installation guidance says that if a chair sinks after a cylinder swap, the fix may be adjusting the bolt or pin that presses the cylinder button, a quarter turn at a time with a hex key. A chair that sinks is not automatically a dead chair.

## What does "BIFMA tested" actually tell you about a used chair?

Less than the sticker suggests. ANSI/BIFMA X5.1 is a safety, strength and durability testing standard for general-purpose office chairs. It is not, by itself, a product certification, and "tested to X5.1" is a different claim from "BIFMA Compliant," which refers to a separate program and registry. When a listing claims certification, ask for four things: the exact model, the standard edition, the testing organization, and the report or program behind the claim.

Edition matters. The current X5.1 was released in February 2017 and replaced the 2011 edition, so a report citing 2011 is not the current version. Scope matters too: the standard contains 20 tests and not all of them apply to every chair, so a partial test list is not automatically a red flag.

This table shows the relevant standards and the weight range each one is written for.

| Standard | Covers | Weight basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ANSI/BIFMA X5.1 | General-purpose office chairs | Up to 275 lb (95th percentile male) |
| ANSI/BIFMA X5.11 | Chairs for heavier users | Above 253 lb, up to 400 lb |
| FNEW-83-269E | Continuous (24/7) use | Heavier loads, more cycles |

The two published ranges overlap between 253 lb and 275 lb, so treat that boundary as approximate rather than exact. The practical takeaway: if your weight falls above the X5.1 basis, ask which standard the chair was tested to before you buy it, because a chair built to X5.1 alone is not rated for that range. And "heavy duty" means nothing on a listing unless it names the standard and the lab.

## What should I check before I pay?

- A legible photograph of the manufacturing label, matching the advertised model.
- The seller's answers in writing: age, any replaced parts and their origin, and whether they are an authorized dealer.
- The lift through its full range while you sit in it, held at the top for a minute or two.
- The activation type if you expect to replace the lift later.
- Casters and star base: roll the chair, look for cracks and flat spots.
- Arm pads, back support, tilt tension and tilt lock. On mesh chairs, inspect where the mesh meets the frame.
- Return terms in writing, including who pays return shipping.

A chair that fits is only half a working setup. If the seat and arm heights end up where you want them, [desk height and keyboard position](/blog/desk-height-and-keyboard-position-setting-up-for-comfort/) determines whether your shoulders actually relax.

## When the checks rule the chair out

The checks above cost nothing but time. When they rule a chair out, these are the situations that follow and the kind of product that answers each one.

| Situation | What answers it |
| --- | --- |
| No legible label, no documented history | New chair from the manufacturer or an authorized dealer |
| Sinks under load, structure otherwise sound | Replacement gas lift matched to the activation type |
| User weight above the 275 lb X5.1 basis | Chair tested to BIFMA X5.11, or a 24/7 model tested to FNEW-83-269E |
| Cracked frame or mesh, dealer-only parts | New or authorized-dealer stock, not a rebuild |
