## Quick answer

- Off axis, a cardioid is roughly 6 dB down at 90° and about 20 dB down behind the capsule.
- Distance factor, the number that says how much further a directional mic can sit for the same direct-to-reflected ratio: cardioid 1.7, omni 1.0.
- So an omni 25 cm from your mouth gives the same direct-to-reflected ratio as a cardioid at about 43 cm.
- Shared desk with other voices in the room: cardioid. Quiet room, you alone: an omni can sound better.
- Published polar patterns are anechoic-chamber data, so your room will reject less than the chart suggests.

If you are choosing between two USB microphones for video calls and the main difference is pickup pattern, the room decides more than the brand does. Two facts settle it: who else is talking within a couple of metres of your desk, and how close the capsule can sit to your mouth given your monitor, keyboard and shelves.

## What does a pickup pattern actually describe?

A polar pattern (a chart of a microphone's sensitivity at each angle around its capsule) is what you are really comparing when you weigh a cardioid model against an omni one. Audio-Technica warns that polar patterns are run in an anechoic chamber (a room with no reflecting surfaces), simulating an ideal environment with no walls, ceiling or floor. In a real room, off-axis sound bounces back into the front of the microphone, so a cardioid rarely eliminates unwanted sound completely.

Chart conventions decide how much you can read into the shape. DPA explains that polar plots are normalized at 0° (the angle straight in front), so every curve touches 0 dB at the front even when the microphones differ in sensitivity, and that each shift between emphasized circles is usually a 5 dB step unless the maker says otherwise. DPA also notes that specifications can be measured or calculated in several ways, with IEC 60268-4 (the international standard covering measurement methods for sound system microphones, including directional response pattern) as the common ground, and that the same technical term is sometimes interpreted differently from brand to brand. Directivity can change with frequency, which is why patterns are commonly published in octave bands from 250 Hz to 16 kHz rather than as one shape. Curves may also have been smoothed, which DPA cautions can produce results that are exceptionally smooth, sometimes too smooth.

One distance caveat: DPA states directionality is most commonly measured at 1 to 2 m (3 to 6 ft), including for handheld microphones. Your mouth will be nearer, so read the pattern as a good description of shape and only a rough guide to level.

## How much does a cardioid reject off axis?

The table collects the attenuation figures DPA uses to define a cardioid, alongside LEWITT's typical rear rejection figure for the same pattern.

| Angle off axis | Attenuation (dB) | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ±66° | 3 | DPA |
| ±90° | 6 | DPA |
| 180° (behind the mic) | about 20 | LEWITT |

A 6 dB drop at 90° means a colleague sitting directly to the side of the capsule arrives at roughly a quarter of the power of your voice on axis. Audio-Technica frames the same figure as distance: of two speakers equidistant from the mic, one on axis and one at 90°, the off-axis one would sound twice as far away and would have to halve its distance to match levels. Audio-Technica also states that a cardioid can reduce noise from off-axis directions by about 67%, while cautioning that reflections in a real room mean you rarely get all of the directional capability built into the microphone.

Rear rejection is the stronger number. If your desk faces a doorway where people stop and talk, that figure earns its keep. If the noise is beside you rather than behind you, expect far less help.

Sonarworks adds a trade-off worth weighing: sound arriving from the sides or rear of a cardioid is not only quieter, it may also sound unnatural or tonally coloured, and that coloration is hard to correct later.

## How far should the mic sit from your mouth?

Distance factor (how much further a directional mic can sit than an omni while preserving the same ratio of direct to reflected sound) is the most useful single number for a desk. The table converts published factors into working distances, taking Audio-Technica's own example of an omni at 10 inches and a cardioid at 17 inches and restating it on a 25 cm reference.

| Pattern | Distance factor | Distance for the same ratio (cm) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Omni | 1.0 | 25 |
| Subcardioid | 1.2 | 30 |
| Cardioid | 1.7 | 43 |
| Hypercardioid | 2.0 | 50 |

The practical reading is that directionality buys distance: if your cardioid cannot sit around 40 cm from your mouth, you are not getting the pattern's isolation benefit. Closer than about two inches (5 cm), Audio-Technica notes that most cardioid microphones show a rising bass response, the proximity effect (bass boost as the source moves very close to a directional capsule). That is why a cardioid can sound heavy up close and thin once it drifts back, a behaviour omni designs do not share.

## Is an omni the better choice in a quiet room?

Sometimes yes. LEWITT states that an omni provides the best bass response, the flattest frequency response and the least sensitivity to handling or wind noise of all the polar patterns. Sonarworks notes that because omni capsules are pressure transducers (they respond to pressure at one point rather than to the difference between front and rear), they resist plosive p-pops and mechanical noise better than directional designs. If you work alone in a room with carpet or curtains and your complaint is a thin voice or thumping plosives, omni is the pattern that addresses it.

The catch is the same behaviour seen from the other side. LEWITT states that omni microphones do not isolate a single sound source, which it calls a significant problem in untreated rooms. They are also not perfectly non-directional: Audio-Technica notes that even the best omni models become directional at higher frequencies because the microphone body blocks shorter wavelengths arriving from the rear, so the smaller the body diameter, the closer the mic comes to true omni. LEWITT describes the extreme end of this, saying an omni takes on a figure-8 shape at the 16 kHz mark.

So the honest test is the room. If it is quiet and soft, an omni's bass and plosive behaviour are real gains. If it is not, the pattern's lack of isolation will show up in every call.

## What should you check before buying a desk microphone?

Work through these before comparing two listings.

- **Where the capsule can physically sit.** A cardioid needs roughly 40 cm of clear space in front of your face. Measure that against your monitor and shelf rather than trusting the product photo.
- **Who is beside you rather than behind you.** Rear rejection is strong while side rejection at 90° is about 6 dB, so a neighbour at your shoulder is the harder problem.
- **Whether the pattern is switchable.** A mic offering both cardioid and omni lets you test which suits the room, which matters more than most feature lists.
- **How it is mounted.** Check what the microphone stands on: a shock-mounted desk stand or boom arm is the lever you have against keyboard and desk noise reaching the capsule.
- **Power and ports.** Check how the microphone is powered and whether the port or hub it shares with a webcam and a light can spare it.
- **Where the camera sits.** Audio and framing are solved in the same square metre, so settle the screen position before you commit to a mic position.

## What to do when the pattern is not enough

The table maps four common desk situations to the kind of answer each one needs.

| Situation | What answers it |
| --- | --- |
| Shared desk, a colleague within a metre | Cardioid on a boom arm |
| Solo desk, bare walls, thin voice | Omni or switchable pattern |
| No room for a boom | Desk-stand cardioid |
| You move during calls | Lapel or headset mic |

The pattern is one variable; the mount, the distance to your mouth and the room are three more, and they are usually cheaper to change than the microphone itself. A cardioid that cannot sit around 40 cm from your face is a positioning problem before it is a pattern problem, and a reflective room asks for soft furnishings at the first reflection points before it asks for a different capsule.

The action worth taking now is to measure the distance from your mouth to wherever a mic could sit, then let that number choose the pattern.
