A desk setup is easy to buy and hard to get right, because the mistakes only appear once you are
living with it. The goal is not a photograph. It is a working space that still fits you when your
job, your screens, or your body change.

## Plan for the next three years

Ask what will be on the desk later, not just today. A second monitor, a larger laptop, a printer, or
a microphone all need somewhere to live. Leaving empty space is cheaper than replacing furniture.
Decide early whether you want to sit or stand, because that choice fixes the desk height and the
cables.

Leave one socket and one cable route free for what arrives later, and check the desk's maximum height
now if standing is possible.

## The ten-minute measurement pass

Four numbers decide most of the layout, and a tape measure and a hard chair are enough to take them.

- **Seated elbow height.** With your feet flat and your shoulders relaxed, measure from the floor to
  the underside of your elbow. OSHA's keyboard guidance puts the elbows at about keyboard height,
  with the forearms roughly parallel to the floor.
- **Popliteal height.** Measure from the floor to the crease behind your knee. The chair must adjust
  down to it, and the [chair sizing guide](/blog/chair-seat-depth-and-height-office-chair-sizing/)
  covers the matching seat depth.
- **Seated eye height.** Sitting straight, measure from the floor to your eyes. The top of the screen
  belongs at or slightly below that line.
- **Desk depth.** OSHA asks for at least 50 cm between your eyes and the screen. A 27 inch panel on a
  stand eats roughly 20 cm of depth, so a 60 cm desk leaves about 40 cm unless the screen sits at the
  back edge or on an arm.

OSHA also puts the free height under the desktop at roughly 50 to 72 cm and suggests a work surface no
thicker than about 5 cm.

## Height, fit, and posture first

The desk and chair work as a pair. Your elbows should rest near ninety degrees and your feet should
touch the floor or a footrest. A fixed-height desk only works if your chair can be raised to meet it,
which is why an adjustable chair often solves a problem people blame on the desk. If you might stand
later, a sit-stand frame is cheaper to add at the start than to retrofit.

## Depth beats width for most people

A deep desk keeps a large monitor at a comfortable distance and leaves room for your forearms. Width
is generous but can tempt clutter. Unless you know you need a very wide surface, favour depth and add
a small side table for the printer rather than buying the widest desk in the shop.

## Plan the room before the furniture

Measure the room as carefully as the desk: sockets, the wall that gets the daylight, radiators and
door swings, and how far the chair can roll back. Test that with the chair you own, because if you
cannot push back and stand without turning, no desk will help. A corner position adds depth, which is
OSHA's suggestion for fitting a large monitor.

## Cable management is a design decision

Decide where power, network, and display cables will run before the furniture arrives. A tray under
the desk, a channel up one leg, and a single power strip keep the floor clear and make cleaning and
moving easy. Velcro ties beat plastic zip ties because you will reopen them.

## Light and sight lines

Place the desk so you are not facing a window at eye level, which causes glare, or with a bright wall
behind you, which turns you into a silhouette on video calls. A monitor arm and a small task light
solve more problems than a bigger screen.

## Buy in the right order

Start with the chair and the desk, because they are expensive to change and affect your body. Then add
the monitor and its arm. Then storage and cable parts. Accessories are the easiest to get wrong, so
buy them last once you can see the real gaps.

Each purchase fixes the next constraint: the chair sets your seated height, which sets the height the
desk must reach, and the monitor's weight and mounting pattern decide which arm will hold it. Storage
and lighting depend on the final layout, so buying them early usually means buying them twice.

## When the budget covers one upgrade

Rank the options by hours of contact and reversibility.

- A footrest is OSHA's fix when a chair sits too high, and it costs far less than a replacement.
- A task lamp solves glare for a small outlay and moves with you.
- An adjustable chair changes the most hours of your day and follows you to the next desk; depth
  adjustment matters if it is shared.
- The desk comes last unless you already stand, because it is the least reversible purchase and the
  hardest to move.

## Guard against clutter creep

Give every device a home: a drawer for cables, a shelf for the printer, a hook for headphones. A
setup you cannot tidy in five minutes will not stay tidy. Simplicity here is not minimalism for its
own sake, it is the thing that makes the desk easy to use every day.

Plan for a workspace that can absorb one more screen and one more device without a rebuild, and you
will spend far less over the next few years than someone chasing a new desk every time their job
changes.

## A short checklist

- Measure elbow height, popliteal height, eye height, and desk depth before comparing furniture.
- Decide whether you will ever stand, and treat the desk's height range as the binding constraint.
- Check the free height under the desk and the thickness of the top.
- Choose the desk's position by sockets, window, and chair roll-back.
- Keep one free socket and one free cable run for the next device.
- Put the first upgrade into the equipment your body touches.

## Sources and further reading

- OSHA, Computer Workstations eTool: Desks. The source for the 50 centimetre minimum viewing distance, the 50 to 72 cm clearance, and the guidance on surface thickness.
- OSHA, Computer Workstations eTool: Keyboards, for elbows at keyboard height and forearms roughly parallel to the floor.
- OSHA, Computer Workstations eTool: Chairs, for the seat height and depth criteria and the footrest recommendation.
- ANSI/BIFMA G1, Ergonomics Guideline for Furniture Used in Office Work Spaces, the dimensional standard office furniture is commonly designed against. The document is paid, so use a manufacturer's summary.
- The specification sheet of any desk or chair you consider, for the height range and the depth adjustment.
