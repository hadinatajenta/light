## Quick answer

- Full containment under a desk: choose a closed format, a raceway or a closed sleeve. Spiral wrap is open by design and may not fully enclose the harness.
- If cables get added, swapped or rerouted, spiral wrap or helical-slit loom is the format that allows re-entry.
- Size spiral wrap slightly under the measured bundle diameter; oversized wrap slips along the run.
- Find the polymer and a stated UL 94 flammability rating on the datasheet, not the badge in the marketing copy.

Raceway, sleeve and spiral wrap answer two different questions: how much of the bundle you want covered, and how often you expect to get back inside the covering. Split loom and helical-slit loom sit between them. The right pick follows from the run, not from which format looks tidiest in a product photo.

## What is the actual difference between raceway, sleeve and spiral wrap?

The table compares the three formats on containment, re-entry and installation effort.

| Format | Encloses the bundle | Adding a cable later | Install effort |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Raceway (channel with lid) | Yes | Open the lid | Fix in place, lay cables in |
| Closed sleeve or tube | Yes | Re-thread or cut | Thread cables through |
| Spiral wrap | No, gapped | Press in through the slit | Wind on by hand |

A raceway is a rigid channel with a lid, and a closed sleeve is a tube: both present an unbroken wall once installed. Spiral wrap (a slit tube wound helically so cables are pressed in through the gaps) does not. The manufacturer's own guide states that spiral wraps have an open design and might not fully enclose a wire harness.

That single property settles most under-desk questions. Dust, spilled coffee, pet hair and a cable-chewing cat all reach an open bundle. If any of those are real conditions in your room, the open format fails the requirement no matter how neat the finished run looks.

## How much containment does your under-desk run actually need?

Closed wall versus open wall is the whole argument, so it is worth knowing what the closed options cost you at install.

Non-split corrugated tubing gives better abrasion resistance, because the closed wall leaves no entry point for debris, but it requires threading the cable through during installation. That constraint decides the purchase where the run passes behind a fixed panel or through a grommet. If a connector will not fit through the bore, a closed tube is the wrong buy regardless of its coverage.

Self-closing sleeving (a wrap-around sleeve that closes on itself without tools) is the middle path, but the manufacturer rates it for light abrasion only and says it is not appropriate for sharp edges or sustained heat exposure. A run dragged across the cut edge of a metal frame, or laid against a warm device, is outside its brief.

Where the cable rubs rather than sits still, braided sleeving (a woven fabric tube) is the better answer. The abrasion load spreads across the fabric instead of concentrating at one point, which is why it gets specified for tight bends and the flex zone of a height-adjustable frame.

One caution on the marketing: the spiral wrap guide's own promotional line calls the product perfect protection for anything that moves, and cites no test standard behind the phrase. Treat universal-protection wording as advertising, and ask for the material grade and the flammability rating instead.

## Will you add, swap or reroute cables after the first install?

This is where spiral wrap earns its place. The manufacturer positions it against braided sleeving on exactly this axis: if you need easy access to your cables for maintenance or repairs, spiral wrap is the format to choose. A sleeve that must be threaded is the choice when coverage matters more than later access.

Helical-slit loom, often sold as spiral cut loom, goes further. A continuous helical slit runs the length of the tube, so an individual wire can be pulled in or out at any point along the run without the sleeve being cut. For a desk that gains and loses devices, that is the format that survives a year of changes.

Both formats pay for that access with a gap. You are trading containment for serviceability, and it is worth being honest about which one you will actually use: if you will not re-enter the bundle, a closed format's coverage is worth more than a lid or slit you never open.

## How long should the sleeve be, and how do you size spiral wrap?

Length first. Sleeve and wrap are bought by the length of the run, and the run on a sit-stand desk is not the same length at both heights. Measure the route with the desk at its lowest position, then add the frame's travel plus a service loop at the fixed end, or the wrap pulls taut at full height.

For diameter, measure the outside diameter (the measurement across the whole bundle) at its fattest point, which is normally where a power brick lead crosses a display cable. Spiral wrap relies on being slightly undersized to grip. Choose the closest size slightly smaller than your measurement. Oversized wrap slips along the bundle, and forcing an undersized one on strains the cables inside it.

Published standard sizes run from roughly 0.062 x 0.500 inches up to 1 x 7 inches, in nylon or polyethylene. A listing quoting a single number without stating which dimension is which is a sizing trap for a mixed bundle of power and data cables.

Pitch (the spacing between turns) is set by you during installation, and it changes what you get. Closer spacing reduces flexibility but increases abrasion resistance; wider spacing allows sharper bends. Two buyers working from the same roll can end up with different products.

## Which polymer do you need, and where does heat change the answer?

Nylon is self-extinguishing, and the manufacturer advises confirming that the chosen product carries a UL 94 flammability rating (a standard test rating for how a plastic behaves near flame). That is the line item to find on a datasheet. A listing badge is not the same as a stated rating.

Where the wrap sits against a laptop power brick, an amplifier or a monitor's warm exhaust, specify a heat-stabilized grade, which limits the loss of physical properties as operating temperature rises.

Polyethylene is the budget polymer, specified where a temperature rating is not needed. That is the trade: lower price, lower ceiling. Nylon and polyethylene are both sold as spiral wrap at very different price points, so verify which one the listing ships. Color matters too: the UV resistance cited for polyethylene applies to the black version, which the manufacturer says can be used outdoors; clear or natural product does not carry that stated property.

Split loom adds a variable that photographs hide, because the shape looks identical across resins. Polyethylene is the cost option, polypropylene handles higher temperatures, and polyamide, meaning nylon, gives the best performance in high-temperature and high-flex use. Verify the resin on the datasheet, not the product image.

## Does bundling mains leads into one chase actually matter?

Modestly, yes. Grouping current-carrying conductors together reduces heat dissipation, and NEC Table 310.15(C)(1) requires an ampacity adjustment (the current a conductor can carry continuously without overheating) for bundled conductors. For four to six current-carrying conductors, the adjusted value is 80 percent of the original.

For a desk this is not a reason to skip cable management. It is a reason not to cram four mains leads and every data cable into one sealed chase and call it finished. Keep mains leads out of the tight data bundle where the raceway has room, and do not overfill a closed channel.

## What should you check before you buy?

- Settle the containment level first: closed for dust, spills or pets; open only if access genuinely beats coverage.
- Measure the bundle's outside diameter where the power brick lead crosses the display cable, then buy spiral wrap slightly under that figure.
- Confirm both dimensions on a spiral listing, and confirm the resin: nylon with a UL 94 rating, polyethylene only where heat is not a factor.
- Measure the route at the desk's lowest height, then add frame travel and a service loop before cutting anything to length.
- Check the fattest connector, not the cable diameter, against any raceway entry, tray lip or grommet.
- For a run behind a fixed panel, confirm the tube can be threaded before committing to a closed format.
- Leave mains leads out of the tight data bundle where the routing allows it.

## If the free fixes are not enough

Before buying anything, route the run along the desk frame's own channel, shorten the cables you can shorten, and lift the power brick off the floor. When those run out, the situations below are the ones that force a purchase.

| Situation | Format that answers it |
| --- | --- |
| Raceway adhesive fails on dusty or textured undersides | Under-desk tray or raceway mounted to the frame |
| Cables chafe at a sit-stand frame's flex point | Braided sleeving on the flex zone |
| Run must stay closed behind a fixed panel or grommet | Non-split corrugated tubing, threaded at install |
| Devices are added and removed weekly | Helical-slit loom or spiral wrap |
