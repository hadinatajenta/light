## Quick answer

- Measure seated hip breadth: the widest horizontal distance across your hips while sitting on a firm chair.
- Add 2 to 5 cm for clearance. That total is your minimum seat width.
- Your workable band runs from 20 mm below your measurement to 50 mm above it.
- A 43 to 51 cm seat width on a spec sheet is a starting filter, not proof of fit.

Seat width gets less attention than seat height or lumbar support, and it is the dimension most often settled by guesswork. A chair that feels roomy in a showroom can leave your forearms floating above the armrests at home, and two chairs listed at the same 48 cm can fit very differently once armrest spacing and your own hip breadth are in play. The measurement below takes a couple of minutes and turns the decision into arithmetic you can check against a spec sheet.

## How do you measure your seated hip breadth?

Sit on a firm chair, a dining chair rather than a sofa, with your feet flat and your weight settled evenly. Run a tape measure horizontally across the widest point of your hips and upper thighs, at seat level, keeping the tape parallel to the floor. Measure twice. Soft tissue spreads when you sit, so this number is not the same as your standing hip width or your waist measurement.

Wear the trousers you actually work in, since heavy fabric and full pockets add real millimetres, and take the measurement on a firm surface, because a soft low chair will spread you wider than a hard one.

Then add clearance. The commonly quoted allowance is 2 to 5 cm on top of the measurement, which becomes your minimum seat width. Take the low end if you want a contained feel and the high end if you change position often or wear layered clothing.

## What seat width should you look for on the spec sheet?

The tolerance band around your measurement is asymmetric, which is the part most buyers miss. A seat pan (the flat surface you sit on) can be up to 20 mm narrower than your measured hip breadth, because body weight is carried on the thighs rather than the outer edges. It should not be more than 50 mm wider. Those two limits define what to accept.

The table shows the workable seat width band for a range of hypothetical seated hip breadths, before armrest spacing is considered.

| Seated hip breadth (cm) | Narrowest workable seat (cm) | Widest workable seat (cm) |
|---|---|---|
| 38 | 36 | 43 |
| 42 | 40 | 47 |
| 46 | 44 | 51 |
| 50 | 48 | 55 |
| 54 | 52 | 59 |

On the 46 cm row, anything from 44 to 51 cm is physically sittable, and the 2 to 5 cm clearance rule narrows the sensible target to 48 to 51 cm. Note where the table runs out: the commonly quoted standard band of 43 to 51 cm, which one seating manufacturer ties to BIFMA data, covers the rows up to a 46 cm hip breadth. At 50 cm and above it stops describing you, and the label on a standard chair tells you nothing useful.

## Why is a wider seat not always more comfortable?

Width is often bought as insurance against feeling cramped, and it is the wrong lever. Fixed armrests sit at the outer edges of the seat, so every extra centimetre of pan width pushes them further from your body. Your forearms then rest on the inner edge of the pad or hang past it, which turns an armrest into a shelf you perch on rather than a support you lean on.

There is a costlier consequence. Wider armrests are more likely to collide with the underside of the desk, and when that happens you either raise the desk, which lifts the keyboard above elbow height, or sit further back, which increases eye-to-screen distance. Neither problem is solved by more seat width; extra width is what creates them.

Before you conclude that a chair is too narrow, rule out the two other culprits: a pan that is too long, which pushes the backrest away from the lumbar spine, and armrests set too close together. Measuring hip breadth first rules the width question out honestly.

## Do the certification badges say anything about seat width?

Mostly no, and the distinctions matter because marketing copy blurs them.

**EN 1335-1:2020** is the dimensional part of the European office chair standard. It specifies the dimensions of four types of office work chair plus the test methods used to determine them, which sounds like a fit guarantee and is not one. The dimensions aim to accommodate a larger part of the European population, and the standard notes that people outside that range may need different furniture or adaptations such as a footrest. A compliant chair can still be the wrong width for you. In Italy, EN 1335-1 together with EN 1335-2:2018 is the reference standard used to judge compliance with Legislative Decree 81/2008, so in regulated procurement ask a supplier which part they are citing.

**EN 1335-2:2018** covers safety, strength and durability on a basis of 8 hours of daily use by people weighing up to 110 kg. Above that weight, ask what test basis the chair was built to rather than accepting the badge.

**ISO 24496:2021** standardises how chair dimensions are measured and contains no dimensional requirements, so citing it says nothing about width.

**BIFMA X5.1** evaluates safety, durability and structural adequacy of general-purpose office chairs used by people up to 275 lb (described as the 95th percentile male). It is not a sizing standard.

**ANSI/BIFMA G1-2013** does publish dimensional ranges, but they span the 5th percentile female to the 95th percentile male, which describes an envelope rather than your body.

One check is worth making: manufacturers can self-declare BIFMA conformance, and only products listed in the BIFMA Compliant Registry have been tested by an accredited third-party lab.

## When do you need a wide-seat chair, and what else must you check?

Once hip breadth plus clearance passes about 51 cm, standard pans stop working and a wide-seat chair becomes the honest answer. These are offered in widths up to 61 cm (24 in) and beyond, which covers most of the people the standard band excludes.

Treat width and structural rating as two separate checks. A wider pan does not mean a stronger frame, and the weight standards split at a defined point: BIFMA X5.1 covers users up to 275 lb, while BIFMA X5.11 covers users from 253 lb to 400 lb (the 99.5th percentile male). A heavy-duty chair should be traceable to X5.11 rather than X5.1. Check too that the armrests adjust inward, since a wide seat with fixed arms reproduces the problem above at larger scale.

## What should you check before you pay?

- Measure seated hip breadth on a firm chair, twice, in work clothing.
- Add 2 to 5 cm and write the total down as your minimum seat width.
- Reject any seat more than 50 mm wider than your measurement, however roomy it felt.
- Accept a seat up to 20 mm narrower than your measurement, but no further.
- Check armrest width and inward adjustment, especially if the chair must slide under a desk.
- Confirm seat depth separately. Width will not fix a pan that presses behind the knee.
- Read the weight rating and the standard behind it, and verify BIFMA claims against the Compliant Registry.
- Measure desk clearance at the height you actually sit, not at the chair's lowest setting.

## When the standard chair is the wrong shape

Almost every seat-width problem has one of four answers, and only the first is about width at all. The table matches a common mismatch to the fix that resolves it.

| Situation | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Hip breadth plus clearance over 51 cm | Wide-seat task chair, with the weight rating checked separately |
| Seat width fine, armrests too far from the body | Width-adjustable or inward-mounting armrests |
| Feet off the floor at the correct seat height | Adjustable footrest |
| Badge claimed, no registry listing | Test standard and third-party listing from the supplier |
