Specification sheets exist to sell a product, not to inform you. They are written by people who know
exactly which numbers flatter their design and which ones quietly undermine it. Learning to read them
is a durable skill that saves money on every purchase.

## Ask what the number measures

Every specification has a method behind it, and the method decides the result. A battery figure might
be measured at half brightness with radios off. A contrast figure might be a theoretical ratio rather
than a measured one. Before you compare two numbers, ask whether they were produced the same way. If
the sheet does not say, treat the number as advertising.

## Watch for missing context

The most telling sign of a weak product is what the sheet leaves out. If a monitor lists refresh rate
but not panel type, or headphones list battery life but not the volume used, the omission is the
message. A good sheet states the conditions because it is proud of them.

## Beware of the impressive but irrelevant

Large numbers catch the eye even when they do not matter. Extremely high refresh rates, huge peak
brightness, and enormous megapixel counts sound exciting and often describe conditions you will never
reach. Match each number to a real task and discard the ones that do not.

## Read the fine print and the footnotes

Manufacturers hide the important caveats in footnotes and asterisks. There you find that a port is
not full speed, that a feature needs an app, that a cable is sold separately, or that the quoted
figure applies only in one mode. The footnotes are often the most honest part of the page.

## Compare the right things

Line up candidates in a table using only the specifications that affect your use. If a candidate
cannot supply a number, mark it unknown rather than assuming the best. Then compare like with like,
and remember that a well-documented modest product usually beats a vague impressive one.

## Check the real-world reports

Specifications describe a new unit in ideal conditions. Owner reviews describe the product after a
year. Look for repeated themes rather than single complaints: a port that fails, a finish that wears,
a fan that whines. Patterns across many reviews are more reliable than any single rating.

## A short method to keep

Write your two or three needs, list only the specifications that serve them, and ignore everything
else. Ask what each number measures and under what conditions. Look for what is missing. Then check
long-term owner reports. This method turns a confusing sheet into a clear decision, and it works for
monitors, laptops, storage, and almost anything else you will buy again.
