Specification sheets exist to sell a product, not to inform you. They are written by people who know
exactly which numbers flatter their design and which ones quietly undermine it. Learning to read them
is a durable skill that saves money on every purchase.

## Ask what the number measures

Every specification has a method behind it, and the method decides the result. A battery figure might
be measured at half brightness with radios off. A contrast figure might be a theoretical ratio rather
than a measured one. Before you compare two numbers, ask whether they were produced the same way. If
the sheet does not say, treat the number as advertising.

## Which numbers come from standards

Some figures come from a published method, which makes them comparable across brands.

- **VESA DisplayHDR.** The certification states its requirements per tier: DisplayHDR 400 asks for
  400 cd/m² at a small centre patch and 320 cd/m² across a full screen flash, so the entry badge
  describes a modest peak on a small area.
- **USB-IF.** Certification ties a figure to a data rate. The same 5 Gbps link has been sold as USB
  3.0, USB 3.1 Gen 1, and USB 3.2 Gen 1, so the rate is checkable and the version name is not.
- **IEC 60529.** An IP rating states two digits: the first for dust, the second for water. A sheet
  that prints IPX4 claims splash resistance and says nothing about dust.
- **ENERGY STAR.** Energy programmes define how power is measured, including standby, so their
  figures are comparable in a way that a marketing wattage is not.

Safety marks are separate: UL, ETL, and CE certify a safety requirement, not performance.

## Watch for missing context

The most telling sign of a weak product is what the sheet leaves out. If a monitor lists refresh rate
but not panel type, or headphones list battery life but not the volume used, the omission is the
message. A good sheet states the conditions because it is proud of them.

## Read a range, not a peak

A range has two endpoints, and they answer different questions. A chair with a seat height range of
42 to 52 cm fits a shorter user at 42 and a taller one at 52, so the number that rules it out is the
minimum. A battery figure of up to 14 hours describes one test at one brightness; the figure at your
brightness is usually a footnote rather than a second number. When a figure is a maximum, look for the
condition that shrinks it.

## Beware of the impressive but irrelevant

Large numbers catch the eye even when they do not matter. Extremely high refresh rates, huge peak
brightness, and enormous megapixel counts sound exciting and often describe conditions you will never
reach. Match each number to a real task and discard the ones that do not.

## Read the fine print and the footnotes

Manufacturers hide the important caveats in footnotes and asterisks. There you find that a port is
not full speed, that a feature needs an app, that a cable is sold separately, or that the quoted
figure applies only in one mode. The footnotes are often the most honest part of the page.

## What a missing measurement implies

The absences tell you which questions the seller cannot answer. A monitor listing resolution but not
panel type is selling the pixel count. Dynamic contrast with no static contrast usually means the
native figure is unremarkable. A monitor weight that excludes the stand is useless for arm shopping
until you find the figure with it.

Follow up rather than walking away: retail listings routinely drop details the manufacturer's
datasheet contains, so search for the manual before rejecting the product. When the answer decides the
purchase, ask the seller in writing and keep the reply.

## Compare the right things

Line up candidates in a table using only the specifications that affect your use. If a candidate
cannot supply a number, mark it unknown rather than assuming the best. Then compare like with like,
and remember that a well-documented modest product usually beats a vague impressive one.

## A worked comparison of two hypothetical sheets

Both sheets below are invented for the exercise; the difference in shape is the lesson.

Sheet A states 27 inch, 3840 x 2160, IPS, 350 cd/m², static contrast 1000:1, height adjustable
stand, 75 x 75 and 100 x 100 VESA, USB-C with 65 W power delivery, three year warranty.

Sheet B states 27 inch, 4K, HDR, 178 degree viewing angle, dynamic contrast 100,000,000:1, 1 ms
response, USB-C, slim bezel.

Sheet A answers five questions you can act on: how sharp, how bright, how adjustable, what it mounts
to, and what it charges. Sheet B answers one, the resolution, and surrounds it with figures that need
a method before they mean anything. Only one of the sheets lets you predict what happens on your desk.

## Check the real-world reports

Specifications describe a new unit in ideal conditions. Owner reviews describe the product after a
year. Look for repeated themes rather than single complaints: a port that fails, a finish that wears,
a fan that whines. Patterns across many reviews are more reliable than any single rating.

## A short method to keep

Write your two or three needs, list only the specifications that serve them, and ignore everything
else. Ask what each number measures and under what conditions. Look for what is missing. Then check
long-term owner reports. This method turns a confusing sheet into a clear decision, and it works for
monitors, laptops, storage, and almost anything else you will buy again.

## A short checklist

- Write down two or three needs and only the figures that serve them.
- For each figure, find the method and the conditions, or treat it as advertising.
- For each range, check which endpoint could rule the product out.
- List what the sheet omits, and turn each omission into a question.

## Sources and further reading

- VESA Certified DisplayHDR, performance criteria, for the per tier luminance requirements.
- USB Implementers Forum, for the USB naming and Power Delivery specifications.
- International Electrotechnical Commission, IEC 60529, for the meaning of the two IP digits.
- ENERGY STAR program requirements for displays, as an example of a test method that defines how power is measured.
- OSHA, Computer Workstations eTool, for the ergonomic figures used when a sheet describes a chair, a desk, or a keyboard.
- The manufacturer's datasheet or user manual, which usually contains the test conditions a retail listing leaves out.
