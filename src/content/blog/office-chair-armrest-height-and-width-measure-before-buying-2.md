## Quick answer

- Measure two numbers first: the lowest underside clearance of your desk, and the height of the armrest tops from the floor with the seat at the height you actually sit at. The armrest number has to come in under the desk number.
- Measure the opening between the desk legs, or between a pedestal and the nearest leg, not only the gap under the front edge.
- Set the seat before the arms. Seat height and seat depth decide how far the armrests can drop.
- Treat "adjustable" as a question, not a specification. Ask for the travel in inches and the direction each adjustment moves.

Armrests are the last part of a chair most buyers check and the first thing that decides whether they can sit close to the desk. "Adjustable" on a listing says almost nothing. What matters is whether the armrests travel far enough down, and far enough in and out, to fit your desk and your body.

## How do you measure armrest clearance under your desk?

Sit at the seat height you actually use and measure from the floor to the top of the armrest. Take the same measurement for your desk: floor to the lowest point of the underside, checking the front edge and the area further back where a rail or drawer box may hang lower. The armrest number has to come in below the desk number.

Measure at your working seat height, not the chair's lowest setting. Armrest height changes with seat height, so a chair that clears the desk when dropped down may not clear it once you raise the seat to suit your legs.

The table sets out the measurements that decide the fit, with the seat and work-surface figures taken from Colorado State University's work-from-home ergonomics guidelines.

| Measurement | How to measure it | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Armrest top height from the floor | Floor to the top of the armrest, seat at working height | Below desk clearance |
| Desk underside clearance | Floor to the lowest point under the desk, front edge and further back | Above armrest height |
| Seat height range | Chair specification sheet | Roughly 16-21 in |
| Fixed seat length | Chair specification sheet | Under 16.3 in |
| Work surface thickness | At the desk edge | Under 1.5 in |

A chair can clear the front edge and still foul a support rail behind it, which pushes you away from the keyboard exactly when you wanted to sit closer.

Width is the check people skip. Measure the opening between the desk legs, or between a pedestal and the nearest leg. Armrests that clear vertically can still catch a leg. CSU also warns against leg obstructions such as pencil drawers and file drawers; when a drawer box sits in the knee well (the space under the desktop where your legs go), no armrest setting buys back that space.

## Which armrest adjustments actually change the fit?

CSU lists four armrest adjustments worth checking. Each one changes a different part of the fit.

| Adjustment | What it changes |
|---|---|
| Height | Whether the pads clear your desk |
| Width | The distance between the pads and your forearms |
| Fore/aft | Where the pad sits along your forearm |
| Pivot | Whether the pad swings out of the way |

Ranges differ more than the labels suggest. UCLA's office equipment catalogue describes the Steelcase Amia's optional 4-way arms as pivoting in and out, moving up and down, side to side, and back and forth. That is one manufacturer's definition of four-way; another chair may use the same phrase for a narrower set of directions, so read the directions and the travel distances rather than the label.

If you recline often, fore/aft travel matters more than width, because the gap between the chair back and the armrests widens as you lean back and your forearms lose the pads. Ask where the width adjustment sits and whether you can reach it while seated; a setting you cannot reach from the chair is a setting you will not change.

If I had to choose, I would pay for height adjustment and pivot first. Height decides whether the chair works with the desk; pivot clears the pads when you want them out of the way.

## Why do seat height and seat depth come before armrest settings?

Armrest height is measured relative to the seat, so setting the seat sets the arms. Work out the seat height you need from your popliteal height (the distance from the floor to the crease behind your knee, which is the height that lets your feet rest flat). If it is 42 cm, the seat has to adjust down to about 42 cm; a chair whose lowest setting is 45 cm will not work, whatever the arms do. CSU's suggested range is a starting point, and the guidelines note that some people need slightly higher or lower.

Seat length matters as well. CSU caps the length of a fixed seat because a longer seat pan (the part you sit on) pushes you forward, away from the armrests and closer to the desk edge than you planned. The [guide to chair seat depth and height](/blog/chair-seat-depth-and-height-office-chair-sizing/) walks through those measurements.

One detail that trips people up: the height you measure from the floor includes the castors (the wheel assemblies on the chair base). Compare chairs as they stand on your floor, not as a specification sheet implies. Castors also decide how the chair rolls on a rug or a mat, which the guide to office chair casters covers.

If raising the seat for a fixed desk leaves your feet hanging, an adjustable footrest brings the floor up to your feet. That is a fix for the desk, not a substitute for getting the seat height right.

## What do drawer units and desk thickness do to armrest clearance?

CSU sets a limit on how thick the work surface should be and tells buyers to avoid leg obstructions such as pencil drawers and file drawers. Both limits are structural rather than stylistic: a thick top plus an apron eats under-desk height before the chair arrives, and a drawer box takes the depth your knees need.

When the knee well is the problem, the options are removing the drawer and storing the contents elsewhere, choosing a chair whose arms pivot away, or lowering the keyboard so you do not have to sit as close. A clamp-on under-desk keyboard tray moves the keyboard below the work surface and frees the desk edge; check its clamp range against your desktop thickness before buying. If the desk itself is the constraint, planning a desk upgrade without replacing everything sets out the order to do things in.

## What does ANSI/BIFMA X5.1 tell you about a chair's armrests?

ANSI/BIFMA X5.1 is a test standard for general-purpose office chairs covering safety, strength and durability. It is not, by itself, a product certification. The tested areas include backrest and arm strength, seat impact and durability, stability, swivel and tilt mechanisms, and castors. The task chairs in UCLA's catalogue are listed as meeting X5.1, which makes the standard a useful institutional filter rather than a fit guarantee.

BIFMA Compliant is a separate verification programme with laboratory requirements, a public product registry and report auditing, so a generic BIFMA logo does not put a chair in that programme. If a seller claims certification, check the exact model, the standard edition, the testing organisation and the report behind the claim. Quoted durability cycle counts from different tests are not comparable with one another and do not translate into a guaranteed number of years of service.

For armrests, an arm strength test shows the arm takes load. It says nothing about whether the arm drops low enough for your desk at the height you sit. That number comes from the listing or the seller, and it is the one to ask for.

## What should you check before you buy?

- Measure the armrest top height from the floor at your working seat height, and the desk underside clearance at its lowest point.
- Measure the opening between the desk legs or pedestal, and look for drawers or rails in the knee well.
- Ask for the armrest height range in inches, and whether it is measured from the seat or the floor.
- Check whether the width adjustment is reachable while you are seated, if you expect to change it.
- Check fore/aft travel if you recline often, since the support gap widens as the back reclines.
- Check seat height range against your popliteal height, and fixed seat length against CSU's cap.
- Check desk thickness against CSU's limit if you plan to add a clamp-on accessory.
- Ask which standard edition and which test report sit behind any BIFMA claim.
