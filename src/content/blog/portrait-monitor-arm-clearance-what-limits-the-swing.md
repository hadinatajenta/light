## Quick answer

- Portrait rotation comes from the tilter head — the pivoting plate at the end of the arm — not from arm length.
- Each joint turns through 360 degrees only when nothing blocks it; the wall behind the desk is the usual block.
- Size the arm on the panel's net weight (the display alone, factory stand removed), not on the advertised screen size.
- A gas-spring arm (one that counterbalances the screen with a pressurised cylinder) can have a minimum weight as well as a maximum: Atdec's AWM-AHX is rated from 6 kg to 16 kg (13 lb to 35 lb).

Two monitor arms can look identical in a product photo and behave completely differently the moment you try to stand a screen on its side. The difference usually sits in the tilter head, in how far the panel's rear housing projects behind the mounting plate, and in what is behind the desk. This guide works through those constraints in the order they bite, so you can tell which of two candidate arms will hold a vertical screen where you want it.

The advice here assumes you have already decided an arm beats a fixed stand. If you are still choosing between the two, the clearance questions below are the ones that decide it.

## Does a longer arm let a monitor rotate to portrait?

No. Reach and rotation are separate specifications, and buying more reach does not buy you rotation. Ergomart's component guide states that the tilter head attached to the arm provides tilt and portrait/landscape rotation, and that the range of tilt depends on the tilter head being used. Two arms from the same maker can carry different tilter heads, so the tilter specification is the thing to read, not the arm's length.

The second route to portrait runs through the VESA plate (the plate that matches the hole pattern on the back of the display, set by the Video Electronics Standards Association). Ergomart documents 100 x 100 mm adaptor plates that attach through a single central hole. Fit one of those to a bracket with a matching centre hole and the screen swings between portrait and landscape without touching four screws. Note that the VESA standard defines the edge-mount position suffixes — top, bottom, right and left — as seen from the back in landscape mode: the reference orientation on paper is landscape even when the screen will live vertically.

Dual brackets are a separate case. A dual bracket lets both monitors rotate from landscape to portrait, but height, tilt and swivel move both screens together, so individual adjustment is limited, and the two panels plus the bracket must still fall inside the arm's weight range.

## Where does a portrait swing actually bind?

Arms articulate at the base, the elbow and the tilter head, and each point offers a full 360 degrees of rotation when the mounting surface or the attached peripherals do not inhibit movement. That qualifier does all the work. What limits a portrait swing is physical obstruction, not the data sheet: the wall or partition behind the desk, a shelf above, the cable path, or the panel's own rear housing meeting the arm body as it comes round.

Measure the swing rather than the arm. The table shows how much taller a 16:9 panel stands once rotated, using diagonal-to-side arithmetic and rounded values; the figures describe the image area and ignore bezels and chassis, which vary by model.

| Panel (16:9) | Landscape width (mm) | Portrait height (mm) | Top edge rises (mm) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 24-inch | 531 | 531 | 116 |
| 27-inch | 598 | 598 | 131 |
| 32-inch | 708 | 708 | 155 |

A 27-inch panel loses about 262 mm of width and gains the same amount of height when it turns. Because the pivot sits near the centre of the plate, the top edge climbs roughly 131 mm and the bottom edge drops the same distance. That drop is why the pivot has to sit high enough to clear the desk surface and any keyboard tray lip, and why vertical travel matters in portrait even on a desk where the landscape position felt comfortable. On the SERIES-118 family Ergomart publishes, vertical adjustment runs from 10 in to 28 in depending on the model, a spread wide enough that the lowest-travel version may not lift a tall portrait screen clear of the desk.

## How much room does the back of the monitor need?

Rear travel is the dimension buyers forget. A desk that faces a wall can leave no room for the arm body or the cable path behind the display, even when the clamp and the VESA pattern both check out. Measure the horizontal gap between the back of the panel and the nearest obstruction at the height the panel will occupy in portrait, not at desk height, because the top of a rotated screen sits somewhere the wall may be closer.

A hypothetical example makes the arithmetic concrete. Suppose the clamp post sits 90 mm in front of the wall and the panel projects 70 mm behind its VESA face once rotated onto its side. That leaves 20 mm of arm travel before contact, and a display cable with a straight connector will use most of it. A right-angle connector buys back a little depth; nothing buys back a wall.

Reach varies just as much as travel within a single product family. The published SERIES-118 table spans 16 in (SAA179KIT) to 44 in (SAA4229KIT) of horizontal reach, 10 in to 28 in of vertical adjustment, and 3 lb to 55 lb per monitor. A family name is not a specification, and the exact model number is.

## Will the monitor still balance once it is vertical?

Weight ratings are where most compatibility mistakes happen, because three different weight figures get quoted for the same display. The table shows which one an arm rating refers to.

| Figure quoted | What it includes | Fit it to an arm rating? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Net panel weight | Display only, stand removed | Yes |
| Weight with factory stand | Panel plus stand | No |
| Shipping weight | Panel, stand, box, packing | No |

KTC states that arm ratings apply to the net weight of the display panel itself, not the packaged weight and not the weight with the original stand. Atdec publishes that no safety margin needs to be left on its mounts, with capacity applying to display weight without the stand, but rating conventions differ between manufacturers, so the manual for the exact model is what settles whether a margin is required. Treat the no-margin statement as a manufacturer claim and verify it against the document for the arm you are buying.

Gas-spring arms add a failure mode at the bottom of the range. The AWM-AHX requires monitors of at least 6 kg (13 lb) and covers 6 kg to 16 kg (13 lb to 35 lb), with pressure adjustment needed outside the ideal band. A light panel can therefore be out of range downwards, which is the opposite of what most buyers check. Gas springs can also lose pressure over long periods, and Atdec warns that warranty coverage differs between makers: cite the arm warranty and the gas canister warranty separately when comparing two options.

Orientation does not change weight, so a panel that is compliant in landscape is compliant in portrait. What changes is the load path, because the tilter is the part holding the screen in its new attitude, and that is exactly the joint that curved and ultrawide panels stress. KTC notes that the extra depth of these panels moves the centre of gravity forward, producing downward tilt drift even when total weight is inside the limit, and HUANUO notes that a mount which works with a 24-inch panel may struggle with a 34-inch ultrawide of the same weight because the larger screen creates more leverage. If you plan to rotate often, the tilter's documented rotation range is the number to confirm before ordering.

## Which desk and clamp limits decide where the arm sits?

Three clamp dimensions decide whether the arm mounts at all: jaw range against desk thickness, underside clearance, and throat depth, which is how far in from the edge the jaw reaches before the pole. A deep desk lip or a beadboard back can sit inside that throat and stop the clamp seating flush, and no amount of adjusting the arm fixes a base that never sat down.

A clamp point-loads the desktop. Thin hollow-core tops can dimple or crack within weeks, and the fix is a spreader plate, a flat metal or thick rubber pad between clamp and desk that spreads the force over a wider area. Many arms ship with one; check the box contents before assuming you need a separate plate.

One dealbreaker outranks all of this. Removing the factory stand is a prerequisite for using the VESA holes, but where a display's electronics are housed in the stand, the stand must not be removed, and that monitor will not mount to any arm at all.

## What should you check before you buy?

1. The tilter specification for the exact model, since portrait rotation is a tilter function and not an arm-reach function.
2. Net panel weight, stand removed, against both the minimum and maximum of the arm's range.
3. Rear housing depth, measured from the VESA face to the deepest point of the back, plus the bend a display cable needs.
4. Rotated height against vertical travel: a 27-inch 16:9 panel stands about 598 mm tall in portrait.
5. The gap between the panel and the wall at portrait height, not only at desk height.
6. Clamp jaw range, underside clearance and throat depth, plus any desk lip or bead behind the edge.
7. A spreader plate if the top is thin or hollow core.
8. Whether the display maker's own screws are required, because the VESA standard says they override the screws supplied with the bracket when a recessed housing or spacers change the length needed.

## If the free fixes are not enough

When measurement shows the desk or the display cannot meet the arm halfway, these are the situations where a different product, rather than a different setting, is the answer. The table maps each situation to the kind of mount that resolves it.

| Situation | What answers it |
| --- | --- |
| Desk faces a wall with almost no rear travel in portrait | Wall-mounted arm with a folding elbow, or a short-reach arm clamped at the side edge |
| Panel lighter than a gas arm's stated minimum | Mechanical-spring or fixed-height arm rated down to the panel's net weight |
| Curved or ultrawide panel drifting down at the tilt joint | Arm with a heavier-duty or lockable tilt head, chosen against forward centre of gravity, not weight alone |
| Electronics housed in the factory stand | A different display; no arm can take this one |
