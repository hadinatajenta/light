## Task lighting and ambient lighting do different jobs

Most people who dislike their desk lighting have bought one lamp and asked it to do two jobs at once. Task lighting concentrates higher illuminance on a small work area: a keyboard, a document, a notebook. Ambient lighting fills the room so your eyes are not constantly comparing a bright desk against a dark wall. The two are specified differently and they fail in different ways, so the useful question is not which lamp to buy but which layer is missing.

If your room is already evenly lit and your only complaint is that documents look dim, you need task light. If the room is dark and the desk glows like an island, a brighter lamp will make things worse. That is an ambient problem, usually solved by a ceiling fixture, a wall wash, or a lamp head that can be aimed upward at a pale ceiling.

## Task lighting: aim it, then measure it

The controlling number for task lighting is illuminance at the work surface, in lux. Not lumens, which describe a source's total output, and not watts, which describe power draw. EN 12464-1 in Europe and IES RP-1 in North America both publish recommended maintained illuminance values by task type, and the values differ between reading handwriting, drafting, and screen work. Check the table for your task instead of trusting a general figure.

Manufacturer lux figures only mean something with a distance attached. A datasheet quoting a high lux value at 300 mm tells you nothing about what reaches your desk if the head sits 600 mm away. For a small source, illuminance falls off steeply with distance, roughly following an inverse square relationship, though a wide diffused panel loses less than the arithmetic suggests because it behaves as an extended source.

Verification takes two minutes. Put an inexpensive lux meter flat on the desk, set the lamp in its normal working position with other room lights at their normal levels, and measure at the keyboard, at the document position, and at the outer edge of the lit pool where your eyes will be adapting. Beam angle shapes that pool: task lamps commonly fall between 30 and 60 degrees, and a narrow beam puts more light where you want it but shows a hard edge if it is aimed badly.

## Ambient lighting: the contrast problem

Eye strain at a desk is often a contrast problem rather than a brightness problem. When the lit patch is far brighter than the surrounding walls, your pupils keep adjusting as your gaze moves from paper to screen to room, and adding more light to the desk alone does not fix it.

Practical measures: bounce light off a light-coloured ceiling, keep the area behind the monitor from going fully dark, and avoid a single unshielded lamp pointed straight into your field of view. Glare deserves separate attention. EN 12464-1 uses Unified Glare Rating to set limits for office tasks, commonly UGR \u2264 19, and a lamp with a diffuser, louver, or recessed LED will generally sit lower on that scale than a bare strip of diodes.

If you cannot modify the room, a lamp that adjusts in both directions, down for the work surface and up for the ceiling, covers both roles with one fixture. That is a compromise, and for renters and shared rooms where ceiling work is not allowed, it is often the right one.

## Specifications that change how a lamp feels to use

These belong on your shortlist because they affect comfort daily, not just on the day of purchase.

* Colour temperature (CCT), in Kelvin. Office tasks commonly sit between 3000K and 5000K. Tunable white is only useful if the range and the control method are documented.
* Colour rendering index (CRI or Ra). Above 80 is typical for general desk work; above 90 matters when you judge colour, such as print proofs, fabric, or paint samples.
* Flicker. Ask for a flicker percentage or flicker index. IEEE 1789-2015 gives recommended practice for acceptable flicker levels, and a low-ripple driver is the underlying fix. "Flicker-free" on its own describes visibility, not measurement.
* Dimming protocol. TRIAC, ELV, 0-10V, DALI, and PWM are not interchangeable. A mismatched dimmer can buzz, flicker, or stop well short of the bottom of its range.
* Beam angle and range of motion. Check how far the arm and head travel, and whether the joints hold position under their own weight.
* Voltage and plug type. Imported lamps with external power supplies may be rated for a mains voltage that does not match your region.
* Photobiological safety. IEC 62471 assigns risk groups from RG0 to RG3; RG0 or RG1 is the comfortable end if blue light exposure concerns you.

## Claims that carry no information

Some of the largest text on the box is the least useful.

"Equivalent to X watts incandescent" compares power consumption, not brightness. Two lamps with the same watt equivalence can differ substantially in lumen output, so compare lumens and lux at your working distance.

"Eye-caring" is not a standardised term. "Full spectrum" and "natural light" are not regulated either; without a CRI value or a published spectral power distribution graph, they describe nothing you can check.

"50,000 hours" means nothing without conditions. LED life is normally quoted as L70, the point at which output has fallen to 70 percent of initial lumens, projected from IES LM-80 test data using IES TM-21 methodology. If the datasheet does not cite that framework, treat the figure as decoration. Likewise, "dimmable" without a protocol or a compatible-dimmer list is a coin flip.

## Who each approach suits

A single adjustable lamp that throws light both up and down suits renters, people in shared bedrooms, and anyone working after dark in a room with no ceiling fixture. CRI above 90 pays off only if you judge colour; if you mostly read screens, that money is better spent on flicker quality and glare control. Colour-critical work changes the calculus again, and a lamp with a replaceable bulb such as E26, E27, or GU10 lets you change CCT or CRI later without replacing the fixture, at the cost of being tied to whatever bulb you can source.

Two groups should wait before buying. If your office already has even overhead lighting and your screen is comfortable, a task lamp adds glare risk more often than comfort. And if the monitor is the only thing you look at, raising illuminance around it can wash out the image. Ambient balance matters more than a brighter desk.

## Heat, joints, and the parts that wear out

LEDs generate heat, and where it goes affects both lifespan and colour stability. An aluminium heat sink dissipates it better than a plastic housing. Metal in the joints, arms, and base resists the slow failure that shows up as a head sagging out of position after a year of daily adjustment; plastic joints wear faster under the same handling. Base weight matters too, since a light base with long reach tips as the arm extends.

Integrated LEDs cannot be replaced, so their rated life and warranty carry more weight. A three to five year warranty is a reasonable signal of manufacturer confidence. Look for UL, ETL, CE, or RoHS marks on the product or packaging; their absence is a reason to keep looking.

## Before you buy: a checklist

* Identify the missing layer first. A dark room with a bright desk means you need ambient light, not a stronger task lamp.
* Look up the recommended maintained illuminance for your task in EN 12464-1 or IES RP-1, then confirm the datasheet quotes lux at a distance close to how you will position the head.
* Plan to verify with a lux meter at the keyboard and document positions once the lamp is on the desk.
* Confirm CRI (above 80 general, above 90 for colour work), the CCT range, and whether tunability is continuous or stepped.
* Ask for a flicker figure or evidence of a low-ripple driver rather than accepting "flicker-free".
* Check glare control: diffuser, louver, or shielded LED, plus a UGR value if one is published.
* Match dimming to your existing system and confirm there is a dimmer compatibility list.
* Measure beam angle, arm reach, head travel, and base footprint against the desk you own.
* Verify mains voltage and plug type, particularly on imports with external power supplies.
* Prefer metal heat sinks and metal joints, check the warranty length, and look for UL, ETL, CE, or RoHS marks.
* For smart lamps, confirm whether a hub or bridge is required and which ecosystems are supported before buying.
