## How to size a desk for a monitor

Most desk buyers choose a top by how it looks in a room, then discover that a monitor 24 inches from their eyes is uncomfortable. The fix is not a better arm or a wider top. Decide your viewing distance first, then buy the depth that supports it. Here is the order in which the numbers constrain each other: viewing distance, depth, width, then the hardware details that quietly shrink both.

## Viewing distance is the input, not the output

OSHA's Computer Workstations eTool recommends keeping the screen 20 to 40 inches (50 to 100 cm) from your eyes. That is a wide band, and where you land inside it depends on screen size, resolution, and how your eyes behave after six hours. The general rule is that a larger panel asks for more distance, because the outer edges move further into peripheral vision and require more head movement to scan. Some makers publish a recommended viewing distance in the user manual, and it can sit at the far end of the OSHA range.

The part that catches people: viewing distance is not a property of the monitor. It is a property of where you sit relative to the screen, which makes it a property of the desk.

## The depth arithmetic most buyers never run

When you sit at a desk, your eyes sit behind the front edge by roughly your torso depth plus the clearance you leave for the keyboard. For many people that is 10 to 14 inches. Take a hypothetical: you want a 30-inch viewing distance and your eyes sit 12 inches behind the front edge. The screen surface has to land 18 inches from the front edge. That 18 inches is a floor, not a target, because the monitor needs somewhere to stand.

A monitor stand and base occupy depth behind the screen face. If the spec sheet lists a stand depth of 8 inches, that 18-inch floor becomes roughly 26 inches of required desk depth. A 24-inch desk can still work, but only if you accept a viewing distance near 28 inches or switch to a slim stand or an arm.

Rear obstructions shrink the number further. Privacy panels, cable trays, and modesty panels stop the monitor travelling backwards. Measure clear depth from the front edge to the first thing the monitor would touch, and use that figure instead of the nominal depth in the listing.

## Width is about screen count and angle

For a single monitor, width rarely binds. A 60-inch-wide desk swallows one 27-inch panel with room for a lamp and a notebook. Dual and triple setups are different. No standard covers multi-monitor desk width, so the calculation is yours: add the panel widths, add the bezels, then add the footprint created by angling the outer screens toward your seat. Angling a 27-inch panel inward pushes its rear corner further back than a flat arrangement does, which matters on a shallow top.

If the sum exceeds your desk width, the honest options are fewer panels, a stacked arrangement, or an arm that holds both screens from one clamp. A dual arm also removes two monitor bases from the desktop, returning several inches to your keyboard and forearms. If that is the direction you are heading for a two-screen setup, the MOUNTUP Dual Monitor Desk Mount is one of the entries in our list, and the checks are the same as for any arm: combined weight capacity and VESA pattern support. "Fits any monitor" is worth ignoring unless the weight range and hole pattern are printed on the page.

## Stand depth, VESA, and weight

Three numbers decide whether a monitor and a desk work together.

Stand depth comes first. Manufacturer specifications list stand dimensions, and the base footprint matters as much as the screen. V-shaped and flat bases can need more front-to-back room than a round pole base, because the feet extend forward under the panel.

Second is the mounting interface. Arms and wall mounts follow the VESA Flat Display Mounting Interface Standard, with 75 x 75 mm and 100 x 100 mm being the common patterns. The clamp or grommet also has to fit your desk's thickness, and clamp ranges are usually published as a spread such as 0.5 to 2 inches. A thick solid-wood top and a thin glass top can both fall outside it.

Third is weight. The desk's rated capacity should exceed the combined weight of the monitor, its stand or arm, and anything else on the top. For a dual arm, that means both panels, not one.

An arm has a useful side effect: replacing the stand lets the screen sit close to the rear edge, which buys back the stand's depth without buying a deeper desk. The catch is clearance behind the desk for the clamp and the arm's reach. The arm cannot manufacture viewing distance beyond the depth of the top itself.

Stability deserves a mention too. ANSI/BIFMA X5.5-2014 covers durability and stability for office desks, and a frame that meets it, with adjustable feet for uneven floors, is less likely to sway when you type.

## What "ergonomic" and "space-saving" leave unsaid

A desk marketed as ergonomic without a depth, height range, and weight capacity in the listing has told you nothing checkable. The same word appears on 19-inch-deep writing tables and on 30-inch-deep workstations. Sit-stand adjustability changes your seated and standing height; it does nothing for the distance between your eyes and the screen, so it is not a substitute for depth.

Space-saving is the more expensive euphemism. A shallow top can force the monitor closer than the OSHA range allows, and the symptom shows up as evening headaches rather than as an obvious problem in the showroom. If a listing omits depth, ask the seller for it in writing.

For a standards-based reference, ANSI/HFES 100-2007 sets out human factors guidance for computer workstations, including monitor placement. It is not a buying checklist, but it is a useful tiebreaker between two otherwise similar desks.

## Verify with a tape measure before you pay

You can test the whole calculation in ten minutes. Sit at your current desk, or at a table of similar height, and have someone measure from your eyes to the point where the screen surface would sit at your chosen viewing distance. If the tape reaches the back edge and the monitor has not arrived yet, the desk is too shallow for that distance.

Two related measurements are worth taking while you are there. OSHA recommends the top of the monitor at or slightly below eye level, with the screen directly in front of you, so check that your desk and chair let you reach that position without tilting your chin up. If you are considering an arm, measure the clamp area at the back edge and confirm nothing beneath it, such as a drawer or a crossbar, would block the clamp.

## Buying checklist

Work through these in order before you order a desk.

- Measure the clear depth from the front edge to the first obstruction behind the top.
- Set a target viewing distance inside OSHA's 20 to 40 inch range, guided by your monitor's manual and your own comfort.
- Measure your eye setback, then subtract it from the target distance and add the stand depth. Compare the result with the clear depth you measured.
- For two or more screens, add panel widths, bezels, and the room created by angling the outer screens.
- Confirm the desk's rated weight capacity exceeds the monitor, arm or stand, and accessories combined.
- If you plan to use an arm, confirm the VESA pattern, the clamp range against your desktop thickness, and clearance behind the desk.
- Look for a stability standard such as ANSI/BIFMA X5.5 and for adjustable feet.
- Ignore "ergonomic" and "space-saving" until the listing gives you depth, width, height range, and weight capacity.
