A laptop stand looks like a simple accessory. In practice it changes your whole setup, because raising the screen to a comfortable height puts the keyboard out of reach. That single consequence decides whether a stand works for you or becomes a piece of clutter you move aside every morning.

This guide covers what to measure, which types suit which desks, and the details that separate a stand you keep from one you stop using.

## Decide whether you are building a desk setup

If the laptop is your only screen and you type on its keyboard, a stand can only lift the machine a little. Raise it much further and you are typing with your hands in the air.

That leaves two honest options:

- **A low stand**, which lifts the machine slightly for airflow and a marginally better screen angle, while you keep typing on it.
- **A full setup**, where the laptop is raised close to eye level and you add an external keyboard and mouse.

The second is the one people actually want, and it is worth being clear that it is a three part purchase rather than one. If you are not willing to add a keyboard, choose a low stand and accept the modest improvement.

## Height is set by your eyes, not by the stand

The target is simple: the top of the screen should sit near your eye level when you are sitting up straight, so you look slightly downward at the middle of the display.

Measure the height from the desk surface to your eye level while seated. That distance, minus the height of the laptop itself, is roughly how much lift you need. Most laptops are two to three centimetres thick at the hinge, and a screen of thirteen to sixteen inches needs a stand that raises the base by somewhere between ten and twenty centimetres to reach eye level.

If the stand you are considering does not state a height, or only offers one position, it is a gamble.

## Stability, and the typing test

A stand holds a machine that you press against. If you are typing on the laptop, any flex in the stand shows up as a bouncing screen, which is worse than a low screen angle.

What to check:

- **The footprint relative to the laptop.** A stand narrower than the machine lets it rock sideways.
- **The material and thickness.** Thin folded metal flexes under a heavier laptop.
- **Rubber at every contact point.** Without it, the laptop slides and the stand slides on the desk.
- **The hinge, if it is adjustable.** A hinge that loosens after a few months turns an adjustable stand into a wobbly one.

For a full setup with an external keyboard, stability matters less because you are not pressing on the laptop. For a low stand, it matters a great deal.

## Cooling, and where the vents actually are

Raising a laptop helps in two ways. It exposes more of the underside to moving air, and it lifts the machine off soft surfaces that would otherwise block the intake.

How much it helps depends on the design:

- Vents on the **bottom** benefit most from a stand that holds the base clear of the desk.
- Vents at the **hinge or the sides** benefit less, because they were never blocked.
- A stand with its own fans adds airflow, but also noise and another cable. For most office work, passive lift is enough.

Be sceptical of any claim about specific temperature reductions. The honest statement is that a stand can help airflow, and how much depends on the machine.

## Types, and which desk they suit

**Fixed risers** hold one angle. They are the most stable and the least flexible. Good if you have already found the right height.

**Adjustable stands** with a hinge or a ratchet let you change the angle and height. More useful while you are still tuning the setup, and more likely to loosen over time.

**Desk arms** hold the laptop above the desk on a moving arm. They free the desk surface entirely and pair well with an external monitor, at the cost of a clamp and more setup.

**Cooling pads with fans** are the bulkiest option. They make sense for a machine that genuinely throttles under load, and rarely for writing and spreadsheets.

If the laptop is paired with an external monitor, a stand that positions the laptop screen beside the monitor at a similar height is usually the most comfortable arrangement, because your eyes do not have to refocus between two very different distances.

## A short checklist

- Decide first: low stand with the built-in keyboard, or full setup with an external keyboard.
- Measure your seated eye level, then subtract the laptop thickness to get the lift you need.
- Choose a stand that states its height, and preferably offers more than one.
- Check the footprint against the laptop, and look for rubber at every contact point.
- Prefer passive lift unless the machine genuinely throttles.
- Match the laptop screen height to your external monitor so your eyes do not jump between distances.
- Re-check the angle after a week, because the right height is usually not the first one you pick.

A laptop stand is cheap and small, but it only earns its place as part of a complete setup. Decide the keyboard question first, and everything else follows.
