Most people buy a monitor by choosing a size and a budget, then discover a month later that the stand
does not rise high enough or that text looks soft. A monitor tends to stay on your desk for years, so
it is worth deciding what you need before you open a single product page.

## Start with the work you do

A person reading code, a person editing photos, and a person running spreadsheets need different
things. High text clarity matters most for reading and writing. Wide colour coverage matters for
photo and video work. Vertical space matters for spreadsheets and long documents. Write down your two
or three most common tasks and let them drive the shortlist.

## Resolution and screen size travel together

The number of pixels matters less than how tightly they are packed. A large screen at a low
resolution looks grainy, and a small screen at a very high resolution makes text tiny unless the
operating system scales it. Pick a size and resolution combination that keeps text comfortable
without scaling, because scaling adds blur in some applications and always spends performance.

Pixel density is the figure to calculate. Divide the diagonal in pixels by the diagonal in inches. A
27 inch 2560 x 1440 screen works out to about 109 pixels per inch, and a 27 inch 3840 x 2160 screen
to about 163.

Scaling decides whether that density reaches your eyes. Windows at 150 percent on a 27 inch 4K screen
gives you the workspace of a 1440p screen with 2.25 times the pixels, so text is drawn more finely at
the same apparent size. On macOS, note that Apple removed subpixel antialiasing in version 10.14, so
a non-Retina external screen renders text with grayscale antialiasing and looks softer than it did
before. A 4K screen running a scaled mode keeps its double-density rendering.

## Panel type decides the character of the image

The three common panel families each trade something away. One offers deep blacks and wide viewing
angles but can dim unevenly and may be slower to respond. Another is fast and bright but has weaker
contrast. The cheapest type is fine for office work and poor for colour-critical work. None is simply
best, so match the panel to the task rather than to the price.

Putting names to those trades:

- **TN.** The cheapest and fastest pixels, and the ones whose contrast and colour shift as you move
  your head.
- **VA.** Usually around 3000:1 static contrast against roughly 1000:1 for most IPS panels. Dark
  transitions are slower, which shows up as smearing in dark scenes.
- **IPS.** The office default. Consistent colour and viewing angles at around 1000:1 contrast, with
  corner bleed sometimes visible on a dark screen.
- **OLED.** Effectively unlimited contrast and clean motion. Two documented risks for a document
  desk: static interface elements can leave persistent marks, and text often shows colour fringing
  because the subpixel layout is not what the operating system expects.

For reading and writing all day, an IPS panel with a good record on text is the low-risk choice; VA
rewards a dim room where films also matter. Two IPS screens can still differ widely on brightness,
uniformity, and factory calibration.

## The stand matters more than the specifications

A monitor you cannot position comfortably will hurt your neck no matter how good the picture is. Look
for height adjustment, tilt, and ideally rotation. If the stand is fixed, check whether it uses a
standard mounting pattern so you can add an arm later. Many attractive monitors ship with a stand
that barely moves.

Three measurements decide whether that stand works. The height range decides whether the top of the
screen can reach eye level; bundled stands often move 100 to 150 mm, and some only tilt. The mounting
pattern, 75 x 75 or 100 x 100 mm under VESA's FDMI standard, decides whether an arm can replace the
stand later. The base footprint decides where the screen ends up: on a 60 cm desk a deep tripod base
spends close to 20 cm of the depth you need for viewing distance, and OSHA's desk guidance asks for
at least 50 cm between your eyes and the screen.

## Ports, cables, and charging

Count the devices you need to connect and add one spare. A single cable that carries video, data, and
laptop charging is a genuine convenience, so check the power it can deliver and whether your laptop
supports it. Cables in the box are often short, so budget for a longer one.

Three details decide whether one cable does the job. USB-C video runs over DisplayPort Alternate
Mode, and the same cable can carry power, so the wattage is the number to compare: USB Power Delivery
3.0 tops out at 100 watts, and revision 3.1 extends the ceiling to 240 watts. A monitor that delivers
65 or 90 watts keeps a thin laptop charged, while a large laptop whose own adapter is 140 watts may
slowly drain under load. A charging cable can lack the high-speed pairs needed for video, and power
above 60 watts requires an e-marked cable. On HDMI, the version label is the weakest number on the
page: the 2.1 specification made most of its features optional, so a port with that label can carry
the bandwidth of the older 2.0 standard.

## What to ignore

Very high refresh rates are pleasant for games but rarely change office work. Extremely high
brightness matters only in a sunlit room. Curved screens help mainly at very large sizes. Do not pay
for a specification that will not change your day.

## A simple decision order

Size first, then resolution, then panel type, then the stand, then ports. If two options are equal on
those points, choose the cheaper one and put the savings into an adjustable arm or a better chair.
That order keeps the money where you will feel it every day.

Buying a monitor is really an exercise in knowing your own habits. Get that right and almost any
reasonable model will serve you for years. Get it wrong and the most expensive screen in the shop
will still be the wrong one.

## A short checklist

- Work out the pixel density before reading anything else.
- Prefer a native contrast figure over a dynamic one.
- Check the stand's height range, or the VESA pattern if you plan to add an arm.
- Find the weight without the stand if an arm is likely.
- Compare the port's power delivery wattage with your laptop's own adapter.
- Check the maximum resolution and refresh rate stated for each port.

## Sources and further reading

- OSHA, Computer Workstations eTool: Monitors. The source for the placement criteria: the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level, the centre 15 to 20 degrees below horizontal, a viewing distance of 50 to 100 centimetres, and no more than 35 degrees of side offset.
- OSHA, Computer Workstations eTool: Desks. The source for the 50 centimetre minimum viewing distance and the clearance under the work surface.
- USB Implementers Forum, USB Power Delivery specification. The source for the 100 watt ceiling under Power Delivery 3.0 and the 240 watt extended range added in revision 3.1.
- VESA, Flat Display Mounting Interface standard, for the 75 x 75 and 100 x 100 millimetre mounting patterns.
- Apple's macOS 10.14 release, which removed subpixel antialiasing for text.
- The monitor's own specification sheet, for native contrast, panel type, weight without the stand, and the VESA pattern.
