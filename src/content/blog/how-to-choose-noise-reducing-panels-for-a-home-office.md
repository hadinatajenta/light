## Decide which problem you are actually solving

Two different problems get sold the same product.

The first is a room that sounds harsh. Your voice bounces off bare walls, keyboard clicks ring, and people on the other end of a video call hear a hollow, boxy version of you. The second is noise crossing a boundary: traffic, a neighbour's television, a conversation in the next room.

Acoustic panels address the first problem. They absorb sound energy inside the room, which reduces reverberation and improves speech clarity. They do not address the second. Blocking transmission takes mass, decoupling and sealing, and no panel alone can soundproof a room.

There is a simple test to tell the two apart. If the sound you dislike is generated inside the room, whether that is your own voice, your keyboard or your speakers, absorption will change what you hear. If the sound originates elsewhere and reaches you through a wall, floor, door or window, a panel on your office wall will barely register. Getting this wrong is the most expensive mistake in the category, because you end up buying the right-looking product for the wrong job.

## Read the test numbers, not the packaging

Two rating systems cover most of the market, and neither one is a magic number.

ASTM C423 is the US test method. Panels are measured in a reverberation room and the results are averaged into single figures. The Noise Reduction Coefficient (NRC) averages absorption at 250, 500, 1000 and 2000 Hz on a scale from 0 to 1. The Sound Absorption Average (SAA) averages twelve one-third octave bands from 200 to 2500 Hz and is sometimes published instead. In Europe, ISO 354 covers the measurement and ISO 11654 converts it into a class from A to E based on the weighted absorption coefficient, with Class A the most absorbent.

Three practical points follow from this.

The single number hides the spectrum. A panel with a high NRC can still reflect low-frequency sound poorly, because the average starts at 250 Hz. That matters if what bothers you is a low male voice or a bass line from next door.

NRC is not a percentage. A value of 1.0 does not mean the panel absorbs 100 percent of sound at every frequency. It is an average across four bands, so a panel can be close to perfect at 2000 Hz and weak at 125 Hz. Current versions of ASTM C423 also report unrounded values, which means a figure slightly above 1.0 can appear because of diffraction at the edges of the test specimen. If you see one, read it as "as much as this test can measure", not as a material that absorbs more than everything. A single number at or above 1.0 tells you the material is highly absorbent in the mid and high bands; it tells you nothing about the low end.

Ask for the report, not the claim. A credible manufacturer can supply third-party absorption test results from an accredited lab (NVLAP and A2LA are the common US accreditations) with the ASTM or ISO method named. If a listing shows a number with no method, no lab and no certificate, you have a marketing figure.

## Thickness and density decide what actually gets absorbed

Now ask for the coefficient table. Absorption at lower frequencies generally requires thicker panels and greater airflow resistivity, which is how strongly the material resists air moving through it. Density is not the same property as airflow resistivity, although for a given material the two often move together. A thin panel and a thick panel can both post a high NRC, because the average is dominated by the mid and high bands, while the thicker one absorbs considerably more at the bottom of the range. Heavier is not automatically better across the whole spectrum either, since thickness and airflow resistivity interact.

The document worth requesting shows coefficients across frequency, ideally from 125 Hz to 4 kHz. If a manufacturer publishes one number and nothing else, you have no way of knowing whether the panel suits the specific sound in your room.

## Mounting is part of the specification

ASTM E795 defines mounting configurations for absorption testing, and it exists because mounting changes the result. A panel fixed flat against a wall and the same panel held off the wall on clips behave differently, particularly at low frequencies, where the air gap behind the panel changes how much energy is absorbed.

A coefficient is therefore only valid for the mounting method used in the test. Manufacturer data should state it, for example a Type A mounting under ASTM E795. If the report is silent, ask the supplier directly. Install the panel a different way and you are guessing.

If you plan to mount with an air gap rather than flush, confirm the supplier's test data covers that arrangement before assuming it performs better. It often does at low frequencies, but the honest answer is that it depends on the panel.

## Materials, fire ratings and long-term behaviour

Fire performance is where cheap panels become genuinely risky rather than merely disappointing. In the US, look for an ASTM E84 or UL 723 Class A rating, meaning flame spread of 25 or less and smoke developed of 450 or less. Untreated polyurethane foam generally does not meet those thresholds, and the rating is not something you should assume from the material name: some foam products carry it, others do not, and the facing and formulation both affect the result. Ask for the certificate for the specific product you are buying. A home office is a room where you sit for hours, and a wall panel often ends up near power strips and monitor cables. In Europe, the classification framework is EN 13501-1, which draws on tests such as ISO 1182 for non-combustibility.

Materials age differently. Fibreglass and mineral wool are fire resistant but need encapsulation to prevent fibre release, so check that the facing is intact on every edge, not just the front face. Polyester fibre is common in consumer panels. Foam is light and easy to hang, but it can degrade over time and its fire performance varies by product. Ask the manufacturer what documentation exists on fire classification, longevity, sag resistance and cleanability, because a panel that sags off the wall or traps dust in a textured facing is one you will end up replacing.

## Installation, walls, and what you cannot undo

Attachment is where a reasonable acoustic decision meets an unreasonable wall. The common methods are adhesive and impaling clips. Adhesives can lift paint or wallpaper when removed, and they do not hold equally well on every substrate. Removable picture-hanging strips are appealing in a rental, but they may not support the weight of a dense mineral wool panel.

Test on a hidden patch first, or choose a mounting method you can reverse without repainting. If you rent, this should decide the purchase; it may push you toward a freestanding panel rather than anything fixed to the wall.

## How many panels, and where to put them

There is no universal panel count. The right amount depends on room volume, how much hard surface you have, and how much change you are trying to hear. Ignore claims that a fixed number of panels will reduce noise by a stated number of decibels. Without a stated frequency, distance and room condition, a dB figure is meaningless, and absorption does not reduce sound pressure from a source the way a barrier does.

What you can control is placement. Absorption only affects sound that reaches the material, so surfaces facing you, or sitting directly behind your microphone, are doing more reflecting than a wall already softened by a curtain, a rug or a loaded bookshelf. Start at roughly ear height, since that is where your voice and your call audio are travelling, and treat the side walls between you and your microphone as first-reflection points worth covering before you cover a wall you never speak toward. A 2.4 m by 2.4 m wall is 5.8 m² of hard surface; covering part of it will change the room, but which part matters as much as how much.

Finally, check whether you are buying an absorber or a diffuser. Absorbers reduce sound energy. Diffusers scatter it. For a home office where the complaint is noise and echo, absorbers are usually what you need.

## Buying checklist

- Confirm which problem you have. If noise arrives through a wall, door, floor or window, panels are not the fix and your money belongs elsewhere.
- Request the third-party absorption report: ASTM C423 or ISO 354, from an accredited lab such as NVLAP or A2LA.
- Ask for coefficients from 125 Hz to 4 kHz, not just an NRC, SAA or ISO 11654 class letter.
- Check the mounting method the figures were measured under (ASTM E795, for example Type A) and install the panel that way.
- Verify fire documentation for the specific product: ASTM E84 or UL 723 Class A in the US, or an EN 13501-1 class in Europe. Do not assume a material is rated because of what it is made of.
- Match material to the room. Fibreglass and mineral wool need intact encapsulation; foam is light but varies in fire performance and can degrade over time.
- Test your wall before sticking anything to it. Adhesives can lift paint, and removable strips may not hold a heavy panel.
- Discard any dB reduction claim that does not state frequency, distance and room conditions.
