An office chair is the one piece of home office equipment you cannot easily work around. A slow monitor is annoying and a poor keyboard is tiring, but a bad chair becomes a back and neck problem. The features that prevent that are not the ones the marketing leads with.

This guide is about where the money should go. For the measurements that decide whether a chair fits you at all, see the separate guide on chair sizing, because no amount of adjustability compensates for a seat that is the wrong height.

## Adjustability is the whole point

A chair that fits you beats a chair that is merely plush. Padding is the cheapest thing a manufacturer can add and the easiest thing to photograph, which is why budget chairs lead with it.

What you actually need to be able to change:

- **Seat height**, because your feet have to reach the floor and your knees have to sit at roughly a right angle.
- **Seat depth**, either by sliding the seat pan or by moving the backrest forward and back. OSHA treats depth adjustability as a requirement for chairs shared between users, and it matters just as much for a single user who is shorter or taller than the chair was designed for.
- **Backrest height**, so the lumbar curve meets the inward curve of your lower back rather than pressing on it.
- **Recline**, with tension that holds your weight. OSHA's guidance asks for at least 15 degrees of recline from vertical, with either a lock or adjustable tension.

A chair that cannot be set to your body will not be fixed by a better cushion. That is the single most useful rule in this article.

## The mechanism is what you are really buying

Under the seat is a metal mechanism, and it is the part that decides whether the chair still works in five years. It is also invisible in every product photo, which is why it is worth reading about before you buy.

Three things to look for:

- **Tilt tension that adjusts.** A fixed tension means the chair is either too stiff to recline or drops backward under you.
- **A lock, or several.** Being able to hold the backrest upright for focused work and recline for reading is worth more than it sounds.
- **A mechanism that is serviceable.** Bolts rather than rivets, and a design where the gas cylinder can be replaced, which is the part that fails first on almost every chair.

If the mechanism cannot be repaired, the chair has a fixed lifespan regardless of how good the upholstery looks.

## Seat depth and edge pressure

Seat depth matters more than seat width, and it is the measurement people get wrong most often.

OSHA's criterion is precise: the seat pan should support most of the thigh without contact between the back of your knee and the front edge of the seat. A seat that is too deep presses the front edge into the back of your knee, circulation suffers, and you slide forward to escape it, which costs you the backrest. A seat that is too shallow leaves your thighs carrying weight without support under most of their length.

The front edge should also be rounded, which OSHA describes as a waterfall edge. A square edge concentrates pressure on the same spot behind the knee, and it is one of the few chair features you can check from a photograph.

## Lumbar support done properly

A firm, height-adjustable lumbar pad is more useful than a soft pillow. It should encourage the natural inward curve of the lower back without pushing you forward, and it has to move up and down, because where it belongs depends on where your pelvis ends up once your feet are supported.

If your chair lacks one, a small rolled towel can test the effect before you spend money on an accessory. If the towel helps, an adjustable support will help more. If it does not, the problem is probably the seat height or depth rather than the backrest.

## The armrests often get in the way

Armrests are optional, and a badly adjusted pair is worse than none. The test is simple: your shoulders should stay relaxed and your elbows should stay close to your body while your forearms rest lightly on the pads.

Armrests that are too high lift your shoulders and create tension in the neck. Armrests that are too wide make you reach sideways to use them. Armrests that cannot go low enough can stop you pulling the chair close to the desk, which pushes you into reaching for the keyboard all day.

If yours cannot be adjusted properly, OSHA's own advice is blunt: remove them, or stop using them.

## The base and the casters

Two details that are easy to overlook and hard to fix later.

The chair should have a **five-leg base**. OSHA is explicit that chairs with four or fewer legs are prone to tipping. A five-leg base also spreads the load, which matters on a soft floor.

**Casters should match the floor.** Hard casters roll on carpet and damage hard flooring. Soft casters are the opposite. Many chairs ship with one type and sell the other separately, so if the chair will move between surfaces, check that the casters are replaceable.

## Materials, build, and repair

Mesh breathes and stays cool, while foam feels softer at first but compresses over time. Neither is inherently better; the question is how the chair is built around it.

Check the weight rating and the warranty, and read what the warranty actually covers. A warranty that covers the mechanism and the gas cylinder for years is worth far more than one that covers the frame for one year, because the frame is rarely what fails.

A chair that sells replacement parts, particularly casters, cylinders, and armrest pads, is a better long-term buy than one that does not.

## What is worth the premium, and what is not

Worth paying for: depth adjustment, a recline that locks, a mechanism with a long warranty, and a five-leg base.

Not worth paying for: racing stripes, a logo on the headrest, a massage function you will use twice, and a headrest on a chair you never recline in.

A used premium chair in good condition is often better value than a new budget one, provided three things check out: the gas cylinder holds height under load, the mechanism reclines and locks smoothly, and replacement parts are still sold for that model.

## Test it like you mean it

Sit for at least ten minutes, adjust everything, and notice whether your feet stay flat and your shoulders relax. If a shop will not let you, buy from somewhere with a real return policy.

Your body is the only specification that matters here, and it is worth more than any feature list.

## Sources and further reading

- OSHA, Computer Workstations eTool: Chairs. The source for the seat pan criterion, the 15 degree recline, the waterfall edge, the five-leg base, and the guidance on armrests.
- ANSI/BIFMA G1, Ergonomics Guideline for Furniture Used in Office Work Spaces. The dimensional standard office chairs are commonly designed against. The document is paid, so compare against a manufacturer's summary.
- The chair's own warranty document, which is usually a separate PDF from the product page. Read what is excluded before you decide.
