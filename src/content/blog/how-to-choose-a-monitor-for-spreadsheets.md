Spreadsheet work is not video work. A 240 Hz panel will not make a quarterly model easier to read, and a 4K screen at the wrong scaling can leave less usable cell area than the 1080p screen it replaced. Choosing a monitor for spreadsheets comes down to how much cell area you can read at once, and how comfortably. Pixel density, scaling, panel shape, and viewing distance decide the rest.

## The monitor for spreadsheets test: count rows, not hertz

Open your worst workbook and count the columns you need on screen at once, then the rows in the longest table you read. That pair is the specification you are shopping for.

Refresh rate is close to irrelevant: high refresh rates smooth motion, and nothing on a worksheet moves. A 60 Hz office panel is fine. What matters is how small text can get before it stops being readable, and [this guide to resolution and scaling](/blog/monitor-resolution-and-scaling-for-text-work-a-practical-guide/) covers that trade.

## Resolution and scaling decide how many cells fit

Scaling divides the physical pixel count. A 27-inch 4K at 150 percent is not an 8.3-megapixel workspace; it is a 2560 x 1440 workspace drawn with 2.25 times the pixels.

| Setup | Physical pixels | Scale | Logical canvas | Default rows |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 24-inch 1080p | 1920 x 1080 | 100% | 1920 x 1080 | 54 |
| 27-inch 1440p | 2560 x 1440 | 100% | 2560 x 1440 | 72 |
| 27-inch 4K | 3840 x 2160 | 150% | 2560 x 1440 | 72 |
| 27-inch 4K | 3840 x 2160 | 125% | 3072 x 1728 | 86 |
| 27-inch 4K | 3840 x 2160 | 100% | 3840 x 2160 | 108 |
| 34-inch 21:9 | 3440 x 1440 | 100% | 3440 x 1440 | 72 |

Row counts come from Excel's 15.00 point default row height. A point is 1/72 inch, so a default row is 20 pixels at 96 dpi, and a 1440-pixel canvas holds 72 before toolbars.

Columns resist that arithmetic because Excel measures width in characters of the default font. Plan in ratios: 40 columns at 100 percent become 32 at 125 percent and 26 at 150 percent.

## Worked example: 27-inch 4K at 150 percent versus 1440p at 100 percent

The 4K screen does not show more cells: 3840 divided by 1.5 is 2560, and 2160 divided by 1.5 is 1440, so at 150 percent it presents the same canvas as 1440p at 100 percent. Both hold 72 default rows.

What differs is the drawing. EIZO lists 163 pixels per inch for its 27-inch 3840 x 2160 EV2740X; the same formula gives 109 for a 27-inch 2560 x 1440 panel. A line of 11-point text is 14.7 logical pixels tall, and the 4K panel draws it with 22. Stems separate and 8-point table values stop smearing into grey.

So the 4K pays in clarity at 150 percent, and buys cells one step down. At 125 percent it holds 3072 x 1728, about 86 rows. At 100 percent it holds 108 rows but renders 11-point text about 2.3 mm tall, which most people cannot use at arm's length.

## Aspect ratio: 16:9, 16:10, and 21:9 in cells

16:9 is the default because it is the cheapest shape to build. 16:10 adds height at the same width, about 11 percent more rows for identical horizontal room, which matters when you scroll a ledger to keep its header in view.

21:9 stretches the other axis. A 3440 x 1440 panel is 34 percent wider than 1440p with the same 72 rows; on a 34-inch model the glass is about 800 mm across at 110 ppi. You buy columns, not sharpness. A 32:9 panel doubles that width again, at 5120 x 1440.

| Shape | Resolutions | Default rows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 16:9 | 1920 x 1080, 2560 x 1440, 3840 x 2160 | 54, 72, 108 |
| 16:10 | 1920 x 1200, 2560 x 1600 | 60, 80 |
| 21:9 | 3440 x 1440 | 72 |
| 32:9 | 5120 x 1440 | 72 |

## Portrait orientation for tall sheets

Rotate a 27-inch 4K panel 90 degrees and it becomes 2160 pixels wide by 3840 tall. At 150 percent scaling that is a 1440 x 2560 logical canvas: 128 default rows against 72 in landscape.

For a long ledger or a payroll register, that is the largest jump in visible data you can get without adding a screen. The trade is width. 1440 logical pixels is narrower than a 1080p monitor, so a sheet wider than about 20 default columns will scroll sideways. Check that the stand pivots.

## Panel type, coating, and text clarity

Nearly every business monitor at 27 inches and above uses IPS, because 178 degree viewing angles keep far columns the same shade as near ones. A spreadsheet is mostly white, and white shows off-axis shift first.

- **IPS.** The office default. About 1000:1 typical contrast (ASUS quotes 1000:1), stable colour, some corner bleed.
- **VA.** Higher quoted contrast, which matters for film, not a white grid. Dark tones smear and gamma shifts off-centre.
- **TN.** Cheap and fast, but the vertical angle is weak: rotate one to portrait and the top rows wash out.
- **OLED.** Perfect blacks that a spreadsheet never uses. Static ribbons held in place for eight hours are the known risk.

Coating matters more than buyers expect. A matte, anti-glare surface scatters reflections; EIZO describes its coating as reducing reflections from ambient light. A glossy panel keeps contrast and looks clean in a dark room, but mirrors every window behind you, and a white sheet shows it. If the desk faces a window, matte is the low-risk choice.

## Viewing distance and the desk depth a bigger panel needs

ISO 9241-303 underpins most office display guidance, and the German workplace rule ASR A6 reproduces its character-height table. Required character height grows with distance.

| Viewing distance | Recommended character height |
| --- | --- |
| 500 mm | 3.2 to 4.5 mm |
| 600 mm | 3.9 to 5.5 mm |
| 700 mm | 4.5 to 6.4 mm |
| 800 mm | 5.2 to 7.3 mm |

Those heights span 22 to 31 minutes of arc at the eye. Two consequences follow.

First, the same sheet needs bigger type the further back you sit, and zoom is the cheapest way to get it at the cost of cells. Excel's default 11-point text renders at roughly 3.4 mm on a 109 ppi screen at 100 percent scaling: fine at half a metre, small at 70 cm.

Second, panel geometry caps the distance. ASR A6 gives a rule of thumb for seeing the whole screen without moving your head: diagonal in millimetres times 1.6. For a 27-inch panel that is about 1100 mm, more than most desks allow.

So the real question is how far your eyes can get from the glass. A 600 mm desk, monitor pushed back and chair pulled in, leaves 600 to 700 mm, where a 27-inch panel 597 mm wide fills about 53 degrees of view. [This comparison of 24 and 27 inches at 60 cm](/blog/24-vs-27-inch-monitor-at-60-cm-on-a-shallow-desk/) works that geometry on a shallow desk.

The purchase test: measure eye to panel, then add the depth the stand needs. Under 550 mm, 27 inches is already generous.

## One ultrawide or two monitors?

Two 27-inch 1440p screens give 5120 logical pixels of width and 72 rows, split by a bezel. That is the best value in spreadsheet work: a second panel costs less than a jump to 5K, and you get two windows, one for the model and one for its source. The cost is about 1.2 m of desk width.

A 34-inch 3440 x 1440 gives one cable and no gap, but only 34 percent more width than one 1440p screen. The 40-inch 5120 x 2160 class is the true replacement for two: 2160 tall, about 139 ppi.

| Question | Two 27-inch 1440p | One 34-inch 21:9 | One 40-inch 5120 x 2160 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Logical width | 5120 px | 3440 px | 5120 px |
| Default rows | 72 | 72 | 108 |
| Pixel density | 109 ppi | 110 ppi | 139 ppi |
| Cables | 2 video, 2 power | 1 video | 1 video |
| Desk width | about 1.2 m | about 0.8 m | about 0.95 m |

If you compare two workbooks, two screens win. If you want one very wide sheet and one cable, a 34-inch is enough.

## A short checklist before you buy

- Measure eye-to-screen distance first, then pick the diagonal that fits it.
- Decide the scaling percentage you will run, and count the cells it leaves.
- Choose IPS if the screen will sit off-axis or in portrait.
- Prefer matte unless the room is dark and controllable.

The non-spreadsheet parts, from stand to ports, are in [this home-office monitor guide](/blog/how-to-choose-a-monitor-for-a-home-office/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a 4K monitor worth it for spreadsheets?

Yes, if you run it at 125 or 150 percent. At 150 percent a 27-inch 4K shows the same 2560 x 1440 canvas as a 1440p screen but draws each character with 1.5 times the pixels per side.

### How many rows and columns should I be able to see?

Work backwards from your sheet. Excel's default row height is 15 points, or 20 pixels at 96 dpi, so a 1440-pixel canvas holds 72 rows before toolbars. Columns depend on your widths: 125 percent keeps 80 percent, 150 percent two-thirds.

### Should I use 100 or 150 percent on a 27-inch 4K?

150 percent for reading at 60 to 70 cm, 125 percent if your eyes are good and you want more cells. At 100 percent, 11-point text renders about 2.3 mm tall.

### Is an ultrawide better than two monitors for spreadsheets?

Only if you want one wide sheet. A 34-inch 3440 x 1440 is 34 percent wider than one 1440p screen, while two double the width.

### Is 24 inches too small for spreadsheets?

At 1080p it gives 54 default rows and about 92 ppi, workable at 50 to 60 cm and cramped for wide models. A 24-inch 1920 x 1200 adds 6 rows. Move to 27 inches only if your desk lets you sit back.
