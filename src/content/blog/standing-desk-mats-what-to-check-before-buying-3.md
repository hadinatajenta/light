## Quick answer

- Ask for compression deflection — the share of the mat's thickness that displaces under load — at both 20 psi and 40 psi. The stated optimum is 20 to 60 percent at both pressures.
- Below 20 percent the mat feels hard; above 60 percent it feels too soft. Thickness alone tells you nothing.
- If you buy PVC (vinyl), the top surface must be molded to the foam, not glued. Glued layers curl and delaminate.
- If the floor gets wet, or sees oil, coolant or aggressive cleaners, specify nitrile rubber rather than PVC foam.
- No mat is "OSHA approved." OSHA has no anti-fatigue mat standard.

## Why mat shopping goes wrong

Most standing mat listings sell you a thickness in inches and a vague promise about comfort. Neither answers the question you actually have: whether the mat will still be under your feet in two years, and whether it suits the floor it sits on.

Standing mats are a small purchase with a long daily exposure, which makes them unusually prone to bad spec sheets. The useful numbers come from the maker's own test data, and most retailers do not publish them. That is your first filter: if a brand cannot tell you the compression deflection at 20 psi and 40 psi, or which rubber compound the mat uses, it is selling foam, not engineering.

There is also a genuine limit to what a mat can do. One manufacturer's review of the science, citing the NIOSH review, concedes that objective physiological measures such as leg-volume and circulation data came back mixed and inconclusive, while self-reported reductions in discomfort and fatigue were consistent across studies. That is worth knowing: a mat may well feel better without the blood-pooling mechanism being proven. It is a reason to buy on comfort and materials, not on physiological claims.

## What should a standing mat's compression deflection be?

Compression deflection is the percentage of the mat's thickness that displaces under load. M+A Matting tests it at two pressures — the loads it equates to a 150 lb and a 600 lb user — and states that the optimum deflection at both is 20 to 60 percent.

| Test load | Pressure | Stated optimum deflection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 150 lb user | 20 psi | 20 to 60% |
| 600 lb user | 40 psi | 20 to 60% |

Two loads matter more than one number. A mat that deflects nicely for a lightweight user can bottom out under a heavier one, and a mat firm enough for a heavy user can feel like bare floor to someone light. Testing at both is the point.

Thickness is a proxy at best. A thick mat with dense foam can deflect less than a thin mat with a softer core, and thickness is the easiest spec for a seller to quote. Ask for deflection figures in writing, at both pressures.

## PVC, rubber or nitrile: which material should you pick?

The three materials fail in different ways, and the construction method drives most of it.

| Material | Construction | Main weakness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PVC (vinyl) foam | Extruded, open cell | Porous; degrades with oil; 6 to 12 month maker claim |
| PVC/nitrile blend | Extruded, open cell | Same open-cell limits |
| SBR rubber | Varies by product | Compound often unnamed |
| Nitrile rubber | Molded, closed cell | Higher cost |

Open-cell foam is porous and permeable, so liquids such as oil and water can seep into the material. That is a durability problem and, in some settings, a hygiene problem the spec sheet tends not to mention.

AcroMat puts its own service-life figures on that gap: PVC foam breaking down in 6 to 12 months, against 3 to 8 years for its 100% nitrile in harsh industrial use around the clock. Treat those as the seller's own claims, not neutral test data, but the order of magnitude is the useful part. If you stand eight hours a day and a PVC mat is priced to be replaced annually, that is the cost of ownership you are agreeing to.

If you buy PVC anyway, M+A Matting's recommendation is specific: choose a mat whose surface is molded, not glued, to the foam base, because that reduces cracking, curling and delamination. A glued skin is the failure point you will see first, usually as a lifted corner you keep catching with a heel.

On rubber, the spec sheet should say which compound. M+A Matting splits rubber into two primary types — styrene-butadiene (SBR, the general-purpose synthetic rubber used in tyres and matting) and nitrile — and describes nitrile as offering advantages over both PVC and polyurethane. A listing that says only "rubber" is telling you nothing.

For floors exposed to oils, coolants or aggressive cleaners, standard PVC foam degrades while nitrile resists, so specify nitrile or a cutting-fluid-resistant grade, and ask for chemical-compatibility data rather than accepting the phrase "oil resistant." That phrase has no defined meaning on a mat listing.

## Does the mat have to match your floor and room?

Yes, and this is separate from comfort. CCOHS warns that anti-slip mats and anti-fatigue mats are not the same product, and that buyers should choose a mat matching their needs. If your floor gets wet, you may be solving two problems, and you need to know which one the mat is for.

Wet conditions are the sharpest example. M+A Matting states that vinyl is far less slip-resistant than rubber and that when wet, many vinyl mats do not meet minimum slip-resistance requirements. Anywhere near a water cooler, a kitchen or an entry, that changes the material decision outright.

If you are specifying a mat for a commercial space and a slip rating is quoted, know what it measures. The DIN 51130 test (a German standard for measuring slip resistance on an inclined ramp) places the flooring on a rack with 200 ml of defined motor oil applied per square metre, and a test person walks over it in specified work shoes while the surface is slowly lifted until slip occurs. An "R" rating therefore describes dynamic slip resistance under oiled, inclined conditions. It says nothing about comfort.

For a specific number to request, one manufacturer publishes lab results against ASTM F2913-24 (a standard test method for measuring coefficient of friction on footwear and flooring), using a Neolite heel under 500N vertical force, and compares the results to a 0.42 safety minimum under ANSI/NFSI B101.1. A mat with a wet coefficient of friction near that floor is a different product from one with a high dry figure. Ask for both the dry and wet values.

Cleanability is a selection criterion in its own right. CCOHS notes that mats may need cleaning, and that in areas such as food preparation, being able to clean the mat adequately matters. Closed-cell nitrile is the easier surface to wipe down; open-cell foam holds what spills into it.

## Which certifications are worth anything?

Read every certification by its scope, because most of them cover a narrower claim than the marketing implies.

High-traction certification is the clearest case. NFSI High-Traction certifies slip resistance only. A mat carrying it is not thereby certified for fatigue relief or ergonomic performance, and the two claims have to be checked separately.

Electrostatic discharge matting is the other one to handle carefully. Botron's documentation for its ESD anti-fatigue matting (ESD, electrostatic discharge: the uncontrolled static that damages sensitive electronics) cites ANSI/ESD S4.1, ANSI/ESD S7.1 and ESD TR53 as standards the product meets or exceeds. Ask for the certificate against those standards for the exact product you are buying, not the product family. ESD ratings specify resistance ranges — conductive or dissipative — under ANSI/ESD S20.20, and an ESD mat controls nothing unless it is grounded. The mat is one component of a path to earth, not a standalone fix.

Then there is the phrase to ignore entirely. OSHA has no anti-fatigue mat standard and no prolonged-standing standard. There is no such thing as an "OSHA-approved," "OSHA-certified" or "OSHA-required" anti-fatigue mat. A seller using that language is misrepresenting the regulatory position, which tells you how carefully the rest of the listing was written.

Percentage comfort claims fall in the same bucket. Claims such as reducing tiredness by a stated percentage are brand marketing, and the sensible response is to ask what testing sits behind the number. Do not build a decision on it.

## Mat or better footwear and a stool?

A mat is one input among several, and it is not always the highest-leverage one. If your desk height is wrong for your body, no mat will compensate. Sort the desk first, since the mat sits downstream of everything else in the workspace; pair this with [Planning a desk upgrade without replacing everything](/blog/planning-a-desk-upgrade-without-replacing-everything/) if you are fixing several things at once.

Two trade-offs matter here. A thick, soft mat can be a hazard if you roll a chair between standing and sitting; a very soft mat under casters makes the chair lurch. And a bevelled edge — the tapered border that lets you step on without tripping — matters more on a hard floor than on carpet, where the pile hides the lip.

The Ergodriven Not Flat is the one mat in our product list, and it is a specific answer rather than a default: a shaped, terrain-style mat designed to encourage small foot and stance changes rather than uniform cushioning. That suits someone who shifts position constantly and dislikes the dead feel of a flat slab. It is a poor fit for anyone who wants a plain uniform surface, works in shoes with a hard sole, or needs a mat that rolls up flat for storage.

If you are deciding between a mat and adjustable footwear or a sit-stand stool, the mat wins on cost and simplicity; the stool wins if your floor is already comfortable and your issue is posture rather than surface. Neither substitutes for a correctly sized desk or chair, and if the chair is the unresolved variable, [Sizing an office chair: what the standards actually require](/blog/chair-seat-depth-and-height-office-chair-sizing/) covers the measurement sequence.

## What to check before you add it to the cart

- Compression deflection at both 20 psi and 40 psi, in writing, ideally landing in the stated 20 to 60 percent band. No figure, no purchase.
- Material named precisely: molded nitrile, SBR, or PVC foam. "Rubber" and "foam" alone are not answers.
- For PVC, confirmation that the surface is molded to the base, not glued.
- Dry and wet coefficient of friction if the floor may get wet, with the test method named. Vinyl is the material to avoid when wet.
- For ESD use, the certificate against ANSI/ESD S4.1, S7.1 or ESD TR53 for the exact product, plus the grounding path.
- Cleanability confirmed against your environment if food, oils or chemicals are present.
- Edge profile and mat thickness checked against your chair's casters, so rolling on and off stays stable.
- Chemical-compatibility data for oil, coolant or aggressive cleaners, not the phrase "oil resistant."
- No reliance on "OSHA approved" or a percentage comfort claim. Neither describes a tested product property.
