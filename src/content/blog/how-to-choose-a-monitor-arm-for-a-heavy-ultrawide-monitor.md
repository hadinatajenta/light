## Start with the panel, not the arm

Arm listings lead with screen size because it sells, but diagonal inches carry no load. Two numbers decide whether a mount can hold a heavy ultrawide: the net weight of the panel and the hole pattern on its back. This guide is for the desk arm that has to hold a large or ultrawide panel, where the gap between working and sagging is small enough to be worth measuring.

Weight gets muddled in a predictable way. Listings often quote shipping weight, which includes the carton, foam, cables and the manufacturer's own stand, none of which hangs off the arm. As the KTC Play guide to VESA compatibility and weight ratings puts it, arm ratings refer to the net weight of the display panel itself. If your manual only gives a boxed figure, weigh the panel: hold it on a bathroom scale, note the number, weigh yourself alone, and subtract.

Then leave headroom. Installers quoted in the same guide suggest a 20 to 25 percent margin between net weight and the arm's maximum rating, to absorb the extra force of pushing the screen back or swinging it sideways, plus the slow loss of gas-spring pressure over years of use. A 9 kg panel on a 10 kg arm can fit on paper and still droop in practice. The same panel on a 12 kg arm has margin to spare.

## VESA patterns: confirm the fit before you shop

VESA numbers are shorthand for the Flat Display Mounting Interface standard, which fixes hole spacing and screw sizes so displays and mounts can be compared at all. The pattern most people picture, 100 x 100 mm, is not universal.

The table shows the pattern each class of display commonly uses, per KTC Play's compatibility guide.

| Display class | Typical pattern (mm) |
| --- | --- |
| 27 to 32 inch | 100 x 100 |
| Larger or ultrawide | often 200 x 100 or 200 x 200 |

Plenty of single-arm heads ship with only the 100 x 100 plate, so a 200 x 100 panel can be a dead end no matter what it weighs. Measure hole centre to hole centre in millimetres, horizontally and vertically. Edge-to-edge measurements add the hole diameter, and inches invite conversion mistakes on spacing measured in millimetres.

Two details on the back of the panel matter as much as the pattern. Many gaming displays have a recessed or circular mounting area, and a flat plate may need 10 to 20 mm standoff spacers to seat against it; the wrong spacers either foul the housing or leave the screws too short for the holes. If your display has no standard holes at all, edge-clamping adapter kits exist, but they are the least stable option for a heavy screen you adjust often. Treat an adapter as a compromise for a light, seldom-moved panel, not a solution for a heavy ultrawide.

## Curved panels sag for reasons the weight rating misses

Curvature adds a problem that has nothing to do with kilograms. Because the panel bows forward, its mass sits farther from the mounting plate, moving the centre of gravity away from the arm and adding torque at the tilt joint. KTC Play's guide describes the symptom directly: curved and ultrawide monitors can drift downward on their own, and the effect grows with the depth of the curve. A 1000R panel moves its centre of gravity farther forward than a shallower 1800R or a flat screen of the same weight, because more of the panel sits ahead of the plate.

That makes the tilt joint as important as the lift capacity. An arm rated for the weight but fitted with a light friction hinge will hold the screen at first and creep later. Spec sheets rarely quantify that, so read two signals instead: whether the tilt mechanism is described as reinforced for curved panels, and what tilt range is quoted. A range of -30 to +45 degrees is asymmetric, offering more travel above horizontal than below. If your working position needs only a few degrees of downward tilt, you are using a small part of the mechanism's travel, which is where it holds best.

Panel technology is a weak predictor here. Two screens of the same diagonal, one OLED and one Mini-LED, will not arrive at the same net weight, and the type tells you nothing about the torque your arm will see. Weigh the panel and apply the margin.

## Reading a heavy-duty arm's spec sheet

VIVO's STAND-V101G1 appears here as an example of how one manufacturer documents a heavy-display arm, not as a recommendation; its panel weight still has to be checked on the product page. The table collects what its documentation states.

| Specification | Documented value |
| --- | --- |
| Screen size | up to 32 in flat, up to 49 in ultrawide |
| Screen types | flat and curved, including 1000R |
| Height adjustment | 10.5 to 23.5 in to screen centre |
| Tilt (deg) | -30 to +45 |
| Swivel (deg) | 180 |
| Rotation (deg) | 180 |
| Construction | aluminium, flexible joints |

Note what that sheet does not say: it publishes no weight range. Screen size ceilings are fit statements, not load ratings, so the weight figure has to come from elsewhere on the product page. Do not read a size ceiling as evidence that a mount can carry your panel. On this documentation the arm is a fit for a flat or curved panel inside those size ceilings, mounted on a desk inside the thickness ranges below; the load question stays open until you find the weight figure.

Where a gas spring does the lifting, check whether preload is adjustable and how it is set. A heavy panel hangs on the spring's factory tension, and because spring pressure falls off over years of use, a tension screw or dial is the practical way to bring the arm back to level. A sealed spring with no adjustment leaves the panel's height dependent on the setting it shipped with.

Mounting method is the other hard constraint, and the two options do not accept the same desk. The table shows the desktop thickness each method supports on that arm.

| Mount | Desktop thickness (in) |
| --- | --- |
| C-clamp | 0.4 to 3.3 |
| Grommet | 0.4 to 1.9 |

A butcher block or sit-stand top above 1.9 inches rules out the grommet route, and that is worth knowing before you drill. Thin particleboard introduces the opposite risk: a clamp concentrates load on a small area and can crush the surface, so a steel reinforcing plate under the desk is cheap insurance. Aluminium in the arm structure is the material signal to look for on a heavy mount; a moulded plastic joint or a plastic-and-steel hinge is where creep and cracking start.

## Height, distance and the desk you are clamping to

Read a height figure in context. That VIVO number, 10.5 to 23.5 inches, is roughly 27 to 60 cm measured to the middle of the display, not to the top of the panel and not to the joint. Your own body sets the target. The table gives the two dimensions OSHA's computer workstation guidance pins down.

| Target | OSHA guidance |
| --- | --- |
| Screen top | at or slightly below eye level |
| Viewing distance | 20 to 40 in |

Work those numbers in the order the guidance gives them. The rule is written to the top of the screen, so a target for the screen centre sits half a panel lower. If your seated eyes are 44 cm above the desk, the top of the screen wants to be near 44 cm and the centre lands about 17 cm below that, roughly 27 cm above the desk on a 27-inch 16:9 panel. That is at the very bottom of the arm's range, so a taller panel or a lower desk can push the target below what the arm reaches. This is why the desk and the arm are chosen together rather than one after the other.

Reach deserves the same treatment. An arm's forward travel has to bring the panel to your viewing distance, and in a small room the binding constraint is often the wall behind the desk or a window sill rather than the arm itself. Measure from the front edge of the desk to the wall, subtract the depth of the panel and the folded arm, and check whether the screen can sit where your eyes want it. If you clip a light bar over the top of an ultrawide, add its height when you check clearance to a shelf above.

## Before you buy: a checklist

- Find the panel's net weight, not the shipping weight and not the weight with its stand.
- Add 20 to 25 percent, then compare the result against the arm's rated capacity.
- Measure the VESA pattern hole centre to hole centre in millimetres, and confirm the head includes it (100 x 100, 200 x 100 or 200 x 200).
- Note the curvature. A 1000R panel needs a reinforced tilt joint, not just a high weight number.
- Measure desktop thickness and choose clamp or grommet by the published ranges.
- Subtract your desk height from your seated eye height, then check that the resulting screen-centre height falls inside the arm's quoted range.
- Confirm the structural material, and whether gas-spring preload is adjustable on the model you are considering.
- Check whether cables can be routed along the arm, since a large display usually brings a heavy cable bundle.
- For a recessed back, plan on 10 to 20 mm standoff spacers and check the screw length supplied with the kit.
