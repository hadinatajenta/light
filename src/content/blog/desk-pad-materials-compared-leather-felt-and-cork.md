## Quick answer

Match the material to how you use the desk. Wool felt gives the best control surface, but it cannot be written on and it absorbs spills. Leather and coated synthetics sit at the speed end; only the better hide grades age well, and even good leather marks under a wet cup. Cork is firm, dual-sided and thin. Whatever the material, size the pad in millimetres, keyboard width plus mouse sweep, and check the base and the stated thickness before you order.

The guide is organised around the three ways a pad disappoints: it soaks up a spill, it wears through where your forearm rests, or it is the wrong size for the mouse sweep you make. Manufacturer statements and my own reading of them are labelled separately, and none of the numbers below come from lab testing.

## Which desk pad material fits how you use the desk?

The table sets out what the cited sources state about each material, with no ratings added.

| Material | Documented specification | Documented limitation | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Merino wool felt | 3 mm felted mat; works with an optical mouse | Not good for writing on | Grovemade |
| Standard wool felt | Fibre about 40 microns | Coarser fibre than merino | Minimal Desk Setups |
| Leather | Laminated to a cork base | Cup rings can mark it | Grovemade |
| PU (polyurethane) |  | Cracks and peels with daily use | Wyoming Jack |
| Bonded leather | Shredded hide with a binder | Not leather; behaves like vinyl | By Design |
| Cork | 2 mm, 800 x 400 mm, dual-sided |  | Info Cork |

Felt absorbs. Wyoming Jack's comparison lists absorbency as felt's weakness, and Minimal Desk Setups notes that compacting felt lowers the chance of immediate water absorption, which buys the seconds to wipe a spill away. Standing liquid still soaks in. PU fails differently: it cracks and peels under daily use, and no cited source gives a construction specification for it, which is why that cell is blank rather than filled with a guess. No cited source states how PU behaves with a spilled drink either.

Glide is the other axis. HYTE's pad guide splits surfaces into control, meaning a textured finish that makes mouse movement easier to stop, and speed, where friction is low and the same hand movement sends the pointer further. Felt sits at the control end; leather and coated synthetics sit nearer speed. Coming from a cloth pad, leather will feel quick and slightly less exact, and that is a preference rather than a defect.

The cork listing gives construction and dimensions but states no limitation. The trade-off I would flag is thickness: the cork pad is the thinner of the two in the table, so it offers less cushion than the felt mat, and that is my reading of the two specifications rather than a manufacturer claim.

## How does merino felt differ from standard wool felt?

Felt is wool matted into a solid sheet with heat, moisture and pressure rather than woven, which is why it has no grain and no fraying edge. The table compares the two wool grades on the figures Minimal Desk Setups gives. A micron is a thousandth of a millimetre.

| Wool grade | Fibre diameter (microns) | Practical effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard wool | About 40 | Coarser, wears more slowly |
| Merino | 20 to 25 | Softer, more water-resistant, wears faster |

Minimal Desk Setups attaches the trade-off to those figures: merino's finer fibres make the pad softer, better insulating and more water-resistant, and the same fineness means sleeves and mouse movement rub the surface away faster. You are trading comfort against service life, and the wear shows first where your forearm and mouse hand rest. Grovemade describes felting merino with pressure and steam into a mat, so stated thickness is a fair thing to ask a seller for; a listing that gives no thickness could be anything.

## Is the leather on a desk pad actually leather?

Sometimes, and the listing will not tell you which kind. PU (polyurethane, a synthetic polymer used as a faux-leather coating) cracks and peels with daily use. Bonded leather (shredded hide mixed with a binder and stuck to a backing) is a composition, not a hide; the By Design explainer states plainly that it is not leather and behaves more like vinyl. A cheap pad calling itself leather is usually one of those two.

"Genuine leather" is real hide at the lowest grade, according to Business Insider, which describes full-grain leather, the whole outer surface of the hide, as the highest grade. That ranking decides whether the pad still looks deliberate in three years or looks worn out.

Even good leather has a running cost. Grovemade laminates a leather surface to a cork bottom for structure, and notes that water rings from cups can leave a mark. Coasters are part of the purchase.

## How wide does a desk pad need to be for a keyboard and mouse?

Measure your keyboard's width, add the horizontal distance your mouse travels without leaving the pad, and that total is your minimum. A 360 mm keyboard and a 250 mm sweep need at least 610 mm before any margin. A narrower pad forces you to lift and reposition the mouse mid-task, and no material upgrade fixes that. The table lists the footprints the cited sources give.

| Pad format | Documented footprint | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small pad | 240 to 300 mm x 200 to 250 mm | HYTE |
| Cork desk pad | 800 x 400 mm | Info Cork |

HYTE also warns that size names are not standardised between brands, so one seller's large can match another's medium. Read the millimetres. The rest of the decision belongs to the desk: a full-width pad on a shallow top can overhang the front edge, and a monitor arm baseplate usually needs to sit clear of the pad. If the pad and the desk are both in question, settle the desk first, as set out in Planning a desk upgrade without replacing everything. In a very small room, Choosing a desk for a laptop-only setup in a small space covers what a full-width pad cannot fix.

## What keeps a desk pad from sliding, curling or marking the desk?

Friction is the whole mechanism, and the base is the part listings describe least clearly. The table sets out what the cited sources say about each base.

| Base | Documented detail | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rubber foam under cloth | Cloth wrapped over foam | HYTE |
| Natural rubber | 1.5 to 6 mm roll, soft, A or B hardness | creatprinting |
| Cork | Dual-sided, darker cork back | Info Cork |

My reading of the hardness grades is compression rather than grip: a softer base flattens more under a resting wrist. Cork takes another route, with grip coming from the same material as the surface instead of a rubber layer that can separate. Minimal Desk Setups added rubber micro-feet to its own pad and reports the result as no-slip; treat any no-slip claim as conditional on your desk finish and test it inside the return window.

A pad that creeps sideways is often being pulled rather than slipping: cables crossing the back edge tug at it whenever you move the keyboard or dock. An under-desk cable management tray from Univivi takes that run off the desktop, and the planning is covered in [Cable management for a small desk](/blog/cable-management-for-a-small-desk/). Curling is separate. A rolled pad straightens faster if it has weight and a laminated backing, and a plain felt sheet has neither, which is my reading rather than a documented specification.

## What should you check before ordering?

- Keyboard width plus mouse sweep in millimetres, before you look at size names.
- Fibre diameter rather than the word wool: ask the seller for microns.
- The base: natural rubber, cork, or nothing at all.
- Whether you write on the pad. If you do, felt is out.
- Whether you drink at the desk. If you want leather, budget for coasters.
- Leather grade. Full-grain over genuine; PU or bonded only if you accept cracking and peeling as the end state.
- Stated thickness, so you know how much cushion sits under your wrists.
