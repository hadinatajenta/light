## Quick answer

The table sets out the target figures for a monitor on an arm, from screen angle to the weight and depth the arm has to carry.

| What to set | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Screen centre below eye level | 15–20° |
| Top-edge cross-check | eye line 5–10 cm below top edge |
| Viewing distance | 20–30 in (about 50–76 cm) |
| Backward tilt | 10–20° |
| Fore-aft travel, shared desk | about 12 in |
| Sit-stand rise | 4–6 in |

Monitor height stops being a matter of taste once the display goes on an arm. A factory stand gives you one or two fixed positions; an arm gives you a range, and that range either covers your numbers or it does not. Three figures decide the purchase: the angle from your eyes to the centre of the screen, the distance from your eyes to the glass, and the weight and depth of the panel the arm has to carry at the end of its reach.

## How high should a monitor be on an arm?

The figure to set is a downward angle from horizontal eye level (a line straight out from your eyes, parallel to the floor) to the centre of the screen of 15 to 20 degrees. Workstation manufacturer Xybix Systems publishes that range in its compliance guidance and adds that monitors placed above horizontal eye level should be avoided. It is a centre-of-screen angle, not a top-edge rule, and reproducing it is the whole job of an arm's height range.

Sit as you normally work, let your shoulders drop, and measure from the floor to the corner of your eye. Then work out the drop. The table shows how far below eye level the centre of the screen needs to sit at three common viewing distances; values are rounded to the nearest centimetre.

| Distance (cm) | Drop at 15° (cm) | Drop at 20° (cm) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 50 | 13 | 18 |
| 65 | 17 | 24 |
| 76 | 20 | 28 |

In the middle of the range — eyes about 118 cm above the floor and a viewing distance near 65 cm — the screen centre belongs somewhere between about 94 and 101 cm above the floor. Whether the head can hold that position with the desk at your working height is the first thing to check on a spec sheet.

A second rule circulates and does not agree with the first. ErgoLink's guidance puts your eye line on a point 5 to 10 cm below the top edge of the panel. Apply that to a 27-inch 16:9 screen, which is about 34 cm tall, and your eye line lands roughly 7 to 12 cm above the screen centre. At arm's length that is a shallower angle than 15 to 20 degrees. Set the height by the angle and use the top-edge rule as a check that you are not looking up into the upper third of the panel. Bifocal and progressive lens wearers should drop the screen another inch or two so the head does not tip back to find the reading zone.

## How far should the monitor be from your eyes?

Twenty to 30 inches, or about arm's length, so the whole screen stays visible without leaning in or squinting, according to ErgoLink. You can test it in your own chair: sit back and extend one arm, fingertips just touching the screen.

Distance is set by desk depth and arm reach together. A clamp arm mounted at the rear edge of a shallow desk leaves you short of the full range once your torso is over the keyboard, so a shallow desk and a large panel combine badly. UpDown Desk notes that monitors 24 inches and larger need to sit further back to keep the whole screen inside your natural field of vision, and that higher-resolution panels let you sit further out without losing clarity.

Xybix Systems calls for monitors to move fore and aft by approximately 12 inches to meet individual visual requirements. Check both ends of that range. Many desk-clamp arms extend generously but retract poorly, and the retracted figure is the one that matters when the arm is pushed back against a wall. On a shared desk, the 12 inches is not optional.

## What tilt angle should a monitor arm hold?

Ten to 20 degrees of backward tilt supports a natural viewing posture, per UpDown Desk's guidance, and tilt also decides where reflections land. A screen angled back catches ceiling lights, and catches a window if one is behind you. Keeping the angle of view as close to perpendicular to the screen as possible limits distortion and reflection. If glare is the real complaint, lighting and panel settings usually deliver more than another degree of tilt.

Two checks on the arm itself. First, does the published tilt range include 10 to 20 degrees backward. Second, does the mount hold position under the panel's weight: a mount pushed past its capacity will not hold position and the display will droop or drift downward, which undoes a correct setup.

## Will your arm hold the monitor you already own?

Screen size is a guideline, not a compatibility check. Look up two numbers from the monitor's own documentation: weight without the stand, and depth without the stand. Listings often give dimensions as width by height by depth with and without the stand, and only the without-stand figures describe what the arm actually carries.

Depth matters because of torque (the rotational force a load applies to a pivot). A deeper display places more rotational force on the mount's pivot points, so two monitors of equal weight can behave differently on the same arm. Curved panels extend further from the mounting point and shift their centre of gravity forward, which is why they often need a higher-capacity arm than a flat panel of the same weight. Look for a curved-display rating specifically rather than assuming the weight figure covers it.

Capacity is a ceiling, not a target. Atdec states that exceeding it means the arm will not hold position and the display will droop or drift downward, and that the monitor can fall in extreme cases. Count anything mounted alongside the panel. If you are unsure which plate and screw pattern your display uses, check the display's documented VESA pattern (VESA is the standard screw-hole pattern on the back of a display) and screw length before ordering.

## How do you handle two screens or a sit-stand switch?

Two screens should sit at equal focal lengths (the eye-to-screen distance for each panel) so your eyes do not re-focus every time they cross the gap. The placement rule that follows: primary screen directly in front at about arm's length, second screen beside it and slightly to the side, so you are not twisting to see the whole panel. A single crossbar holding both screens at one height will not satisfy the equal-focal-length rule unless each head adjusts independently, which is the feature to look for in a dual mount.

For sit-stand use, expect the screen to rise roughly 4 to 6 inches when you stand. That figure is offered as a fast calibration target rather than a rule, because both your eye height and the desktop height change when the desk moves. Set the body-to-desk relationship first. Monitor height cannot rescue a bad keyboard height: if the keyboard is too high the shoulders lift, and if the desk is too low the wrists and upper back compensate.

## Buying checklist

- Measure floor-to-eye height sitting and standing before you shop.
- Convert your preferred distance into a target band: eye height minus distance times tan 15 degrees, down to eye height minus distance times tan 20 degrees.
- Confirm the arm reaches the low end of that band with the desk at working height, and the high end when standing.
- Match weight without stand and depth without stand to the arm's rating, and check the curved-panel rating if you own one.
- Confirm the tilt range covers 10 to 20 degrees backward, and that the mount holds position under the panel's weight.
- Check extension against your desk depth plus your seated distance, and check the retracted length too.
- Check the clamp or grommet against desktop thickness and material.
- Confirm the VESA pattern and screw lengths before ordering.
- Ask for evidence rather than adjectives. A vendor page claiming to be the only maker meeting every requirement of the ANSI/HFES 100-2007 workstation guidelines, with no audit attached, is marketing. The standard is published by HFES, an independent professional association, and a test report is the thing to request.
