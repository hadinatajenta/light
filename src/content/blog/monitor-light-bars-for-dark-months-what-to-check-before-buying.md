## Quick answer

- Aim for roughly 300 to 500 lux on the desk and 3500K to 4500K neutral white. Check those, not the lumen headline.
- Beam control matters more than brightness. Asymmetric optics (a lens or reflector shaped to throw light down and forward, away from the panel) are the feature. A dimmer is not a substitute.
- Measure your monitor's top bezel, rear housing and camber before ordering. Curved and thick-backed monitors are where clamps fail.
- If you read paper or judge colour, a shaded desk lamp usually serves you better than a bar.

A light bar is a strip of LEDs clamped to the top of a monitor that throws a band of light onto the desk below it. In the dark months, when the window stops doing the work, that band is often what separates comfortable screen work from squinting at four in the afternoon. It is also a category where the marketing is loud and the useful specifications are thin. What follows is what to measure, what to ask for in writing, and when a bar is the wrong tool.

## How bright should a light bar be, and where does that number come from?

KTC's guide to ultrawide workspace lighting puts the practical target at roughly 300 to 500 lux for typical computer work, with neutral white around 3500K to 4500K and a higher colour rendering index for colour-critical tasks. Lux (a measure of light falling on a surface) is the number that matters, because lumens measure total output in every direction, not what arrives at your keyboard. Colour temperature, or CCT (correlated colour temperature, the kelvin figure that describes how warm or cool the white looks), and CRI (colour rendering index, a 0 to 100 score for how faithfully colours appear under the light) are the other two.

The table below shows the numbers worth holding a light bar against, either as a target for your own desk or as a figure that should appear in test data.

| Metric | Unit | Target or check |
|---|---|---|
| Desk illuminance | lux | 300 to 500 |
| Correlated colour temperature | K | 3500 to 4500 |
| Colour rendering index | Ra | higher for colour work |
| Test geometry | 2π or 4π | 2π for a down-firing bar |

Most listings publish lumens and nothing else. A photometric report (laboratory data on what a fixture actually emits) is the document that fills the gap. aspectLED explains that for white fixtures it should list CCT, lumens, power consumption, efficacy, CRI Ra and TM-30 Rf, and it should state the sphere geometry used. An integrating sphere is a hollow coated sphere that collects all the emitted light for measurement; in 2π geometry the fixture sits at the back so only the hemisphere it actually emits into is measured, which is the correct arrangement for a bar that fires downward. If a brand cannot say which geometry was used, the output figure is not comparable to anything.

## Screen bar vs desk lamp: which one fixes your problem?

The table compares how each option puts light on the desk, because that difference decides the purchase more often than brightness does.

| Option | Where light lands | Screen glare risk | Desk space used |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bar clamped to monitor | Narrow band below the panel | Low with asymmetric optics | None |
| Shaded desk lamp | Aimable pool, wider reach | Moderate, depends on shade angle | Base or clamp |
| Clip-on lamp on shelf | Downward, wider spread | Low if aimed away from panel | None |

The bar's advantage is geometry, not power. It sits above the desk and the beam falls where your hands are. Its limitation is the same geometry: light lands in a band roughly the width of the monitor and fades quickly to the sides, so a document to the left of the keyboard can sit in shadow. A shaded lamp reaches wider and can be aimed at paper, at the cost of surface area and one more thing to knock over.

The question to answer with a tape measure is how wide the area you need lit actually is. If everything you read sits within the monitor's width, a bar covers it. If you spread documents across the desk, budget for a lamp instead.

## What causes monitor light bar glare, and how do you avoid it?

KTC states that a good light bar uses asymmetrical optics, meaning the beam is shaped to illuminate the desk surface while avoiding the screen. When the beam hits the panel instead, you have moved the glare source closer to your eyes, and dimming only darkens the desk without removing the reflection. Glossy screens show that reflection as a hard band; matte screens scatter it into a wash that lowers contrast, which people then compensate for by raising monitor brightness. If that trade sounds familiar, [monitor brightness and contrast for office lighting](/blog/monitor-brightness-and-contrast-for-office-lighting/) covers the screen side of it.

Two checks before buying: does the head rotate forward independently of the clamp, and does the listing actually say the optics are asymmetric? A fixed head on a monitor with a tilted back cannot be corrected after purchase.

## Will it fit your monitor?

KTC advises choosing a bar whose clamp accommodates curved backs and lets the beam be rotated forward, because curved ultrawides, thick rear housings, webcams and aggressive top bezels can make some light bars unstable or misaligned.

Measure three things with a ruler: the thickness of the top bezel at the centre, the depth of the rear housing below the top edge, and the camber of a curved panel. A clamp sized for a thin flat bezel will not sit square on a curved back, and the head will tilt with it. A webcam mounted on the same top edge competes for the same few centimetres and often pushes the bar off centre, so look for a clamp with a gap or offset in the middle.

Height matters too. A bar raises the visual top edge of the monitor, and if the screen is already too high you will tilt your head back to see it. [Monitor height on an arm: eye line, tilt, and viewing distance](/blog/monitor-height-on-an-arm-eye-line-tilt-and-viewing-distance/) covers where that top edge should sit before you add anything to it.

## Which claims on the box are worth checking?

The table pairs each common label claim with the document that would actually back it up.

| Claim | Document to ask for |
|---|---|
| Flicker-free | ISO 9241-307 test report |
| Low blue light | 2PfG 2383 report |
| Photobiological safety | IEC 62471 risk group |
| Rated life | LM-80 data plus TM-21 projection |
| Driver safety | UL 8750 listing |

Flicker is the first one to probe. MMD, which markets Philips displays, describes ISO 9241-307 as the test standard that ensures a flicker-free experience. On a light bar the equivalent question is whether dimming uses pulse width modulation (PWM, where the LED is switched on and off rapidly and the eye sees the average) or reduced current. PWM is rarely printed on a light bar box, so ask, especially if you notice flicker on other LED products.

Blue light claims need a band, not a mood. TCL's support documentation says the certification requires significantly reducing blue light output in the 415 to 460 nm range, achieved either through hardware tuning or a software mode, so a manufacturer claiming it should be able to say which applies. Eyesafe states that to receive TÜV Rheinland certification, products must filter at least 20 percent of blue light at 435 to 440 nm and also meet criteria for correlated colour temperature and luminance. A bar that simply shifts its overall tint warmer has not been measured against that.

For eye safety, IEC 62471 specifies exposure limits, measurement techniques and a classification scheme for photobiological hazards from optical radiation sources including LEDs. Konica Minolta notes the risk classes run from 0 (exempt) to 3 (high risk), so a product can carry a rated group rather than a vague description. IEC 62471-7:2023 is the part covering luminaires that emit mainly in the visible range, 380 to 780 nm, which is where desk lighting sits.

Rated life is the claim most often disconnected from evidence. IES LM-80 is the approved method for measuring output and colour maintenance of LED packages, arrays and modules, not of the finished fixture, and it typically runs a minimum of 6,000 hours, with 10,000 preferred for accuracy. Under IES TM-21 a projected lifespan cannot exceed six times the actual test duration, so a 100,000-hour claim would need at least 16,667 hours of LM-80 data. LM-80 data is also collected at three case temperatures (55°C, 85°C and one the manufacturer chooses), and a report run only at 55°C may not represent how the LED runs inside a warm bar.

Finally the driver, which is the part that usually fails first. The Department of Energy finding cited by Hyperlite names driver failure as the leading cause of premature system failure, so a stated LED lifetime is close to meaningless if the driver dies sooner. UL 8750 is the safety standard for LED drivers, and a power factor (the ratio of usable power to total power drawn) above 0.9 indicates a driver that wastes little.

One regulatory note if you are buying for a workplace: Commission Regulation (EU) 2019/2020 sets ecodesign requirements for light sources and separate control gears, and identifies energy consumption in the use phase and mercury content as the significant environmental aspects, with mercury governed by the RoHS Directive rather than by ecodesign rules.

## Buying checklist

- Does the listing give illuminance at the desk, or only lumens? If only lumens, expect to measure the result yourself.
- Is the colour temperature fixed or adjustable within 3500K to 4500K, and is CRI Ra stated?
- Are the optics described as asymmetric, and does the head rotate forward?
- Does the clamp fit your actual monitor: bezel thickness, rear housing depth, curvature, webcam position?
- Can the manufacturer supply an ISO 9241-307 or equivalent flicker test?
- Can it supply a 2PfG 2383 report, and does it say which wavelength band was measured?
- Is there an IEC 62471 risk group, and is it exempt or low risk?
- For long-life claims, what was the LM-80 duration and at which case temperatures?
- Is the driver UL 8750 listed, with a power factor above 0.9?
- Where does it plug in? A bar adds one more load to a busy hub, and [surge protection for a desk: power planning and what to buy](/blog/surge-protection-for-a-desk-power-planning-and-what-to-buy/) covers how to lay that out.

## If the free fixes are not enough

The table below matches a specific constraint to the kind of product that resolves it.

| Situation | What answers it |
|---|---|
| Desk too shallow for a lamp base | Monitor light bar with asymmetric optics and a rotating head |
| Curved or thick-backed monitor that standard clamps will not grip | Bar with a clamp specified for curved housings, or a bar mounted on a shelf above the desk |
| Reading paper to the side of the keyboard, outside the bar's band | Shaded desk lamp on an adjustable arm, aimed across the page |
| Colour-critical work where the light itself is the reference | Lamp or bar with a published photometric report, high CRI Ra and a TM-30 Rf figure |
