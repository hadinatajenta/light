The China AI bubble burst is not a single moment. It is the name investors gave to a violent repricing of the companies that sold the picks and shovels of China's artificial-intelligence boom. Between its summer peak and late October 2026, the STAR 50 index of Shanghai-listed technology companies fell more than 40%, wiping out roughly $480 billion in shareholder value. The CSI 300, a broader benchmark of China's largest companies, entered a bear market. October was the worst month for Chinese stocks since 2016, according to Reuters.

That sounds like the end of a story. It is closer to the end of a valuation story, and the beginning of a harder one. The stock-market bubble has burst. The technology has not. The difference matters for anyone trying to understand what happens next in China, and for the global AI trade that was, until this week, still debating whether the same mechanics could show up in the United States. They did. On 5 November, the Nasdaq fell 5.5% and entered a correction; on 6 November, Asian technology stocks tumbled. The China selloff was the first act, not an isolated event.

## Quick answer

- China's STAR 50, the main index of Shanghai-listed AI and technology stocks, fell more than 40% from its summer 2026 peak, wiping out about $480 billion in market value.
- The CSI 300 entered a bear market, and October was the worst month for Chinese stocks since 2016.
- This was primarily a repricing of expectations, not evidence that Chinese companies stopped using AI. At the peak, the STAR 50 traded at nearly 250 times trailing earnings; after the fall it was still around 100 times, versus roughly 30 times for the Nasdaq 100.
- Several pressures met at once: tax bills, US restrictions on Chinese data-center equipment, aggressive price competition, doubts about monetising open-weight models, and a market that had become extremely concentrated in AI.
- Beijing can support liquidity, strategic firms, and the IPO pipeline. It cannot invent global demand, remove export controls, or guarantee that every AI listing deserves its valuation.
- China's AI industry continues to build models, chips, robots, and applications. A stock-market correction can coexist with real adoption.
- The US is not a clean read-across, but it is no longer a hypothetical. US mega-caps have earnings and cash flow that many Chinese AI listings do not, yet the Nasdaq fell 5.5% on 5 November and entered a correction as Democrats won Congress and AI-regulation fears hit chipmakers. The China episode was an early warning about concentration, capex, and financing risk.
- Watch STAR 50 earnings and forward valuations, the IPO window, Beijing's support measures, US export rules, and whether US hyperscaler capex starts producing visible returns.

## What is the China AI bubble?

The phrase "China AI bubble" describes a two-year rally in Chinese technology stocks that became increasingly concentrated in artificial intelligence. After China's 2024 stimulus, the STAR 50 climbed about 230% through its June 2026 closing high, according to The Daily Upside. The index tracks 50 major technology and innovation companies on the Shanghai Stock Exchange's STAR Market, with heavy exposure to semiconductors, AI infrastructure, robotics, and hardware suppliers.

The rally had a real foundation. China was building domestic chip capacity, funding AI labs, deploying models in consumer apps and factories, and turning robotics into a national industrial priority. The problem was the price. By the peak, investors were paying nearly 250 times the combined trailing earnings of the index's constituents, according to Reuters. That is not a valuation that leaves much room for a missed quarter, a delayed product, or a change in export rules.

The concentration made the market fragile. Goldman Sachs estimated that AI-related stocks accounted for about 42% of market capitalisation and 57% of trading activity across mainland-listed A shares and Hong Kong-listed H shares. When a market's direction is that dependent on one theme, a change in sentiment can move the whole index faster than fundamentals can respond.

That is why the correction looked so violent. It was not that every company in the index stopped growing. It was that the market had priced many of them for a version of the future in which growth never slowed, global demand never closed, and margins never compressed.

## What actually burst: the STAR 50 selloff

The numbers are stark, but they need context. The STAR 50's 40% drawdown was measured from its summer peak to late October 2026. The index did not fall in a straight line. It had sharp rebounds on policy signals and sharp reversals when those signals disappointed. By the time the first headlines declared the bubble burst, the market had already spent months repricing the most speculative names.

Here is the scorecard that matters:

| Metric | Late October 2026 |
| --- | --- |
| STAR 50 drawdown from summer peak | More than 40% |
| Market value lost | About $480 billion |
| STAR 50 trailing price-to-earnings at peak | Nearly 250× |
| STAR 50 trailing price-to-earnings after the fall | About 100× |
| Nasdaq 100 trailing price-to-earnings | About 30× |
| AI share of China market capitalisation | About 42% |
| AI share of China trading activity | About 57% |
| Nasdaq drawdown from its record | Roughly 6–8% |

The gap between 100× and 30× is the heart of the story. Even after a 40% decline, the STAR 50 was still more than three times as expensive as the Nasdaq 100 on trailing earnings. Investors had repriced the bubble, but they had not made Chinese AI stocks cheap. They had moved from "priced for perfection" to "priced for a very good outcome."

The damage was concentrated in the companies that had been treated as pure AI proxies. Reuters reported that suppliers of optical equipment to data centers, chipmakers, and other providers of AI infrastructure saw share prices fall 50%, 60%, or 70% over a few months. These were the "nuts and bolts" of the AI revolution: the companies that benefit when data centers are built, even if they do not own the model or the application.

Some individual stories show how far expectations had travelled:

| Company or segment | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| Unitree, robotics | The humanoid-robot maker jumped 460% above its IPO price on its first trading day and was about 8,000 times oversubscribed by retail investors, according to Reuters. By the end of October, the stock was about 23% below its first-day level even though third-quarter revenue rose 70% year over year. |
| CXMT, memory chips | The memory maker fell about 25% in October despite a strong earnings report, a sign that good results were not enough to justify the preceding valuation. |
| Moore Threads and MetaX, GPUs | Both GPU makers had soared roughly 400% and 700% on their debuts in late 2025, then each lost about half their value from July to late October. |
| AI infrastructure suppliers | Optical equipment, chip, and data-center hardware names fell 50–70% from their highs as the market questioned global demand and export risk. |

Unitree is the cleanest example of the gap between a hot IPO and a durable public valuation. Revenue was growing. The robot business was real. But an 8,000-times oversubscribed retail offering is a sentiment event, not an earnings model. When the sentiment turned, the stock had to find a new anchor.

## Why the China AI bubble burst

No single headline caused the selloff. A market that had been priced for perfection met a series of imperfect facts.

### 1. Valuations ran ahead of earnings

The first problem was arithmetic. A 250× trailing multiple requires extraordinary growth for years. When the market began to ask when that growth would show up in free cash flow, the multiple had to come down. The 40% decline was the mechanism. The 100× multiple that remained was the residual optimism.

This is not unique to China. It is the same question that hangs over AI stocks everywhere: how much of the spending on chips, data centers, and power turns into revenue, and how much turns into depreciation and debt service? In China, the question arrived while many of the listed companies were still smaller and less profitable than the US hyperscalers.

### 2. The earnings test arrived

The third quarter of 2026 was the first real test. Unitree's revenue rose 70%, but its stock was still down from its debut. CXMT reported strong results, but its shares fell about a quarter in October. The market was no longer rewarding growth alone. It was asking whether the growth came with margins, cash flow, and a path to competitive advantage.

That is a healthy change in one sense. It is painful in another. When a stock trades at a speculative multiple, a good quarter can still produce a bad return.

### 3. Tax bills and liquidity

Reuters reported that tax bills falling due in China were one trigger for the selling. That is a reminder that market corrections are often made of small, mechanical pressures, not one grand narrative. Higher margin requirements, a drain in liquidity, and profit-taking after a two-year rally can turn a valuation problem into a stampede.

October's result was the worst month for Chinese stocks since 2016. That is not a normal pullback. It is what happens when a crowded trade unwinds.

### 4. Geopolitics and export risk

The AI trade is also a trade in access. Chinese suppliers had been counting on global data-center demand, including demand from the United States. As Washington signalled restrictions on Chinese equipment in American data centers, investors had to discount the possibility that some global revenue would disappear. The US also sanctioned five Chinese companies connected to the founder of Shanghai Zhisuan Technology, according to the Associated Press, adding another layer of geopolitical risk.

The result is a valuation problem with a policy tail. A company can grow its domestic business and still be worth less if its addressable market shrinks.

### 5. Competition and monetisation

China's AI market is crowded. Companies are competing on model quality, cloud capacity, robotics, and price. Aggressive pricing can squeeze margins even while demand grows. Open-weight models, which anyone can download and adapt, are good for adoption but difficult to monetise directly. Investors began to ask where the profit would come from, not just where the users would come from.

Gavekal Dragonomics' Christopher Beddor put it plainly to CNA: some Chinese AI companies are struggling to monetise open-weight models. That is a business-model problem, not a technology problem.

### 6. The chip constraint

The deepest constraint is not financing. It is access to advanced chips. US export controls have limited Chinese firms' access to some of the most sophisticated AI processors, and domestic alternatives are still catching up on capacity and performance. Beddor told CNA that "the real constraint on China's AI ambitions is not financing but access to chips."

That matters for the stock market because it caps how quickly Chinese AI companies can convert capital into compute. It also explains why Beijing's support cannot simply buy a solution. Money can build fabs and fund research; it cannot instantly replicate a supply chain that took decades to build elsewhere.

## The companies caught in the repricing

The selloff did not hit every Chinese technology company equally. It hit the companies that had been valued as pure expressions of the AI theme.

The first group was the hardware suppliers: optical modules, memory, GPUs, and data-center equipment. These businesses have real customers and real revenue, but their valuations had been driven by the assumption that AI infrastructure spending would grow indefinitely. When the market began to question the pace of global data-center buildout, the stocks de-rated quickly.

The second group was the newly listed story stocks. Unitree is the best example. Its robots are impressive, its revenue is growing, and its IPO was a retail sensation. But the first-day pop and the 8,000-times oversubscription created a price that required years of flawless execution. The stock's decline is not evidence that robots are a fad. It is evidence that the IPO price was a bet on sentiment.

The third group was the large platforms and chip champions that investors treated as national strategic assets. These companies have more support and more durable businesses, but they are not immune to a market-wide de-rating. When the index falls 40%, almost everything correlated to it falls.

The lesson is not that China's AI companies are bad. The lesson is that a good company can be a bad stock at the wrong price. The correction is separating those two things.

## What Beijing can and cannot fix

Beijing has tools that most governments do not. It can direct state-backed funds, encourage bank lending, support corporate bond issuance, and signal that strategic industries will not be allowed to fail. It can also adjust margin requirements and slow or accelerate the IPO pipeline.

Those tools can stabilise liquidity and confidence. They can protect strategically important firms. They can keep a bad month from becoming a systemic crisis. They cannot make every AI company profitable, and they cannot force global customers to buy Chinese equipment.

Stephen Wong of the CAIA Association made the distinction well in CNA's analysis: "Government support can facilitate infrastructure, research and capacity building, but it cannot by itself ensure that every company develops a sustainable business model or that every security represents good value."

That is the line investors should hold onto. Beijing can support the industry. It is not underwriting every shareholder's entry price.

The policy response has also been incremental rather than decisive. Reports of a market-support meeting and easier liquidity helped Chinese stocks rebound on some days. The next selloff often arrived within days. That pattern is typical of a market trying to find a floor: policy support reduces the downside tail, but it does not resolve the earnings question.

## What did not burst: China's AI industry

The stock market is not the industry. A 40% index drawdown can coexist with more AI adoption, more chip capacity, and more capable models. In fact, a lower share price can help the industry by forcing discipline on capital allocation.

China still has several advantages. It has a large domestic market, a manufacturing base that can turn AI into physical products, a growing robotics sector, and a government that treats AI as a strategic priority. It has a generation of open-weight models that are cheap to deploy and easy to adapt. It has companies that are learning to build on top of those models rather than only selling the underlying compute.

The application layer is where the next phase of value creation sits. AI agents, design tools, coding assistants, and workflow automation can produce value without owning a data center. The [FigCraft MCP guide](/blog/figcraft-mcp-cli-ai-agents-guide) is one example of how quickly the tooling layer is maturing; the [local AI guide](/blog/can-i-run-ai-locally-guide) explains why falling inference costs and smaller models change the unit economics for companies that do not want to rent the largest cluster in the room.

None of that makes China's AI industry immune to overcapacity or price wars. It does mean that the bear case for Chinese AI stocks is not the same as a bear case for Chinese AI. The market can correct while the technology keeps diffusing.

The chip constraint remains the real bottleneck. If China cannot secure enough advanced compute, the most ambitious training runs will be slower and more expensive. But inference, robotics, industrial vision, and applications can run on less advanced hardware. The industry can grow even while the frontier is constrained.

## Is the US AI trade next?

Until the first week of November, the honest answer was "not yet." Then the US selloff arrived. On 5 November, the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.5%, its deepest one-day drop in 19 months, and confirmed a correction more than 10% below its 6 October record. The S&P 500 fell 4.36%, wiping about $3 trillion from S&P 500 companies. The Nasdaq 100 fell 6.4%, erasing roughly $2.5 trillion in market value. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index dropped 12%; Nvidia fell 9%, AMD fell 17%, and data-center names such as CoreWeave, Nebius, Applied Digital, Cipher Digital, and IREN fell 10–15%. On 6 November, the KOSPI plunged more than 8%, triggering a circuit breaker, and the Nikkei fell 5%.

The trigger was political. Democrats won control of Congress in the US midterms, and investors began to price in tougher AI regulation, especially around data-center construction. Local opposition to data centers, power constraints, and water use had already been building. UBS analysts estimated that about 30% of expected US data-center capacity additions in 2028 could be at risk from construction pauses. The market had spent months debating the return on AI capex; the election gave it a new reason to sell the companies that benefit from the buildout.

The US is still not China. Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta have established businesses generating substantial revenue and cash flow. They can fund AI capex from operating profits, not only from speculative equity issuance. The US drawdown is younger and less severe than China's 40% index collapse. It does not have to repeat it.

But the same mechanics are now visible. US AI stocks are concentrated, expensive, and dependent on a capex cycle that has to produce returns eventually. The difference is the starting point: the US is correcting from a record high, with stronger earnings and a deeper capital market, while China was correcting from a retail-driven mania in smaller, more speculative listings. That makes a 40% US crash less likely, not impossible.

Gavekal's Beddor was right that there is no mechanical read-across: "China's stock market marches to the beat of its own drum, which is quite different from US markets and sometimes even the underlying Chinese economy." But the correlation in sentiment is real. OANDA's Kelvin Wong described China's selloff as an early warning of valuation and financing risks, rather than a sign that global AI demand was faltering. The US selloff is the second test of that warning.

The pressure points are the same: hyperscaler capex is rising faster than cloud revenue, data-center financing is moving into private credit and debt markets, power constraints are real, and the market is paying a premium for AI exposure. The political layer is new. "The market's moving from the promise of AI to the price of AI," Ted Parkhill of Incline Investment Management told Reuters. "There's no question that AI is going to be transformative for the world in the long run. But that doesn't mean every AI-related stock is currently priced appropriately."

That is the useful base case now: not "the US is next," but "the US is repricing the same assumptions, from a stronger starting point and with a political trigger." The [Gemini 4 Argon comparison](/blog/gemini-4-argon-google-frontier-model-reasoning-coding-cybersecurity) shows how quickly the model layer can change, which is both a growth driver and a valuation risk. The [Harness AI DevOps guide](/blog/harness-ai-devops-agents-guide) shows the enterprise side of the same question: adoption is real, but the return on investment arrives on a different schedule from the capex.

A correction can still hurt. It compresses multiples, punishes companies that cannot show returns, and forces a shakeout among smaller AI vendors. It does not require a 40% index crash to change the narrative. The US may get a sharper correction than the "slow deflation" case, because the political trigger arrived before the earnings evidence did.

## What to watch next

The next few quarters will decide whether this was a valuation reset or the start of a longer bear market. The signals to watch are specific.

| Signal | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| STAR 50 forward earnings and valuation | A trailing multiple of 100× can fall quickly if earnings grow. Forward earnings and cash flow tell you whether the index is still expensive. |
| The IPO window | A healthy IPO market needs buyers. If listings stay frozen, smaller AI companies lose a key funding route. |
| Beijing's support measures | Look for targeted liquidity, state-backed funds, and margin rules. Broad bailouts are less likely than support for strategic firms. |
| US export rules | Restrictions on Chinese data-center equipment and advanced chips change the addressable market for Chinese suppliers. |
| China's chip capacity | Domestic AI accelerators, memory, and advanced packaging determine how much compute the industry can actually deploy. |
| US AI regulation and data-center permitting | Democratic midterm wins raised the risk of tighter rules and local opposition. Watch permits, grid connections, and water use. |
| US hyperscaler capex and cloud revenue | The spending still needs to turn into revenue, not just capacity. The selloff raises the bar for every quarter. |
| Credit spreads and data-center financing | AI infrastructure is increasingly funded with debt. CoreWeave's credit default swaps widened to a record during the selloff; wider spreads raise the cost of the buildout. |
| Market breadth | A selloff concentrated in AI is a repricing. A selloff that spreads to banks, consumers, and industrials is a different problem. |

The most important number is not the 40% drawdown. It is the earnings growth that the remaining 100× multiple requires. If Chinese AI companies can grow into their valuations, the correction will look like a painful but healthy reset. If they cannot, the market will keep repricing until the price matches the cash flow. The same test now applies to the US: the Nasdaq's correction is a warning that the market will not wait forever for AI capex to become AI earnings.
