A standing desk is one of the few home office purchases that changes how you work rather than just how the desk looks. It also fails more often than most furniture, because the two things that matter most in a small room, stability and depth, are the two things marketing photographs never show.

This guide covers what to measure before you buy, which specifications actually change daily use, and which features are worth paying for.

## Start with your own numbers, not the catalogue

The single most common mistake is buying a desk that cannot reach the right height for your body. A desk that stops two centimetres short of your standing elbow height is not a standing desk. It is a desk you stand at uncomfortably.

Measure two things with your shoes on:

- Your **seated elbow height**, sitting in the chair you will actually use, with your feet flat on the floor.
- Your **standing elbow height**, standing relaxed, arms hanging, then bent to ninety degrees.

Your desk surface should sit roughly at those heights, because your forearms rest on it while you type. The desk has to cover the gap between them, plus a margin at each end. If your seated height is 66 cm and your standing height is 104 cm, a desk with a 71 to 120 cm range gives you room. A desk with a 75 to 115 cm range also works, but a desk that starts at 80 cm will leave you reaching upward when seated.

Write those two numbers down before you look at anything else. They eliminate most of the market immediately.

## Depth matters more than width in a small room

A small room pushes you toward a narrow desk, and narrow is usually fine. Shallow is not.

For a single monitor, you want enough depth to place the screen roughly an arm's length away and still have room for a keyboard and your forearms. Once you add a laptop beside the monitor, or a second screen, the requirement grows quickly. Depth is also what makes a standing desk usable, because standing pushes the keyboard further from your body than sitting does.

If the room is genuinely tight, take the depth you need and reduce the width instead. Two monitors fit comfortably on a narrow but deep desk. They do not fit on a wide but shallow one, no matter how it looks in the photograph.

## Stability is the feature you cannot add later

A standing desk moves. The question is whether it moves only when you tell it to.

Two numbers tell you most of what you need. The **lift capacity** should comfortably exceed the weight you will put on it, including monitors, arms, and anything you keep on the desk permanently. The **frame construction**, particularly the number of leg stages and the thickness of the column, decides how much the desk rocks when it is raised.

You cannot fix a wobbly desk. You can add a monitor arm, a cable tray, or a drawer later. Stability is decided at purchase.

In practice this means:

- A desk raised close to its maximum height is at its least stable. Check the stability at your standing height, not at the middle of the range.
- A single-motor desk raises more slowly and often flexes more than a dual-motor frame.
- Casters and uneven floors make any desk worse. If the floor is not level, plan to level the desk rather than move it.

## Motors, memory, and the features that matter daily

Most standing desk features are neutral. A few change how often you actually stand.

**Memory presets** matter more than they sound. A desk that takes twenty seconds of holding a button to reach standing height gets used less than one that moves to a saved position in one press.

**Anti-collision detection** protects whatever is under the desk. If you keep a drawer unit or a chair tucked underneath, this is worth having.

**Motor noise** matters if you share the room or take calls. A loud desk discourages you from moving it during a meeting, which is exactly when a quick height change is most useful.

**Cable management** is usually an accessory rather than a built-in feature, and it matters more on a moving desk than a fixed one. Cables that hang loose behind a rising desk get caught. Plan for a tray or a spine before you buy, not after.

Features that rarely change anything: built-in USB ports that go obsolete, phone apps for a desk that has buttons, and preset counts beyond three or four.

## What to check about the room itself

Before you commit, check three things about the space:

1. **The rise path.** A desk going up needs clear space above it and nothing behind it that the frame will hit.
2. **The power outlet.** A desk that moves needs slack in the cable, or a trailing socket that moves with it.
3. **The door swing and the chair.** A deeper desk in a small room can block a door or leave no room to push a chair back. Measure the chair's travel, not just the desk's footprint.

If the room cannot take a full standing desk, a converter that sits on an existing desk is a reasonable compromise. It gives you the height change without the footprint, at the cost of a smaller working area and less stability.

## A short checklist

- Measure seated and standing elbow height with your shoes on, and write both down.
- Confirm the desk range covers both numbers with margin at each end.
- Choose depth first, then width, then everything else.
- Check stability at your standing height, not at the middle of the range.
- Prefer memory presets and anti-collision over extra ports and apps.
- Plan cable management before the desk arrives, not after.
- Measure the chair's travel and the door swing, not only the desk.

Get the height range, the depth, and the stability right, and a standing desk earns its place in a small room. Get any of those wrong and it becomes a fixed desk with a motor you stop using.

## Frequently asked questions

### What height should a standing desk reach in a small room?

A desk that tops out around 48 inches covers most standing users. The low end matters more in a small room because it sets your seated height. Measure your seated and standing elbow height before choosing, and use the [height range guide](/blog/standing-desk-height-range-measuring-for-sit-stand-use/) for the calculation.

### How much depth does a standing desk need?

A 24-inch top is the practical minimum for a monitor on a stand. A 30-inch top leaves room for a monitor arm and a keyboard, but it takes floor space from a small room. Measure the clear depth before you go wider.

### Can a standing desk fit under a window?

Yes, if the top clears the sill and the frame does not block a radiator. Check the sill height, the radiator depth, and the desk's highest setting before ordering.

### Is a 48-inch desk wide enough for two monitors?

A 48-inch top can hold two 24-inch monitors on a dual arm. Two 27-inch panels need about 50 inches of usable width, so check the arm's reach and the desk's actual width.

### Do I need a four-leg frame for stability?

A two-leg frame is enough for most home-office loads. A four-leg frame adds stability at full height and costs more. The [stability checklist](/blog/standing-desk-stability-what-to-check-before-buying/) covers what to test.


## Sources and further reading

- OSHA, Computer Workstations eTool: Monitors. The preferred viewing distance is 20 to 40 inches (50 to 100 cm) from the eye to the screen, and the centre of the screen should sit 15 to 20 degrees below horizontal eye level. That is what sets the minimum depth your desk needs.
- OSHA, Computer Workstations eTool: Desks. Covers table depth, the space needed under the work surface, and keyboard tray clearance, which together decide whether a deep desk is usable at standing height.
- OSHA, Computer Workstations eTool: Workstation Environment. Relevant if the room is small enough that lighting and glare become part of the decision.
- ANSI/BIFMA G1, Ergonomics Guideline for Furniture Used in Office Work Spaces. The dimensional standard for adjustable work surfaces. Paid document, so compare against a manufacturer's summary.
