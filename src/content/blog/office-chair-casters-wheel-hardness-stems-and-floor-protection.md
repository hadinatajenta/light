## Quick answer

- Measure before ordering. One caster supplier reports grip-ring stems (the push-in fitting that holds a caster in the base) of 10 x 22 mm on most IKEA office chairs and 11 x 22 mm on many others.
- Size the load with W/(C-1)=R, total weight divided by one fewer caster than the base has: a 300 lb chair and sitter on five casters needs 75 lb per caster.
- Match the tread to the floor: soft and non-marking on hardwood, tile and laminate, harder on carpet, and no dual-wheel on high pile.
- A hardness number is useless unless it names its scale. Shore A is what caster listings usually mean.

Office chair casters are the cheapest part of a desk setup and the part most likely to be bought twice. The usual failure is not wheel quality. It is a stem that does not fit the socket, a tread that fights the floor, or a load rating that was never meant to carry a person plus a chair.

## What actually damages a hardwood floor under a desk?

When a hardwood floor starts to look tired under a desk, the chair gets the blame. Odin Lake's hardwood guide reports that the first visible damage usually appears in the finish rather than the wood: faint arcs, dull tracks, scuffs or a hazy patch where the chair repeats the same movements. The same guide lists hard or narrow casters as a main mechanical cause of that damage, and sets finish type, installation quality, floor age, maintenance and grit alongside wood species as contributing factors.

That list sets the limit of what a caster purchase can fix. Hard or narrow wheels concentrate the load of an occupied chair into a small contact patch, and on hardwood that load works on the finish. But the finish that is down, how well the floor was laid, how old it is and how much grit is underfoot decide whether damage shows up this year or in ten. Casters are one contributor among several, and the only one you can change without touching the floor.

So you are not shopping for the best wheel in the abstract. You are matching a tread to a floor, a stem to a base, and a load rating to the person in the chair.

## How should you match the tread to the floor?

Caster and chair manufacturers converge on one rule: the tread should oppose the floor. PU in the table below means polyurethane, the synthetic tread material used on most replacement casters.

| Floor | Caster choice | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hardwood, tile, laminate | Soft, non-marking PU or rubber | Conforms instead of scraping |
| Low-pile or industrial carpet | Dual-wheel caster | Preferred on short pile |
| High-pile carpet | Avoid dual-wheel | Does not roll well |

A caster retailer's guidance puts the logic plainly: soft casters suit hard surfaces such as tile, hardwood and laminate, while hard casters suit softer surfaces such as carpet, partly because hard treads roll with less force and tolerate weight better. A chair brand's floor guide sharpens the same advice for hard floors, naming soft, non-marking polyurethane or rubber treads and telling buyers to confirm the wheel suits both the chair and the floor.

The mechanism is contact area. A hard tread that behaves well on carpet transfers the same concentrated load into a finish when it rolls across oak or engineered boards.

Soft tread is not a free upgrade. It adds drag, so the chair feels heavier to move and rolls sluggishly on carpet, and a soft wheel picks up grit that then acts like sandpaper under load. If you spend the day rolling between a desk and a shelf on low-pile carpet, a hard tread or a dual-wheel caster is more comfortable and the floor is not at risk anyway.

## What does a hardness number actually tell you?

Polyurethane hardness is quoted on the Shore A scale (a durometer scale for softer rubbers and plastics, where a higher number is harder), with common grades such as 70A, 80A, 90A, 95A and 98A. A listing that says "95" without naming a scale is not a usable specification, because it does not tell you whether you are looking at a soft, quiet tread or a hard one that transmits vibration into the floor.

A polyurethane supplier's guidance is blunt about the failure mode: too hard a grade brings noise, vibration and premature wear on both wheel and floor. That is the trade against the higher load capacity and lower rolling resistance of hard grades. Temperature matters too. Polyurethane becomes harder and less elastic in the cold, so a nominal 90A can behave like a much harder wheel in an unheated garage office.

The table below compares the four common caster materials. Its load and floor rows reflect a distributor's material comparison, not independent testing.

| Material | Load character | Floor behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nylon | Supplier: more weight than rubber or PU of same size | Hard tread family, better suited to carpet |
| Polyurethane | Varies with Shore A grade | Supplier's pick for hard floors |
| Rubber | Absorbs shock well | Wears quickly under heavy loads |
| Steel | Carries very heavy loads | May damage delicate floors |

The distributor reports that pound for pound, nylon handles more weight than many rubber or polyurethane wheels of the same size and resists water, chemicals and corrosive substances, while rubber absorbs shock but wears out quickly under heavy loads. By the soft-versus-hard rule above, a nylon tread belongs on carpet rather than a finished hard floor. The polyurethane row is the distributor's judgement, and it holds only where the tread is a soft, non-marking grade.

Load ratings need the same care. A caster supplier states that the capacities on caster listings are working, dynamic loads (the load a caster carries while rolling, as opposed to a static load sitting still), and it publishes a sizing formula that divides total weight by one fewer caster than the assembly uses, so the result carries a safety factor: W/(C-1)=R. If you weigh 250 lb and the chair adds 50 lb, that is 300 lb over five casters. Divide by four rather than five and each caster needs a 75 lb rating. Ask whether a quoted figure is dynamic or static.

## Which stem size does your chair take?

Caster makers are explicit that chair wheels are not universal. Stem diameter and stem length are the critical dimensions, and the existing stem should be measured before ordering. One supplier sells grip-ring stems in two sizes and reports that the split follows the brand.

| Chair | Stem size (mm) |
| --- | --- |
| Most IKEA office chairs | 10 x 22 |
| Many non-IKEA office chairs | 11 x 22 |

Treat those as the sizes to expect rather than a guarantee. The base socket decides fit, not the wheel, so pull one caster out and measure the stem diameter and length instead of trusting a universal-fit claim. While it is out, look at the fitting, the component that joins wheel to chair. A caster retailer warns that plastic fittings are common on office chair casters and can fracture even when the nylon or polyurethane wheel is intact, which means replacing the whole caster rather than the wheel. If a fitting has already broken once on your chair, ask what it is made of before reordering the same model.

Replacing casters as a full set keeps rolling behaviour consistent and stops the base pulling to one side.

## Do chair mats help, or just move the problem?

A mat separates the casters from the finish and creates two problems of its own. Mats trap debris, and a soft wheel can still carry sand across whatever surface it rolls on. The chair maker's hardwood guide is direct that no wheel should be treated as permanently scratch-proof, and it cites floor manufacturers on backings: Shaw's hardwood care guidance warns that certain rubber- or foam-backed plastic mats may discolour flooring, while other floor care guidance advises against dense backings that restrict airflow and trap dirt or moisture. Check the care instructions for your floor before putting anything under the chair.

The second cost is height. A thick mat raises the surface the chair rolls on, which raises the seat relative to a desk that stays where it is. If your gas lift was already near the bottom of its range, that height cannot be taken back by lowering the chair. An adjustable under-desk footrest restores a supported foot position without pushing the chair below its minimum height.

One more case: if the same floor serves a standing desk, an anti-fatigue standing mat is a separate purchase with a separate job. It cushions standing, not rolling. Keep the chair's path off the mat edge, because a thick lip is something casters catch on.

## Buying checklist

- Remove one caster and measure the stem diameter and length before ordering; the two common grip-ring sizes are in the stem table above.
- Check the fitting. A plastic fitting can fracture while the wheel is still fine, and then you replace the whole caster anyway.
- Read the flooring maker's care instructions before choosing a tread or a mat.
- Match the tread to the floor using the floor table above.
- Size the load with W/(C-1)=R, and confirm whether the quoted capacity is dynamic or static.
- Make sure a hardness figure names its scale, and discount it if the caster lives somewhere cold enough to stiffen polyurethane.
- Treat a mat as a compromise: verify the backing is allowed on your floor, accept that it traps grit, and allow for its thickness in how the chair ends up sitting relative to the desk.
- This is a chair-base check, not a caster check. If you weigh more than 275 lb, ask whether the chair was tested to ANSI/BIFMA X5.11 rather than X5.1, the ANSI/BIFMA seating standard. Micom Testing's published scope summary of X5.1 describes it as written around users up to 275 lb and notes that X5.11 exists for heavier users; the sources I hold do not name an edition, so ask for the edition shown on the test report.
- When a seller says "BIFMA certified", ask for the model, the standard edition, the testing organisation and the report. The standard is a testing standard, not a certification in itself.
