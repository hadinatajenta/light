A monitor arm looks like a small upgrade. On a narrow desk, it is often the difference between a setup that works and one you fight every day. The stand that ships with most monitors eats the most useful part of the desk, and it rarely puts the screen at the right height.

It is also the accessory most often bought wrongly, because the two numbers that decide whether it works, the weight it must hold and the desk edge it must grip, are the two the buyer never measures.

## Start with the desk, not the arm

Before you compare arms, measure the desk. A clamp arm needs a flat edge and enough thickness for the clamp to bite. Most clamps specify a range, often something like ten to fifty millimetres, and both ends matter: too thin and the clamp bottoms out, too thick and it will not close.

The material matters as much as the thickness. If the desk is hollow, made of thin veneer over a cardboard core, or has a steel frame running under the edge, a clamp can crush the surface or simply refuse to grip. Two alternatives exist for those desks:

- A **grommet mount** passes a bolt through an existing cable hole in the desktop. It is the most secure option, and it requires a hole you are willing to use.
- A **wall mount** takes the desk out of the equation entirely. It is the right answer when the desk cannot take a clamp and the wall is close enough to reach.

Check the depth too. A short arm on a shallow desk holds the screen closer than you expect, which is usually what you want, but only if the arm can fold back far enough when you are not working. If the arm cannot fold flat, the screen ends up in your face at the end of the day.

## Match the weight range

The most common mistake is buying an arm that is too strong for the monitor. A gas-spring arm is designed around a weight range, and it only balances properly inside it. If your monitor sits at the very bottom of that range, the arm may float upward on its own. At the top of the range, it sags over time and no amount of adjustment fixes it.

Find the monitor's weight without the stand. That number is in the specification, and it is usually well below the weight in the box, because the stand is heavy. Then pick an arm whose range contains it with room on both sides rather than sitting on the boundary.

Two other numbers belong in the same check:

- **VESA pattern.** Almost everything modern is 75 x 75 or 100 x 100 millimetres. Larger monitors and some unusual designs use 200 x 100 or 200 x 200, and an adapter plate is then part of the purchase.
- **Weight with accessories.** A laptop tray, a second small screen, or a heavy aftermarket plate all add load. An arm chosen for the bare monitor may sit at the top of its range once anything is attached.

A fixed arm with a mechanical joint is the alternative to a gas spring. It cannot drift, because nothing is holding it under tension, but you adjust it by hand every time you move it. That trade is worth taking if the screen almost never moves.

## Reach and articulation: what actually moves

An arm is described by how many joints it has, which tells you very little on its own. What matters is how far the screen can travel and how many directions it can move in.

The useful questions are:

- **How far forward does it reach?** If you want the screen over the front edge of a shallow desk, the arm needs enough forward travel to get there without pulling the base off balance.
- **Can it rotate at the screen plate?** Rotation is what lets you switch between landscape and portrait, which is genuinely useful for long documents and spreadsheets.
- **Does the joint hold position?** A joint that needs retightening every few weeks becomes a joint you stop adjusting.
- **How far back does it fold?** This is the one buyers forget, and it decides whether the desk is usable when the screen is pushed away.

## Cable routing is the quiet dealbreaker

An arm only looks tidy if the cables disappear. Look for a channel that runs the length of the arm and is wide enough for a display cable plus a power cable. Too narrow, and you will be taping cables to the outside, which defeats the point of the arm.

Two details separate a channel that works from one that does not. First, it should open along its length rather than requiring you to thread a connector through a closed tube. Second, the entry and exit points should be rounded; a sharp edge will eventually cut a cable that flexes every time the arm moves.

If the arm will sit on a height-adjustable desk, plan the run to the floor as well, because a cable that is fine at sitting height can pull tight at standing height.

## Getting the screen height right

The arm exists to put the screen where your neck wants it, so it is worth knowing what that position is rather than guessing.

OSHA's workstation guidance is specific: the top of the screen should be at or slightly below eye level, and the centre of the screen should sit roughly 15 to 20 degrees below horizontal eye level. The screen should face you directly, no more than 35 degrees off to either side, and sit somewhere between 50 and 100 centimetres from your eyes.

That guidance produces two practical consequences for arm shopping. A screen that must sit 15 to 20 degrees below eye level needs to be lower than most people expect, which means the arm has to hold a low position without sagging. And a screen at arm's length needs the desk to be deep enough, or the arm to fold back far enough, that you are not sitting closer than you planned.

## When a fixed stand is enough

If you never move the screen, a solid desk stand with height and tilt adjustment does the same job for less money and no clamp risk. An arm earns its price when you need reach, when two people share a desk, or when the desk is small enough that the stand's footprint is the problem.

## A short checklist

- Measure desk thickness and the edge type, and check both ends of the clamp range.
- Confirm the desk material can take a clamp, or plan for a grommet or wall mount.
- Note the monitor weight without its stand, then choose an arm whose range contains it with margin.
- Confirm the VESA pattern, including any adapter plate you will need.
- Check the reach, the fold-back, and whether the screen plate rotates.
- Make sure the cable channel opens along its length and has rounded edges.
- Set the screen so the top edge is at or slightly below eye level, then check the angle rather than trusting it.

Done well, a monitor arm gives you back desk space and puts the screen at the height your neck actually wants. Done badly, it is a clamp you do not trust and a screen that slowly droops.

## Frequently asked questions

### What weight can a monitor arm hold?

The arm's published per-monitor limit decides it. Check the monitor's weight without the stand, not the screen size. A 27-inch panel can weigh 8 to 15 pounds, and a 32-inch panel can exceed 20 pounds. The [VESA compatibility guide](/blog/vesa-mount-compatibility-plate-sizes-and-screw-patterns/) covers the plate and screw side.

### How thick can my desk be for a monitor arm?

It depends on the clamp. Many C-clamps take tops up to 3.25 inches, but some stop at 2 inches. Measure the top at the clamp point, including any edge banding.

### Does a monitor arm fit a glass desk?

Only if the desk is rated for a clamp load and the glass is thick enough. A grommet mount or a freestanding base is safer than a clamp on untempered glass. Check the desk maker's clamp guidance first.

### What VESA pattern do I need?

Most monitors use 75 x 75 mm or 100 x 100 mm. Check the monitor's spec sheet and the arm's supported patterns. Adapters exist, but they add depth between the panel and the arm.

### Can a monitor arm work on a standing desk?

Yes, if the clamp fits the top and the arm's height range covers both seated and standing eye levels. Check the arm's vertical travel against the desk's height range.
