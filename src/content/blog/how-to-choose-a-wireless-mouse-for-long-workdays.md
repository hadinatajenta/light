## The mouse is the one device your hand never leaves

Most desk equipment gets chosen once and then ignored. A mouse is different. It sits under your hand for the whole working day, and it is the only peripheral where a two-millimetre mismatch between shell and palm gets repeated thousands of times before lunch. A mouse can be technically excellent and still be wrong for you.

"Ergonomic" on a product page is not a specification. It is a claim that a shape suits somebody, and the useful question is whether that somebody has your hand length, your grip, and your desk. There is no universal fit standard for pointing devices. What follows are the properties you can actually verify.

## Fit: measure your hand before you read the spec sheet

Start with hand length, measured from the crease at the base of your palm to the tip of your middle finger. Manufacturers who take fit seriously publish length, width, and height diagrams, and those three numbers do more work than any marketing copy.

- **Length.** A shell noticeably shorter than your hand tends to leave the heel of your palm resting on the desk. A shell noticeably longer can push the scroll wheel beyond comfortable reach in a palm grip.
- **Height.** This sets how much support you get under the palm. Tall shells suit palm grip. Low, flat shells suit fingertip grip, where the fingers do the work and the palm floats.
- **Width.** Narrow mice encourage the thumb and little finger to pinch the sides, the position most likely to irritate over a long day.

Claw grip sits between the two extremes and usually tolerates a wider range of shells. Since shape cannot be judged from a photograph, treat the return policy as part of the specification. Ordering two candidates and returning one is a legitimate way to buy, and it beats a week of research.

Placement matters as much as shape. OSHA's computer workstation guidance recommends keeping the pointer within easy reach, close to the keyboard and at about the same height as it, with the elbow near ninety degrees rather than extended forward. If your monitor's own base occupies that depth, a desk-mounted arm such as the MOUNTUP Dual Monitor Desk Mount clears the space so the mouse can sit closer to your body. That helps if you are stuck reaching forward for a mouse that is otherwise the right size. Reaching forward all day is a shoulder problem no mouse shape will fix.

## Connectivity, receivers, and interference

Confirm the port first. A 2.4 GHz dongle that needs USB-A is useless on a laptop with only USB-C unless you carry an adapter, and adapters add a failure point to something that lives in a bag. Then choose between three arrangements.

Dongle-only gives the lowest latency and the most reliable connection, but occupies a port. Bluetooth-only means nothing to lose and works directly with tablets, though Bluetooth generally carries higher latency than 2.4 GHz and reconnection after sleep can lag. Mice that support both are the most flexible, though some features are dongle-only, which is worth knowing before you rely on Bluetooth.

Radio interference is the failure people blame on the mouse. The 2.4 GHz band is shared with Wi-Fi, and USB 3.0 ports are a known noise source when a receiver sits beside them. Frequency hopping in the mouse's radio helps, and so does distance. A short USB extension cable that moves the receiver onto the desk, a hand's width from the mouse, is the cheapest fix available.

For reassurance rather than performance, look for FCC or CE marking on the radio, Bluetooth SIG certification on Bluetooth models, and USB-IF compliance on dongles. These confirm basic interoperability and safety, not build quality.

## Sensor and DPI: separate the useful numbers from the noise

Polling rate describes how often the mouse reports its position. 125 Hz is the long-standing office standard. 1000 Hz produces smoother cursor motion, which some people notice when dragging windows or selecting text, and it draws more power from the battery.

DPI — sometimes CPI — is sensitivity. For work, 800 to 1600 DPI covers most monitor setups, and adjustable DPI is genuinely useful if you share a machine or move between a laptop screen and a large display. Five-figure DPI numbers are aimed at competitive gaming.

Two sensor details matter on unusual desks. Optical sensors generally track better across varied surfaces, while laser sensors can work on glass but may introduce acceleration you did not ask for. Lift-off distance determines when the sensor stops reading, so if you habitually lift and reposition the mouse, a low figure keeps the cursor from jumping as you set it down. Glossy and glass surfaces are worth checking against the manufacturer's compatibility notes.

## Battery, switches, and how the mouse ages

Battery claims deserve translation. A figure such as "up to 24 months" is typically calculated on something like eight hours a day, five days a week, with sleep mode active, so real-world life is usually shorter.

Replaceable AA or AAA cells and a built-in battery are a genuine trade-off. Replaceable cells mean no downtime and no cable, at the cost of buying batteries and, on some models, having no charge indicator. A built-in battery charged over USB-C is more convenient and often lighter, but check whether the mouse works while charging and whether an indicator warns you before it dies mid-meeting. USB-C on both ends is worth insisting on; micro-USB is a daily irritation.

Switches wear out before sensors do. Manufacturers publish click lifespan ratings, commonly in the range of tens of millions of clicks, and Omron is one of the names that appears in those specifications. Those are manufacturer figures rather than independent test results, but they remain the most useful comparison available. User-replaceable switches extend the working life of a mouse well beyond its warranty.

"Silent click" is the trade-off most often hidden in marketing. Silent switches reduce noise, usually by softening tactile feedback and shortening rated lifespan. If you share a room or take calls all day, that can be a good deal. If click feel helps you work accurately, it is not.

Materials decide how the mouse feels in year three. Rubberised coatings can degrade and turn tacky. PTFE feet improve glide and are often replaceable. Weight and balance matter as well, though there is no ideal number: heavier shells feel steadier, lighter ones reduce the effort of movement, and front-to-back balance often matters more than total grams.

## Software, operating systems, and the features that vanish

Check OS support before buying. Custom button assignments, DPI profiles, and gesture features often depend on proprietary software that may not cover macOS or Linux fully, and Bluetooth connections sometimes expose fewer features than the dongle does.

If you use a locked-down work laptop where you cannot install software, onboard memory is the feature that matters. Settings are stored in the mouse and travel with it. Without it, every customisation disappears on the machine you use most.

## The buying checklist

- Measure hand length and match it against published mouse dimensions, not against the word "ergonomic".
- Confirm which ports your computer has and whether the mouse needs USB-A, USB-C, or Bluetooth.
- Buy from a retailer whose return policy lets you test fit properly.
- Decide whether you need multi-device pairing, and whether it works over Bluetooth or only with the dongle.
- Check polling rate and DPI range. 125 Hz and 800 to 1600 DPI is enough for most office work.
- Verify surface compatibility and lift-off distance if you use glass, glossy, or unusually small desks.
- Read the assumptions behind the battery claim, check for a battery indicator, and confirm USB-C charging.
- Note the switch type, its rated lifespan, and whether switches are user-replaceable.
- Confirm OS support for any software needed for button remapping.
- Keep the receiver close to the mouse and budget for a short USB extension cable if interference appears.
