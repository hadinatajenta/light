## Quick answer

- Measure the return wall (the short leg of the L) on its own: the second number in a size name is the return's own length, not the total run.
- Return depth is 19 in on the two smaller sizes and 24 in on the largest, so a shallow return is a secondary surface, not a second desk.
- Add 42 in of chair room and a 36 in clear walkway to the desk footprint before you trust a room size.
- Put the primary monitor on the main top, centred about 15 degrees below your horizontal eye line.

A corner desk for a small room is usually sold on the promise of fitting two walls. The number that decides whether it works is not the total length but the return depth, the distance the short leg projects into the room, plus the floor space you need to sit and roll back. This guide uses one manufacturer's L-desk sizing notes and published ergonomic guidance to show what to measure before you order.

## What does corner desk return depth mean?

On an L-shaped desk the return is the shorter leg. Its depth is how far it projects from the return wall into the room; its length is how far it runs along that wall. The two are easy to swap, and the size name only gives you the second one. In a size name such as 64 x 63, the 63 is the return's own length, not the total run, and one end of the return tucks under the main top, so do not add the main top's depth when you measure the return wall.

The table separates the figures a buyer has to keep apart for the quoted sizes.

| Size name (in) | Return length (in) | Return depth (in) |
|---|---:|---:|
| 64 x 63 | 63 | 19 |
| 74 x 63 | 63 | 19 |
| 84 x 72 | 72 | 24 |

In metric terms the 24 in return is about 61 cm, the only one of the three inside the 61 to 76 cm depth commonly quoted for a desk; the 19 in return is about 48 cm. The main top is a separate 30 or 36 in deep. So the 64 x 63 and 74 x 63 work when the return is a secondary surface for papers, a dock or a lamp, while the 84 x 72 buys 5 in more return depth along with a much larger footprint that needs a full 10 x 10 ft and no trim intruding. A 19 in return is not a reliable full-time second workstation.

## Which L-shaped desk measurements should you take?

Measure both wall runs at the finished surface, not the nominal room size. The return wall deserves the same care as the long wall: a desk can pass the main-wall check and still fail on the short side by blocking drawers, a radiator, a cupboard or the walkway. Door swing, window trim, skirting and the delivery path belong on the same sketch. Usable knee room and the position of the support panels also decide how big a corner desk feels, so a shorter desk with better legroom can outperform a longer one.

The table sets out the planning figures that sit behind a room fit. They are not desk dimensions.

| Check | Figure | Basis |
|---|---:|---|
| Chair room from the sitting edge | 42 in | Seller's planning figure |
| Clear walkway past the open end | 36 in | ADA minimum clear width |
| Bare-minimum room for the 84 x 72 | 10 x 10 ft | No spare inches |
| Window sill that clears the main top | above 30 in | Main top height |

The 42 in is the seller's own planning number, not a standard, so test it against your body, your chair and how far you roll back. The 36 in traces to the ADA minimum clear width for walking surfaces, which is the only cited code basis behind these floor figures. The 84 x 72 is described as a bare-minimum fit in a 10 x 10 ft room with no inches to spare, which is why baseboards and trim can break the fit. A window only obstructs the desk if its sill sits below the main top height of about 30 in; a higher sill lets the desk run under it.

## How deep does the return need to be for what you put on it?

A 19 in return is best treated as a shallow shelf: a laptop dock, papers, a task lamp or a second keyboard, but not the predictable place for the primary screen. The 24 in return gives more room to push a keyboard back or fit a larger lamp, but that extra depth only comes with the largest footprint, so the trade is room space for a modest gain.

Put the monitor on the main top. CCOHS guidance places the monitor centre about 15 degrees below the horizontal eye line, within a preferred visual zone of roughly 30 degrees, and the main surface depth is what lets you set the screen there without hunching. [Monitor height on an arm: eye line, tilt, and viewing distance](/blog/monitor-height-on-an-arm-eye-line-tilt-and-viewing-distance/) covers the height and tilt relationship. If you plan a monitor arm, confirm the top can take a clamp load, the force the clamp puts on the panel, because some thin or hollow panels need extra support. A task lamp on the return works if it does not wash out the screen, and [monitor light bars for dark months](/blog/monitor-light-bars-for-dark-months-what-to-check-before-buying/) covers the glare trade-off.

## What construction details matter more than the size label?

Check the corner joint first. The corner connection should have adequate support rather than relying on a thin, unsupported panel, because that is the weak point on cheaper corner designs. The details worth asking a supplier for are panel thickness, frame material, load guidance, adjustable feet, fastener quality, edge sealing and replacement hardware availability. Those answers separate a desk that survives daily use from one that only looks substantial in a listing.

A BIFMA claim also needs an edition. ANSI/BIFMA is voluntary, so a claim is a choice the maker made, not a legal baseline. At the time of the source comparison, the published desk/table standard dated from 2014, with a later draft under ballot. The same standard tests static stability with extendable elements open, exactly the condition a return drawer or pull-out creates in normal use, and its durability cycles are meant to replicate roughly 10 years of heavy commercial use. If the desk is height adjustable, note the 4.5 in minimum clearance beneath a product suspended from the surface, which matters for under-desk storage and CPU mounts.

## What should you check before ordering?

- Measure both finished wall runs, including skirting, trim and radiators, and record the return length and the main top depth separately.
- Tape out 42 in of chair room and a 36 in clear walkway on the floor, then test your own roll-back.
- Check the window sill against the main top height of about 30 in.
- Decide whether the return is a secondary surface or a full-time second desk; that choice points to 19 in or 24 in more clearly than the room label does.
- Confirm knee room and support-panel position at the corner, not just total length.
- Ask for panel thickness, frame material, load guidance, adjustable feet, fastener quality, edge sealing and replacement hardware.
- For a monitor arm, confirm clamp load and panel construction before clamping or drilling.
- Check the cable route at the corner: a grommet or under-desk tray needs a size and position that clears the frame, and [desk grommets and cable ports: sizes to check before buying](/blog/desk-grommets-and-cable-ports-sizes-to-check-before-buying/) explains the measurements.
- Comparing metric listings, remember standard desk depth is quoted as 61 to 76 cm; convert before assuming a 19 in return is close enough.
- If a BIFMA claim appears, ask which edition it refers to and whether stability with extendable elements open is included.

## When the measurements rule a desk out

Sometimes the numbers rule out the desk you wanted, and one part of the setup has to change rather than the whole office. The table pairs each failed measurement with the piece to change.

| Situation | What to change |
|---|---|
| 19 in return needed as a second desk | Shallow freestanding console or fold-down wall shelf |
| Main top too shallow for an eye-line monitor | Monitor arm rated for the screen, after a clamp-load check |
| No 42 in for chair roll-back | Smaller-footprint chair and desk |
| Corner joint flexes, no cable route | Supported corner panel, under-desk cable tray |
