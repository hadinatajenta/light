## Quick answer

- A headrest supports the back of your head during pauses, not your neck while typing.
- Set your working recline first: roughly 100 to 110 degrees for typing and mousing.
- Buy only if it adjusts in height and tilt. A fixed headrest cannot be adjusted to fit you.
- If you regularly recline for breaks, a headrest earns its place. If you stay at a typing recline, lumbar support matters more.

A common reason to shop for a headrest is neck ache at the end of a long day. A headrest is not a fix for every cause of that ache, and adding one on top of a badly set chair does not correct the setup.

That does not make headrests useless. It makes them a specific tool with a narrow job, and the job is rest. Published manufacturer guidance is direct about this: the headrest on an office chair is not for resting on while typing. Once you accept that, the buying question changes from 'does this chair have a headrest?' to 'will this headrest reach the back of my head at the angle I actually sit at?'

## What is a headrest actually for?

A headrest is a rest surface, not a posture corrector. The intended use is a pause: leaning back between tasks, listening on a call, or thinking through a problem with your head supported so your neck can relax. Manufacturer guidance describes it as support for the neck during pauses rather than a surface to press against during active screen work.

That distinction matters because it sets the test. A headrest you have to push your head back into while typing is not supporting anything; it is fighting the neutral head position that screen work calls for. OSHA's description of neutral working posture keeps the head forward-facing, balanced and generally in line with the torso. A headrest that pushes the head forward, or one you need in order to hold your head up, works against that reference.

So the honest framing is this: buy a headrest if you take real breaks in the chair and lean back for them. Skip it if your breaks mean standing up, and put the money into lumbar adjustment and seat depth instead.

## How far back do you need to lean before a headrest helps?

No single recline angle makes a headrest necessary for everyone. The useful test is whether you actually recline for pauses. Set the chair to your working angle first. The table separates three numbers that are often compared as if they were the same thing.

| Figure | What it describes | Read it as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100-110° | Working backrest angle for typing and mousing | A chair setting range |
| 135° | Trunk-to-thigh hip angle in a positional MRI study | Not a backrest setting |
| 2:1 | Synchro-tilt backrest-to-seat ratio | A mechanism ratio |

For typing and mousing, the working range in the table is the one to set first, with your back in full contact with the backrest including the lumbar area. At that angle your head is still doing its own work. Test a headrest with the chair set at your working recline, not fully upright, because a fully upright chair will not show whether the pad clears your head at the angle you use.

On a synchro-tilt mechanism, the backrest and seat are linked at a fixed ratio, so a headline recline figure is not the same as the change in your hip angle. The MRI figure in the table is a study measurement, not a setting you can dial in.

Category matters too. 'Reclining office chair' describes two different products: a task chair with a tilt mechanism that travels roughly 20 to 30 degrees of backrest angle, and a desk recliner with a footrest that lies back past 135 degrees. Recline figures from those two categories cannot be compared, so check which product a listing is actually describing.

## Where should the headrest touch you?

The contact point is the back of your head, above the neck, not the neck itself. Guidance on adjustment is consistent here: align the headrest with the back of your head rather than your neck, and it should give gentle, firm support without pushing the head forward or tipping it back.

This is the check to run in a showroom or during a return window. Sit at your working recline. Lean back slowly. Your head should meet the pad on the bony ridge at the back of your skull, with your head in a neutral, forward-facing position. If the pad lands on the curve of your neck, it is not meeting the intended contact point. If you must tilt your chin down to make contact, the pad is not positioned for a neutral head position.

If you cannot get the pad onto the back of your head with the adjustments available, the headrest does not fit you, regardless of how good the chair's other specs look.

## Which headrest adjustment do you actually need?

Headrest structures come in fixed and adjustable forms, including 2D and 3D versions. The minimum useful specification is height plus tilt. For an ergonomic chair, the headrest needs to adjust in both height and tilt angle so it can be set for different users. A height-only or tilt-only headrest is a weaker proposition, and a fixed one cannot accommodate different people's neck positions at all.

The table shows the minimum adjustment to confirm before buying.

| Headrest type | Height adjustment | Tilt adjustment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed | No | No |
| Adjustable 2D/3D | Yes | Yes |

If a listing adds other adjustments, check what they do. The essential question is still whether the pad can be set to the back of your head at the angle you actually use.

## Does the recline mechanism support the headrest?

A headrest is only as good as the recline it works with. On cheaper mechanisms, the seat can rise as the backrest reclines, which breaks your floor contact and undermines the whole movement. If your feet come off the floor as you lean back, the headrest will feel like something you are lying on rather than resting against, and it will not give the rest you are buying it for.

Synchro-tilt designs are the ones usually cited as keeping the seat pan tilting slightly back with the backrest, which helps keep your feet planted. If you are buying a headrest chair specifically to spend real time reclined, the mechanism deserves more scrutiny than the pad.

## What do standards tell you about headrests, and what do they not?

BS EN 1335-1:2020, the European standard that sets dimensions for four types of office work chairs and the test methods for determining them, added dimensions for neck rests and headrests as a technical change from the 2000 edition. If you see a claim citing EN 1335, check which edition it refers to: a chair tested to the older edition tells you nothing about headrest dimensions.

The standard also separates chairs into types Ax, A, B and C with different dimensional ranges and adjustability, where Type Ax is the extended-range classification. A product page or tender document should name the type, not just the standard. 'EN 1335 certified' on its own does not say which type.

Part 2 of the standard covers safety, strength and durability, and those requirements assume 8 hours of daily use by people weighing up to 110 kg (about 17 stone). Heavier users or multi-shift use fall outside that baseline, and no headrest changes that.

To be clear: OSHA has no chair-specific standard. The agency offers general ergonomics and office furniture recommendations instead, and those emphasize adjustability of seat pan depth, backrest height and tilt. A headrest is not the focus of that guidance.

## Buying checklist

Before you pay for a headrest chair, work through these in order.

- Set your working recline first. Confirm the headrest does not push your head forward at that angle.
- Confirm height and tilt adjustment both exist. Ask for the specification, not the photo.
- Check the contact point on your own head, not the display model's: back of the skull, neutral head position.
- Sit in the chair reclined long enough to judge it. A headrest that feels fine for a moment can be wrong over a work session.
- Test the mechanism under recline: do your feet stay planted, and does the seat stay stable without lifting?
- Check the standard edition and chair type if a certification claim is part of the pitch.

## If adjustment is not enough

Sometimes the setup is right and the chair still does not fit. These are the situations where a specific product type is the answer rather than more adjustment.

| Situation | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Backrest does not recline enough to rest your head, but the chair is otherwise good | An add-on headrest with independent height and tilt |
| Chair has a fixed headrest that hits your neck, not your skull | A replacement adjustable headrest that reaches the back of your head |
| You recline to rest but your feet lift off the floor | A footrest, plus a chair with a synchro-tilt mechanism |
