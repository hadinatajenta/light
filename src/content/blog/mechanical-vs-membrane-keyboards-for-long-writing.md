Writing all day puts a different kind of demand on a keyboard than gaming does. You are not chasing
speed, you are managing fatigue, accuracy, and the small aches that build over months. That changes
which features are worth caring about.

## The difference in one paragraph

A mechanical keyboard gives every key its own physical switch, so the key actuates at a defined point
and can be made to click, bump, or move silently. A membrane keyboard sits keys on a single rubbery
sheet, which is cheaper and quieter but tends to feel vague and to soften with age. Neither is
objectively better, but they fail in different ways.

## Travel and force: what the numbers describe

A switch description is a set of physical measurements, and those measurements predict how the board
feels. A standard mechanical switch travels about 4 mm in total and registers around 2 mm down, which
leaves a short distance between registration and the bottom of the key. A low profile switch shortens
that to roughly 3 mm of travel and a little over 1 mm to register, which is why laptop style boards
feel quicker. A rubber dome board travels much like a full height mechanical one, but it registers at
or near the bottom, so every press is a full press. Force adds a second dimension: light switches
commonly sit around 35 to 45 gram force, medium ones around 50 to 60, and heavy ones above 65. A
light tactile bump marks the registration point, which lets a typist release before the bottom, while
linear switches are smooth and give no cue at all.

## What long writing actually needs

Your hands spend hours in the same position. What helps is consistent key feel, a sensible amount of
travel, and a layout that keeps your hands near the home row. Tactile feedback matters because it
tells your fingers the key registered without forcing you to press all the way down, which reduces
the effort of every keystroke.

Key pitch belongs in the same list. OSHA's keyboard guidance puts the horizontal spacing between key
centres at 18 to 19 mm and the vertical spacing at 18 to 21 mm, which is why a cramped travel
keyboard feels wrong even when the keys themselves are full size.

## Switch choice for typists

If you share a room, prioritise quiet switches over loud ones. If you like certainty, look for a
light tactile bump. Avoid very heavy switches unless you have tried them, since they punish long
sessions. The honest advice is to try a few types before committing, because preference is personal
and reviews cannot predict your fingers.

## Noise in a shared room

Sound is the constraint many writers share, and it is hard to compare from a specification because
there is no standard keyboard noise test. A clicky switch adds a deliberate click to every press,
while a dampened tactile switch or a low profile membrane board trades most of that away. The source
of the sound matters as much as the switch: keycaps bottoming out on the plate and a hollow case both
add noise, so a quiet switch in a loud board can still carry. O-rings under the keycaps shorten the
travel slightly and soften the bottom out. If you share a room or record calls, the test that matters
is a recording from about a metre away, which is where the other person sits. Without a listen, treat
quiet as a claim rather than a specification.

## Durability, repair, and what you replace

A membrane keyboard fails as one part. The domes sit in a bonded sheet, so a worn or damaged section
is not replaceable, and the failure appears as mushiness or dead zones after years of heavy use. Dome
sheets are commonly rated for a few million presses, and budget boards often publish no figure at
all. A mechanical switch is usually rated in tens of millions of actuations, with the rating coming
from the switch maker rather than an independent test. The practical advantage is repair: a switch on
a hot swap socket lifts out, a worn keycap set comes off, and a damaged cable on a removable board is
a cable rather than a keyboard. If you intend to write on the same board for a decade, that is the
argument for mechanical, not the typing feel.

## Membrane still deserves a place

A good membrane keyboard is quiet, cheap, spill-resistant, and perfectly capable of forty thousand
words a month. If your priority is a low-profile, silent board for a shared office, it is a rational
choice rather than a compromise. The weakness appears after years of heavy use, when worn domes
become mushy.

## Layout and size

Smaller layouts save desk space but push functions onto layers, which you must learn. Full-size boards
give you a number pad that many people never touch. A compact layout with a separate number pad is a
common compromise. Whatever you pick, make sure the keys you use most are within easy reach.

Keycaps deserve their own line. ABS plastic develops a shine from finger oils, while PBT keeps its
texture for longer, and double shot legends are moulded through the cap rather than printed on top.
Tall sculpted caps also put the fingers at a different reach from flat ones.

## How to decide based on how you type

Start from a test, not a category. Type for ten minutes on your current board and note three things:
how hard you press, whether you bottom out on every key, and where your hands ache afterwards. Then
apply the constraints in order.

- **Noise.** If anyone else can hear the board, or you record audio, clicky switches are out.
- **Layout.** Count the keys you actually use. A number pad you never touch is desk space the mouse
  could have.
- **Force.** Match the switch to the test above. A light hand on a heavy switch tires, and a heavy
  hand wastes a light one.
- **Material.** PBT keycaps and double shot legends outlast the alternatives.
- **Repairability.** Hot swap sockets are the feature that matters on a five year horizon.

If you cannot try the switches, a tester costs less than a keyboard and answers the question that
reviews cannot.

## What to ignore

Rapid trigger features and extreme polling rates are aimed at competitive games and will not improve
your writing. Per-key lighting is decoration. Hot-swappable sockets are genuinely useful, because they
let you change switches without a soldering iron, but they should not be the reason you buy.

## Practical closing advice

Set your keyboard so your elbows rest near ninety degrees and your wrists stay straight. Add a wrist
rest only if it supports the palm without bending the wrist upward. Then choose the switch feel that
lets you type for an hour without thinking about your hands. That single test is worth more than any
specification list.

## A short checklist

- Try the switches, or a tester, before buying a full board.
- Choose force from how hard you actually press.
- Treat noise as the first constraint if you share a room.
- Check the layout against the keys you use, not the keys in the photo.
- Prefer PBT keycaps and a switch rating in tens of millions of presses.
- Prefer hot swap sockets if you want the board to last.

## Sources and further reading

- OSHA, Computer Workstations eTool: Keyboards. The source for elbows at keyboard height, straight wrists, the 18 to 19 mm horizontal key spacing guidance, and the advice to take a five minute break every hour.
- OSHA, Computer Workstations eTool: Work Process, for the note that a proficient typist can exceed 18,000 keystrokes per hour. That repetition is why posture matters more than any single component.
- ISO 9241, Ergonomics of human-system interaction, the series covering workstation layout and physical input device design.
- Switch manufacturers' published specifications, for travel, actuation force, and endurance ratings. Treat the endurance figure as a design rating rather than an independent test result.
- The product's specification sheet, for keycap material, switch force, and layout.
