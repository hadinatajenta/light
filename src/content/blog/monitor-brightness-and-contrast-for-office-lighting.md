## Quick answer

- Compare static (native) contrast, not the dynamic number on the box. IPS panels sit near 1000:1; VA panels often reach 3000:1 or more.
- Black level drives contrast as much as peak white does. Halve the black floor at the same peak white and the ratio doubles.
- Matte suits a desk under ceiling lights; choose glossy only if you control every reflection.
- Buy brightness headroom and run the panel below maximum. Running at 100 percent can age the backlight two to three times faster than running at about half.
- Test the finish before you pay: switch the screen off and look at its reflection. Mirror-like means glossy; soft and blurred means matte.

Most monitor advice stalls on resolution and refresh rate, because both are easy to compare. The numbers that decide whether you can keep reading at four in the afternoon are harder to line up: how much white the panel can put out, how dark its black stays once the room lights are on, and whether the coating in front of the glass hands the ceiling back to you.

## How do brightness and black level combine into contrast?

A nit is one candela per square metre, the unit used to describe how much light a screen emits. Contrast is the ratio between the brightest white a panel holds and its black level in the same frame, and you can run that arithmetic on a spec sheet before you spend anything.

The table shows how the same peak brightness produces two very different contrast ratios once the black level changes.

| Example screen | Peak white (nits) | Black level (nits) | Contrast |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 300 | 0.3 | 1000:1 |
| B | 300 | 0.1 | 3000:1 |

Doubling peak output doubles the ratio only if the black floor stays where it was. Brightness is the number a bigger backlight can add. The black level is what room light attacks first.

Published contrast figures are usually taken in two steps: one full white screen, then one full black screen. Neither step resembles a desktop with a document, a chat window and a video call up at once, and neither accounts for light bouncing off the panel. In a lit room, reflections raise the black level the panel can reach, so the contrast you actually see, sometimes called ambient contrast, is lower than the published figure. That is why the same 1000:1 panel can look crisp in a hallway and muddy beside a south-facing window.

## How many nits do you actually need in a lit office?

No single figure suits every desk, and any guide that hands you one is hiding the variable that matters, which is where your light comes from. What you are buying is white output that stays ahead of the light bouncing off the screen surface.

There is a test you can run on the monitor you already own. At the brightest hour of your working day, set brightness to maximum, open a white document and look at black text on it. If the whites read grey and the gap between white and black has narrowed, the panel cannot answer the room. If whites still read white and letter edges hold, you have enough output and the problem is position or glare rather than brightness.

Headroom is worth paying for even if you never use it. A monitor run at 100 percent brightness can degrade its LEDs two to three times faster than one run at around 50 percent, and backlight ageing shows up as dimmer output, weaker contrast, a white-balance shift and uneven brightness rather than as sudden failure. The liquid crystal layer is not the wear item; it can stay functional for decades, and the 30,000 to 50,000 hour life figures quoted for LCDs describe the illumination behind it.

Badges do not settle this. An HDR10 label describes the signal format a display accepts, not a guaranteed peak brightness, so a model sold on that badge, such as the Samsung ViewFinity HDR10 monitor, still needs its documented brightness checked against your room. The figure you want is in the manual, along with the setting you would run day to day.

## Why is the dynamic contrast number not worth comparing?

Native contrast, also called static contrast, describes what a panel can do within a single frame. Dynamic contrast describes how far the display moves its light output between different moments, usually by dimming the backlight during dark scenes. There is no universal standard for assessing dynamic contrast ratio, so manufacturers measure it their own way and the figures cannot be compared across brands.

That matters because a dynamic figure in the tens of thousands can look spectacular on a spec sheet while feeling ordinary in a real desktop scene. A desktop is not a dark movie; it is bright text beside bright windows beside mid-grey toolbars. When you compare two monitors, look for the words static, native or typical next to the ratio, and treat anything much larger as a different measurement wearing the same units.

Even an honest static figure is a best case, since the standard method pits full white against full black, content that rarely appears together in office work. Use published contrast to rank panels, not as a promise about your screen at 3pm.

## Which panel type should an office buyer pick?

IPS (in-plane switching) and VA (vertical alignment) are the two liquid-crystal panel types behind most office monitors, and the contrast gap between them is wide enough to plan around.

The table compares the static contrast each panel type typically delivers.

| Panel type | Typical static contrast | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| IPS | Around 1000:1 | General office work |
| VA | 3000:1 or more | Dark interfaces, photo review |

IPS is the predictable choice for a desk full of documents, spreadsheets and code: the black level is unremarkable, but the panel is stable, widely available and consistent between brands. VA's deeper black floor earns its keep wherever near-black tones have to stay separable instead of merging into one grey.

Two qualifications. A deeper black on the spec sheet can vanish in a lit room, because reflected light, not the panel, sets the black you perceive; fix the light before you pay for contrast. And self-emissive panels, where each pixel makes its own light and black comes from switching a pixel off rather than blocking a backlight, sit outside this comparison. That route is worth taking only once the rest of the workspace is sorted.

## Matte or glossy: which finish survives an office ceiling?

You can identify a finish without a light meter. With the screen switched off, look at its reflection: a clear, mirror-like image means glossy, and a soft, blurred image means matte or a lighter semi-matte coating.

Under overhead office lighting, matte is usually the right answer, because it breaks up the large reflected patches that tire your eyes across a working day. The anti-glare layer behind the effect is applied during panel production and works by diffusing light that hits the surface, which cuts glare and improves visibility when a source is aimed at the screen.

Diffusion has a cost. The same layer scatters a little of the light the monitor emits, which slightly reduces sharpness and takes something off colour and contrast. So matte trades a small amount of image punch for a stable image all day. In a dim room, with blinds down and no fixture in the reflection path, glossy earns that punch back.

Matte also shows dust, fingerprints and smudges less readily and needs cleaning less often, which matters on a hot-desked setup. What it does not do is cancel glare. If a bright source sits directly behind the screen, it still eats contrast, and that is a positioning problem rather than a coating problem.

## What can you change in the room before you buy a monitor?

OSHA's computer workstation guidance is direct: bright light sources behind a display create contrast problems and make the screen difficult to see. The recommended fixes cost nothing, including blinds or drapes over the window, indirect or shielded lighting instead of a bare fixture in your eyeline, and reorienting the workstation so the window sits beside the desk rather than straight behind or in front of you. OSHA has no specific standard for computer workstations, but ergonomic hazards still fall under the general duty to keep a workplace free of recognised hazards, so asking for a blind is a reasonable request rather than a personal preference.

Position is the second lever. A panel that tilts and pans changes where a reflection lands, and an articulating mount buys that freedom when the stand does not. Check the arm's rated capacity against your panel's weight and the clamp limits against your desk thickness; the guide to [choosing a monitor arm for a heavy ultrawide](/blog/how-to-choose-a-monitor-arm-for-a-heavy-ultrawide-monitor/) is where those numbers usually run out. Two screens lit by one window are harder, because the reflection is duplicated and each panel needs its own angle. A dual arm that aims each screen separately, such as the MOUNTUP dual monitor arm, is what makes that workable, and the [two-monitor arm setup guide](/blog/how-to-choose-a-monitor-arm-for-a-two-monitor-setup/) covers the geometry before you commit.

## What should you check before you buy?

- Map the light first. Note every window, ceiling fixture and lamp that can reach the screen or your eye. Position explains a large share of office monitor disappointment, well before panel quality does.
- Run the reflection test on any candidate. Screen off, look at the surface, decide which finish suits the room you actually work in.
- Ask for the static, native or typical contrast figure in writing. If a listing only offers a number in the tens of thousands, ask what it measures.
- Find peak brightness in the manual, not on the badge, then confirm the setting you would use daily sits below maximum.
- Buy where the return window covers a fortnight of real work. Coating and black level are the two things a spec sheet cannot tell you.

If the screen has been moved, the blind is down and the image is still unstable, the next step is a purchase.
