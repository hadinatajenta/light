## What actually decides whether a dual monitor arm will work

Screen size is the number most shoppers compare, and it is the least useful one for this purchase. Mounting is a mechanical problem. A plate bolts to the back of each monitor, an arm carries that plate, and a clamp or grommet holds the assembly to the desk. Every failure point is a measurement: the hole pattern on the monitor, its mass without the stand, the thickness and material of the desk, and the geometry of the arm itself. Get those four right and almost any well-built arm will do the job.

The checks below are in the order I would run them. Where a marketing phrase hides the detail that matters, I have said so.

## Start with the VESA pattern and the screws

Displays intended for mounting follow the VESA Flat Display Mounting Interface Standard (FDMI), which defines the hole patterns. The table below shows the patterns you are most likely to meet and the screw size the standard specifies for each.

| Pattern (mm) | Screw (FDMI) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 75 x 75 | M4 | Thread depth often shallow |
| 100 x 100 | M4 | Most common on 24 to 32 inch panels |
| 100 x 200 |  | Check screw size and depth in the manual |

The M4 figure is the standard's default, not a guarantee: some monitors use M5 or M6, and the hardware in the box is not always the right size for yours. Screw length matters as much as diameter. A screw that runs too deep can damage the monitor housing, while one that engages too little thread can let the plate pull free under load. Recessed mounting points, common on curved backs, may need spacers or longer screws so the plate sits flat instead of rocking on the chassis.

Measure before ordering. If the monitor manual lists a maximum screw depth, that is the number to respect.

## Weight capacity belongs to each arm, and so does the minimum

Use the monitor's weight without the stand, as listed in its specifications, not the shipping weight and not the weight with the base attached. Then add whatever you hang on it: a webcam, a light bar, a small PC strapped to the back. Those accessories count toward the load the arm has to hold.

Gas spring arms have two limits, not one. There is a maximum weight they will hold, and often a minimum weight below which the spring cannot be adjusted far enough down. A light panel on a spring rated for heavy displays tends to drift upward no matter how you turn the tension screw. Most arms include a tension adjustment, usually an Allen key at the joint, and the installation manual describes the procedure for your specific model. Allow a few minutes per arm for that adjustment, and recheck it a week later once the monitors have settled.

Manufacturers often market arms by screen size, as in "supports up to 32 inch monitors". That is a rough guide. A thin 32 inch panel can weigh less than a 27 inch display with a dense backlight and a metal mounting plate. Weight decides.

"Heavy-duty" is not a standardised term. It carries no rating. Compare the stated capacity per arm and the listed materials instead.

## The desk is half the decision

Clamp and grommet specifications are where most returns begin. Manufacturers state a supported desk thickness range, for example 10 to 85 mm, and that range differs between products. A desk thinner or thicker than the stated window will not clamp safely, and no amount of tightening fixes it.

Edge shape matters too. A beveled or lipped desktop reduces the flat surface the clamp can grip, and a cable tray or drawer frame under the desk can physically block the jaw from closing. A grommet mount has its own numbers: the required hole diameter range and the desk thickness the bolt assembly can span, both in the installation manual.

Material is the quiet risk. Clamping force is concentrated on a small area, and the arm plus two monitors loads it continuously. Some manufacturers warn against glass desks or require padding; check the manual rather than assuming. Thin hollow-core and low-density particleboard tops can crush or bow over months of static load. If your desk is in that category, a freestanding dual stand or a wall mount transfers the load elsewhere, at the cost of desk space or drilling.

"Fits most desks" is the claim that ignores thickness limits, edge shape, and material in a single phrase.

## Motion, height, and reach: the numbers behind "full motion"

Tilt, swivel, and rotation ranges are usually published in degrees, and they describe nominal travel. Real travel is smaller. A wide panel swings into the arm or the wall before it reaches the quoted swivel figure, and a thick chassis limits how far it tilts before the top edge contacts the joint housing. If you need portrait orientation for documents or code, confirm the arm rotates a full 90 degrees with your panel size installed.

Height adjustment is what makes the setup ergonomic. OSHA's computer workstation guidance puts the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level. If your desk is fixed and unusually tall, or you are shorter than average, check that the arm's height range actually reaches that position. A dual arm that only rises a few centimetres above the base will not rescue a tall desk.

Reach works as simple arithmetic. Suppose the clamp sits at the rear edge of a 60 cm deep desk and you want the panel about 70 cm from your eyes while you sit roughly 10 cm back from the front edge. You need enough extension to place the panel a few centimetres forward of the base, plus spare travel to swing it aside when you are not using it. ISO 9241 covers workstation layout and viewing distance in general terms; the figure that suits you depends on your screen size and your eyesight.

## Cable routing, materials, and what the warranty covers

Cable channels sized for thin wires will not always accept a thick DisplayPort lead or a moulded power connector. Where the documentation states a maximum cable diameter, that is the figure to check against the thickest cable you own. Leave slack loops so the arm can travel through its full range without tugging on the connectors at the back of the monitor.

Steel and aluminium arms and joints hold up. Plastic bushings, covers, and cable clips tend to crack or wear first, and warranty coverage for them is often limited. Gas springs can lose tension over time and are not always replaceable; mechanical springs tend to hold their setting longer but need more careful adjustment at installation. Neither design is automatically better. What matters is whether the tension mechanism is user-serviceable and whether the warranty names the spring and the joints, or only the frame.

## When a monitor arm is the wrong answer

Skip the arm if your monitors have no VESA mounting holes at all, which happens on some all-in-one units and budget panels. Skip it for glass desks where the maker warns against clamping. Skip it if the combined load sits above every arm in your budget, or if you rent and cannot risk marking a desk edge with a clamp. Freestanding dual stands and wall mounts exist precisely for those situations.

## Buying checklist

Work through this in order. If a number is missing from the listing, find it in the manual before you buy.

- Weigh each monitor without its stand, then add webcams, lights, and anything else mounted to the back.
- Confirm each monitor's VESA pattern and the screw size and maximum depth the manual allows.
- Check the arm's per-arm weight range, including the minimum if it uses gas springs.
- Measure your desk thickness at the point where the clamp will sit, not in the middle of the desk.
- Look under the desk for cable trays, drawer frames, or braces that would block a clamp or grommet.
- Measure the grommet hole diameter if you plan to mount through the desk.
- Verify the desk material is rated for clamping, and read any glass or thin-top warning.
- Check height adjustment range against your seated eye level and your fixed desk height.
- Check reach against the distance you want between your eyes and the screen.
- Confirm tilt, swivel, and rotation suit your panel size and orientation needs.
- Compare cable channel diameter with your thickest cable.
- Read the warranty for coverage of the gas spring and joints, not just the frame.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much weight can a dual monitor arm hold?

Check the per-arm limit, not the total. A dual arm rated at 20 pounds per arm can hold two 20-pound monitors, but the desk clamp and the desktop also have to carry that load. Confirm the clamp rating with the desk maker if the top is thin or hollow.

### Do both monitors need the same VESA pattern?

No, as long as each arm's plate supports the monitor's pattern. Most arms include 75 x 75 and 100 x 100 plates. The [VESA compatibility guide](/blog/vesa-mount-compatibility-plate-sizes-and-screw-patterns/) covers the exceptions and the screw depth.

### Can one dual arm fit two 27-inch monitors?

Often, if each monitor is within the per-arm weight limit and the arm's reach clears the desk. Check the maximum extension and the total width of the two panels before ordering.

### How thick does a desk need to be for a dual arm clamp?

That depends on the clamp. Some take tops up to 3.3 inches, others stop at 2 inches. Measure the top at the clamp and check the grommet option if the edge is not usable.

### Is a dual arm better than two single arms?

A dual arm uses one clamp and one post, which saves desk space. Two single arms give each monitor its own height and reach. On a small desk, the single clamp is usually the deciding advantage. The [single-arm guide](/blog/how-to-choose-a-monitor-arm/) covers the alternative.
