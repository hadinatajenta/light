## Start with your body, not the product listing

Standing desk converters exist because replacing a desk is expensive, disruptive, or impossible in a rented flat or an office with built-in furniture. They are a compromise device, and the compromise shows up in three places: height range, load capacity, and footprint. All three are checkable with a tape measure and the manufacturer's specification sheet.

Ignore "ergonomic" as a label. The word is unregulated on product listings. A converter is ergonomic for you only if its keyboard tray reaches your standing elbow height and its monitor platform puts the screen top where your eyes need it.

## Measure standing elbow height and screen height

Three measurements decide whether a converter works for you, and the table below shows what each one has to be checked against.

| Measurement | Sets the target for | Check against |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standing elbow height | Keyboard tray height | Tray adjustment range |
| Standing eye height | Screen top position | Platform range plus monitor height |
| Available desk depth | Base and keyboard clearance | Converter footprint |

Standing elbow height is often quoted as around 60% of total height, but that rule of thumb is a starting point, not a measurement: body proportions vary, and the gap between an average and your own body is easily a few centimetres. Measure it directly instead. Stand in the shoes you work in, relax your arms, bend one elbow to 90 degrees, and measure from the floor to the underside of the forearm. If that lands at 105 cm and your desk surface is 74 cm, the converter has to lift the keyboard tray at least 31 cm. A tray that tops out below your elbow height leaves you reaching down or hunching.

Screen height is the second constraint. OSHA's computer workstation guidance and the ISO 9241 series describe the same arrangement: forearms roughly horizontal, upper arms hanging relaxed, screen top at or slightly below eye level. Because eye height sits above elbow height, the monitor platform normally needs more range than the keyboard tray. Check the platform's highest position with your monitor's own height added, and remember that a VESA-mounted monitor sits differently from one resting on its base.

Watch the units on the specification sheet. Some makers quote travel from the desk surface to the platform, others quote overall converter height.

## Weight capacity comes as two numbers

Most converters publish a total load limit and a separate limit for the monitor platform. Total load includes monitors, keyboard, mouse, wrist rest, dock, laptop and lamp; the platform figure is usually displays only. Add your own numbers up honestly. If they come to 13 kg, buy capacity comfortably above 13 kg rather than exactly at it, because a published limit is a ceiling, not a working target.

Dual-monitor claims deserve a second round of scepticism about width. A platform can be rated for two displays by weight and still be too shallow for two bases or two VESA plates. If a listing promises dual monitors without publishing both the weight limit and the platform dimensions, treat the claim as unfinished.

## Footprint and desk fit

Freestanding converters fail on shallow desks. The base sits behind the keyboard tray, so base plus tray has to fit inside your desk depth with your forearms still supported. On a 60 cm desk, a converter that needs more depth leaves the keyboard overhanging the front edge, which shifts load forward and makes tipping more likely.

Clamp-on models trade footprint for a clamp, and that is where compatibility breaks. Check the clamp's maximum opening against your desk thickness including edge banding, and look at whether your desk edge is beveled or rounded. Grommet mounts need an existing hole of the right diameter in the right place. Neither detail appears in most marketing copy; both are in the manual.

Measure width too. A converter that leaves no room for a mouse beside the keyboard pushes everything onto the tray, which makes tray depth and the clearance between tray and platform your next limits.

## Adjustment mechanisms

The table compares the common lift mechanisms on what you notice daily.

| Mechanism | Adjustment | Power | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gas spring | Lever or paddle assist | None | Higher effort at heavy payload |
| Electric | Push button | Outlet | Cost and cable routing |
| Manual lift and lock | Lift to a detent | None | Few settings; heavy when loaded |

Frequency of switching decides more than price. Someone who raises the desk once in the morning will not care about a manual lift. Someone alternating every hour will resent it, and an electric model earns its cost. Check whether adjustment is continuous or stepped, because a handful of detents may not include your elbow height.

If you go electric, read the specification sheet rather than the images. Electric desktop converters such as the [Huuger electric standing desktop model](product:huuger-electric-standing-desktop-computer) are only useful to you if the published height range, capacity and footprint clear your measurements.

## Stability, materials and durability

Wobble at standing height is the complaint that recurs in owner reviews. Individual reviews are anecdotes rather than evidence, but a pattern that repeats across many of them is worth reading. What a listing can actually verify: a wide base, a locking mechanism that fixes the platform when raised, and stated compliance with ANSI/BIFMA X5.5, the desk and table standard covering stability, strength and durability. A maker claiming that compliance should be able to produce test documentation. "Sturdy" on its own means nothing.

Materials matter most at the joints. Powder-coated steel or aluminium frames with high-pressure laminate or solid wood surfaces hold up; thin plastic in load-bearing parts does not. On laminate and particle board, GREENGUARD certification is a useful marker, though it speaks to emissions rather than durability. Read the warranty by component: how long the frame and mechanism are covered tells you what the maker expects to fail.

## Who should buy one, and who should not

Buy a converter if you cannot change the desk: a rental, a built-in desk, a shared office, a fixed-height desk you like. It also makes sense as a low-commitment trial of standing work before committing to a full sit-stand desk.

Skip it if your desk is shallow and your payload is heavy, because footprint and platform requirements start to conflict. Skip it if you want the full range and stability of a real sit-stand desk; a converter inherits the height of whatever it sits on, seated as well as standing. And if you work from a laptop alone, a raised stand plus an external keyboard solves the same posture problem, usually for less money and with less depth.

If you already own a monitor arm, read the mounting section of the manual first. Converters rarely have a pass-through for an arm base, and combining the two often leaves displays at a compromise height that suits neither sitting nor standing. Some people keep monitors on a desk-mounted dual arm such as the [MOUNTUP dual monitor desk mount](product:mountup-dual-monitor-arm) and use the converter for keyboard and mouse only. That works if you stand for most of the day, and is a poor fix if you alternate.

Standing tolerance is a separate problem from the converter itself: footwear, flooring and how long you stay upright matter as much as the platform.

## Buying checklist

- Measure standing elbow height in your work shoes; the keyboard tray must reach it.
- Subtract desk height from elbow height for the minimum lift you need.
- Measure standing eye height and add your monitor's height to check the platform range.
- Total the weight of everything going on the converter, then check the platform's own monitor limit.
- Compare platform width and depth with your displays, particularly for two.
- Measure desk depth so base plus tray fits with no overhang.
- Check clamp opening against desk thickness, edge bevels and grommet diameter.
- Confirm continuous or stepped adjustment, and whether any setting matches your number.
- Choose the mechanism by how often you switch.
- Look for ANSI/BIFMA X5.5 documentation, a metal frame, and a warranty naming the mechanism.
- Check VESA pattern (75x75 mm or 100x100 mm) and weight limit if the converter includes a mount.
- Read the assembly steps before buying, not after.
