## Start with the geometry, not the filter

A reflection obeys the same rule as a mirror: light leaves the surface at the angle it arrived. If a window sits behind your left shoulder, the bright patch lands on the screen to the right of whatever you are reading. Rotating the monitor, lowering one blind, or sliding the desk half a metre along the wall usually removes it, and that costs nothing.

The offending source is often not the window but a ceiling fixture or a sunlit white wall. Glare is also partly a contrast problem: the same reflection feels worse in a dim room, because your eyes have adapted to a low background and the specular patch reads as far brighter than it measures. Raising ambient light, or moving a desk lamp out of the reflected sightline, changes how objectionable the reflection is even when the physics has not changed. OSHA's computer workstation guidance treats glare control as a placement question, and ISO 9241-307:2008, which covers analysis and compliance test methods for electronic visual displays including glare and reflections, assumes the easy geometry has already been dealt with.

Only then should you spend money. If reflections survive, you either buy a display whose coating handles them or add a filter to the one you own. Most people reach for the filter first because it is cheaper. That is often right, but the decision comes down to coating type and how much light you can afford to lose.

## Anti-glare and anti-reflective coatings are not the same thing

An anti-glare (AG) coating diffuses reflections using controlled surface roughness. Light hits the roughened surface and scatters rather than returning as a sharp image, which is what makes a matte panel readable in a bright room. The cost is sparkle: on light backgrounds the texture itself becomes visible as fine grain, and the stronger the diffusion, the more visible it gets.

An anti-reflective (AR) coating uses interference layers to suppress reflection instead of scattering it. It preserves clarity and colour, but it is far less effective against a strong direct source, so it is the wrong tool for a window in your eyeline.

The datasheet figure that tells you how much diffusion you are buying is haze, usually quoted as a percentage. If a manufacturer publishes it, ask which test method was used, because ASTM D1003 haze values are not directly comparable with numbers produced another way. Higher haze generally means fewer visible specular reflections and more sparkle. There is no universally correct value, only a trade-off; if you judge colour for a living, a moderate haze is usually the better compromise than maximum diffusion. If the official documentation gives neither a coating type nor a haze value, treat the marketing copy as unverifiable.

## What an add-on filter can and cannot do

A filter reduces the intensity and sharpness of the reflection reaching your eyes, which is a real benefit, but it cannot create brightness the panel does not have. Every filter sits in the light path, so the image dims and, with acrylic and PET especially, can soften slightly. Suppose a filter transmits 80 percent of the light: a display specified at 250 cd/m² behaves like one at 200 cd/m². That is arithmetic, not a product claim, and it is why you check your monitor's specified brightness first. If the panel is already near its limit in a sunny room, a filter pushes it into washed-out territory, and the honest answer is a brighter display.

Know which product you are looking at, too. Privacy filters cut glare as a side effect, but their job is narrowing the viewing angle and they darken the screen noticeably. A blue light filter changes spectral output and does nothing about a window reflection.

## Fit: the number on the box is not the number you need

Filters are sold by diagonal size and aspect ratio, which is not enough. What matters is the viewable area, the active image inside the bezel. A 27-inch 16:9 panel has an image diagonal of about 686 mm and a nominal image area of roughly 598 mm by 336 mm; your monitor's actual viewable area is smaller, because bezel widths vary between models. Measure the lit area across and down with a tape and compare it with the filter's stated viewable dimensions, not its diagonal.

Two traps are worth naming. Curved monitors need curved filters; a flat filter will not sit flush. And monitors with very thin or edge-to-edge bezels may not accept clip-on designs at all, because a clip needs somewhere to grip.

## Mounting: clips, adhesive, VESA, and what they block

Attachments come in three families. Adhesive strips are effectively permanent and can lift coating when removed. Bezel clips are removable and by far the most common, but they occupy the top and bottom edges, which is where webcams, ambient light sensors and sometimes microphones live. VESA mounting is the most secure and the most restrictive: it requires mounting holes on your monitor and a matching pattern. The VESA Flat Display Mounting Interface Standard defines the common 75 by 75 mm and 100 by 100 mm patterns, and fitting one usually means removing the stand or switching to a monitor arm, which adds cost and setup time. Walk a finger around the bezel and list what is mounted there before you order.

## Materials: acrylic, glass, and PET

Glass gives the best optical clarity of the three common materials, but it is heavier and it breaks. Acrylic is light and inexpensive and scratches easily, which matters more than it sounds, because a scratched filter scatters light in every direction and can look worse than the reflection you removed. PET is flexible and suits curved surfaces, with generally lower optical quality. A published hardness rating, such as a pencil hardness figure, is a better durability signal than the word premium.

## Claims to discount

"Reduces glare by 99 percent" means little without a test geometry, since glare depends on the angle of the light source and the measurement setup. Ask whether the figure comes from a defined method such as ASTM D1003 haze measurement and at which angles it was measured. If the documentation does not say, the number is a slogan. Universal fit is the other claim to discount: bezel width, curvature and aspect ratio vary enough between monitors that these filters rarely fit everything they claim to. Use the manufacturer's compatibility list or your own measurements.

## When replacing the panel is the better spend

If your monitor is old, dim, or has a glossy finish you have never liked, filtering it is a workaround for a purchase you are going to make anyway. Check coating type and haze in the datasheet before you buy. Typical brightness, not peak brightness, and native contrast are what determine whether a panel can fight a bright room; dynamic contrast ratios are marketing numbers and should be ignored. If you are shopping in that category, we currently list the ASUS VG27AQ3A among other 1440p models. I am not claiming its coating suits your room. The point is that coating type is verifiable in ASUS's own documentation before you spend, and a panel bought with that check done will beat a mediocre display with a filter screwed to it.

## Buying checklist

* Fix the geometry first: rotate the monitor, move it off the window axis, control the light behind your head.
* Identify the product type. Anti-glare, privacy and blue light filters solve different problems; only the first is likely to help here without further cost.
* Measure the lit image area in millimetres, width and height, and match it to the filter's stated viewable dimensions rather than its diagonal.
* Curved panel means curved filter. There is no workaround.
* Check bezel clearance, plus every sensor, camera and button on the top and bottom edges.
* Decide the mount before buying: adhesive is semi-permanent, clips block edges, VESA needs matching holes (75 by 75 mm or 100 by 100 mm) and may mean removing the stand.
* Note your monitor's specified brightness and assume the filter reduces it.
* Ask for the specification in writing: haze percentage with its test method, plus material hardness if published.
* Buy somewhere the return window is long enough to test for sparkle, colour shift and edge fit in your own room.
* If the display is on its way out anyway, put the money into a panel whose coating you have verified.
