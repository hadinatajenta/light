A desk looks like one purchase. It is really three: the top, the frame that holds it, and the hardware that joins them. Two desks can share the same width and the same height and still behave nothing alike after a year of use, because the difference is in the materials, not the size.

This guide covers what desk tops are actually made of, how the frame is built, and the specific details worth checking before you pay.

## Why the surface decides more than the finish

The top takes the load, the scratches, the heat from a mug, and the weight of whatever you lean on it. A finish hides all of that for the first month and then stops mattering.

Two things fail first on a cheap desk: the surface, where a thin decor layer lifts or a soft core dents under a monitor foot, and the joints, where a screw pulls out of a particleboard edge. Both come down to the material and how thick it is, not the colour or the wood grain.

If you want the layout to work as well as the materials, the [desk setup planning guide](/blog/how-to-plan-a-desk-setup-you-will-not-outgrow/) covers depth, height, and clearance.

## Laminate and melamine: the workhorse

Most office and home desks are a core board with a printed decor layer bonded to it. Melamine is the low-pressure version; high-pressure laminate (HPL) is the denser one you find on contract and laboratory furniture.

What to check:

- **Edge banding.** This is the weak point. A raw, unbanded edge swells when it meets a spill and feels rough on your forearm. Good desks band all four edges, including the underside.
- **Band thickness.** A 2 mm PVC band survives knocks; a thin paper-edge foil does not. This one number separates a desk that lasts from one that does not.
- **Surface texture.** A matte, slightly textured finish hides fingerprints and glides better under a mouse than a high-gloss one. Under a gloss top, every speck of dust shows.

For most people, a well-banded laminate over a dense core is the sensible default. It is dimensionally stable, wipes clean, and costs far less than solid wood.

## Engineered wood cores: MDF, HDF, and particleboard

The core under the laminate matters as much as the decor that covers it.

- **Particleboard** is chips bonded with resin. It is the cheapest and the weakest around screws: a leg that attaches with threaded inserts into particleboard can loosen if the desk is dragged. Fine for a light, fixed desk that stays put.
- **MDF** is ground fibre, denser and smoother, and it holds screws better. It is heavier and still moisture-sensitive at a raw edge.
- **HDF** is the denser MDF used for thin panels and, on some desks, the whole top. Good stiffness for its thickness.

All three swell if water reaches an unprotected edge, which is why edge banding and a sealed underside matter more than the marketing name for the core.

## Solid wood and bamboo

Solid wood is the most repairable top: it can be sanded and refinished, and it ages instead of delaminating. It is also the heaviest, the most prone to moving with humidity, and the most expensive.

- **Hardwood (oak, maple, beech)** resists dents better and moves less than softwood. A glued panel or butcher block is more stable than one wide board.
- **Softwood (pine)** dents and scratches easily and is best used under a mat.
- **Bamboo** is a grass, engineered into strips. It is hard, stable, and often more affordable than hardwood, with a strong straight grain.

Expect a few millimetres of seasonal movement across a solid top. That is normal, and it is why solid tops are usually fixed to a frame that allows a little movement rather than screwed tight at every point.

## Glass and metal tops

- **Tempered glass** is scratch-resistant and easy to clean, but loud, cold, and unforgiving of a dropped mug. It shows every fingerprint and needs a mat under a mouse. Confirm it is tempered; ordinary glass can shatter into dangerous shards.
- **Sheet metal** tops appear on industrial and some standing desks. They are tough and thin, and they dent where a drawer unit is dragged against the edge unless the edge is folded over.

Both are best treated as a style choice. Neither is the most practical surface for a heavy monitor and daily writing.

## Thickness, edges, and load

Thickness is a proxy for stiffness, and stiffness is what stops a desk sagging in the middle.

- A 25 mm top sags less than an 18 mm one over the same span, and much less than a 15 mm one.
- Past roughly 160 cm of unsupported span, add a stiffener or a centre leg, whatever the material.
- Ask for the stated load, then discount it. A rating of 80 kg usually includes the weight of the top itself.

Run a straight edge across the desk at mid-span and press. If it flexes noticeably now, it will flex with a monitor and your forearms on it later.

## The frame and the legs

The top is the visible half. The frame decides whether the desk wobbles.

- **Tube thickness and shape.** A square or oval steel tube resists twisting better than a thin round one of the same diameter.
- **Bracing.** A cross-brace or a full apron under the top stops side-to-side sway. Two bare legs and a top is the least stable layout.
- **Adjustable feet.** On an uneven floor, this is the difference between a wobble and a level surface.
- **Joint type.** Bolted steel frames last; dowels and cam locks into particleboard are the first thing to fail on a cheap desk.

On a height-adjustable frame, check the lowest and the highest height before buying, because a lift often cannot go as low as a fixed desk. The [standing desk guide for a small home office](/blog/how-to-choose-a-standing-desk-for-a-small-home-office/) walks through that range.

## What to check in the listing or the shop

1. Edge banding: material and thickness, on all edges.
2. Core material: particleboard, MDF, or HDF, and the top thickness in millimetres.
3. Stated load, and whether the weight of the top is included.
4. Frame bracing: cross-brace, apron, or none.
5. Adjustable feet, and the height range if the desk moves.
6. Warranty length, which is a fair proxy for how confident the maker is in the core.

If a listing gives none of these, that is itself the answer.

## A short checklist

- Judge the edge banding and the core before the finish.
- Prefer a matte, textured laminate over a high-gloss one.
- Choose a thicker top, 25 mm over 18 mm, if the span is wide.
- Match the frame to the job: bracing and bolted joints, not dowels.
- Ask for the load rating and discount it before you trust it.
- On any adjustable desk, confirm the lowest useful height, not just the tallest.

A desk is a surface and a structure. Buy the structure well and the surface can be refinished or covered; buy the surface and ignore the structure, and you will replace the whole thing.

## Sources and further reading

- ANSI/BIFMA X5.5, Desk and Table Products. The load, stability, and durability test standard office desks are commonly designed against; the load ratings in a listing usually trace back to it. The document is paid, so use a manufacturer's summary.
- ANSI/BIFMA G1, Ergonomics Guideline for Furniture Used in Office Work Spaces. Relevant to the surface height and thickness that follow from a chosen working height.
- OSHA, Computer Workstations eTool: Desks. Covers the free height under the desk and the surface thickness guidance, both of which depend on the top and the frame you choose.
- Manufacturers' technical data sheets for panel products, for thickness, density, and screw-holding values by core type.
