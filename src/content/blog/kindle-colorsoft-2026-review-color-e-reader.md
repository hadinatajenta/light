Amazon's second-generation Kindle Colorsoft went on sale in the US on October 28, and the reviews are in. This Kindle Colorsoft 2026 review pulls together what PCMag, Tom's Guide, The Shortcut, 9to5Toys, TechRadar, and The New York Times found, so you can decide whether a color Kindle deserves $289.99 of your money. The short version: the body is thinner and nicer, the screen improved a little at best, and the price went up $40.

## Quick answer

- The standard Colorsoft costs $289.99 with 16GB. The Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition costs $319.99 with 32GB. Both are $40 more than last year's list prices.
- It is thinner: 6.8mm against 7.8mm for the 2024 model.
- Text still shows at 300 ppi and color at 150 ppi. PCMag says the panel is unchanged, while Amazon calls it completely redesigned.
- Amazon rates the battery at up to 8 weeks, against 12 weeks for the $199.99 Paperwhite.
- The Signature Edition drops the old model's Qi wireless charging for a $59.99 pogo-pin dock.
- Buy it for comics, cookbooks, and color highlights. If you mostly read novels, the Paperwhite costs $90 less and lasts four weeks longer.

## Kindle Colorsoft price: $289.99, and yes, it went up

Amazon announced the new lineup on October 1, [as Android Authority reported](https://www.androidauthority.com/2026-kindle-3718066/). [Amazon's announcement](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/devices/new-kindle-lineup-2026) sets the Kindle Colorsoft price at $289.99 for 16GB in Graphite and $319.99 for the 32GB Signature Edition in Graphite or Fig. [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/tablets/ereaders/the-kindle-colorsoft-just-got-revamped-for-2026-heres-everything-you-need-to-know-about-amazons-color-e-reader) lists the Kids version at $299.99 with 12 months of Amazon Kids+, plus UK prices of £259.99 and £289.99.

You may have read that the price didn't change. That claim traces back to a Tom's Guide review published in June, months before this refresh, which priced the model it tested at $249. Amazon's own [2025 Colorsoft page](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/devices/kindle-colorsoft-expansion-first-color-kindle-for-kids) settles it: the 16GB model was $249.99, the Signature Edition $279.99, and the Kids model $269.99. So both adult models are up $40 at list, and the Kids model is up $30. Tom's Guide's October review, 9to5Toys, and The New York Times all report the same $40 jump.

Don't count on a cheap leftover either. TechRadar found Amazon raised the remaining 2024 stock to the same $289.99.

## What changed from the 2024 Colorsoft

Most of the change is in the body. TechRadar's spec table puts the new model at 6.8mm thick, down from 7.8mm, and [PCMag](https://www.pcmag.com/reviews/amazon-kindle-colorsoft-2026) lists 0.27 inches against 0.31. The standard model weighs 204g (7.2 ounces), down from 211g per TechRadar, though PCMag lists the old one at 7.7 ounces. The aluminum Signature Edition went the other way, from 219g to 229g.

The front is now flush, with no raised border around the screen. Amazon credits a "reverse stack" display construction, a light guide with nitride LEDs and micro-deflectors to cut stray light, and a thin optical coating for clarity. The power button moved from the bottom edge to the top right, which Tom's Guide and 9to5Toys both welcomed because the old spot was easy to press by accident. The Signature Edition adds Fig, a color PCMag describes as an almost burgundy purple. The standard model comes only in Graphite.

What stayed the same: the 7-inch size, the 300 and 150 ppi split, IPX8 waterproofing, and the 8-week battery rating. One small software gain is that you can now highlight in five colors, one more than before, [according to The Shortcut](https://www.theshortcut.com/p/kindle-colorsoft-2026-review).

The fix that matters most is quality control. [9to5Toys' Michael Bower](https://9to5toys.com/2026/10/28/review-the-kindle-colorsoft-signature-edition-is-finally-worthy-of-its-name/) had to exchange his 2024 Colorsoft over yellow banding along the bottom of the screen, and he saw no yellow tint on the new one.

## Color vs black and white: the screen trade-off

Color E Ink screens add a color filter layer over a black-and-white E Ink panel. The Kobo Libra Colour, for example, uses a panel called E Ink Kaleido 3, [per Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/kobos-new-ereaders-include-its-first-with-color-displays-040153210.html). That filter costs you two things.

The first is sharpness. PPI (pixels per inch) measures how tightly dots are packed, and higher means crisper text. The Colorsoft shows black-and-white text at 300 ppi but color at 150 ppi. [Tom's Guide's Tony Polanco](https://www.tomsguide.com/computing/e-readers/kindle-colorsoft-signature-edition-2026-review) calls the drop "noticeable but not egregious." If you want to know why density matters so much for reading, [our guide to resolution and scaling for text work](/blog/monitor-resolution-and-scaling-for-text-work-a-practical-guide/) covers it.

The second is a darker page. PCMag had to raise the front light to about half brightness to match a Paperwhite with its light off, and Polanco, who usually reads novels with the light off, turns it on for the Colorsoft. A front light lights the page from the front, while a tablet's backlight shines through the screen at you. The New York Times found Colorsoft text easier on the eyes than an iPad. If long reading sessions leave your eyes sore, [our eye strain guide](/blog/reducing-eye-strain-at-a-desk-distance-lighting-breaks/) has more fixes.

Color itself is gentle. PCMag counts 4,096 colors, versus millions on LCD and OLED tablets, and says the screen is "closer to newsprint than to a magazine." The Shortcut and the Times both noticed a slight blue tinge. You get two modes, Standard and Vivid: Tom's Guide prefers Standard for detail, and 9to5Toys prefers Vivid for punch.

## Comics, manga, magazines, and cookbooks on a color Kindle

Comics are readable but muted. PCMag read Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles: The Last Ronin and found the colors clear but not vibrant. Double-page spreads shrank speech bubbles to the edge of readability, and the large file took 10 to 20 seconds to open, though page turns were quick after that. Tom's Guide could make out fine detail in drawings but still prefers a 13-inch iPad Pro for comics. 9to5Toys found that comics with Amazon's Guided View, which zooms panel by panel, read far better than those without, so download a sample before buying.

Manga is mostly black and white, so it shows at the full 300 ppi. PCMag notes manga is usually printed at 7.17 by 5.04 inches, close to this screen, yet it still sends manga readers to the cheaper Paperwhite.

Magazines and cookbooks are where color earns its keep. The Shortcut liked covers and magazines in soft color. The Times reviewer used a color photo in a bread cookbook to check whether a loaf was underbaked. TechRadar suggests color-coded highlights for study notes.

The ceiling is clear, though. The Times says comics look more muted than on an iPad mini, and PCMag says glossy magazines still look better on an LCD tablet. If comics are your main diet, read [our iPad mini A20 Pro review](/blog/ipad-mini-a20-pro-review-oled/) before you decide. For handwritten notes, the Colorsoft has no stylus support; the 11-inch Kindle Scribe Colorsoft ($629.99, per PCMag) is the Kindle that does.

## Battery life and charging

Amazon rates the Colorsoft at up to 8 weeks, against 12 weeks for the Paperwhite and 6 for the basic Kindle. 9to5Toys points out that the estimate assumes 30 minutes of reading a day with wireless off and brightness at 13.

Real use varies a lot. Tom's Guide read about an hour a day with auto brightness on and went from 95% to 68% in two weeks. 9to5Toys read about an hour a night in airplane mode and charged once since unboxing. The New York Times read a few hours a day and needed to plug in after about five days, compared with every two weeks on past Kindles. [Gear Diary](https://geardiary.com/2026/11/06/amazon-kindle-colorsoft-e-reader-review/) still calls the battery stellar. The worst case reviewers reported was a charge every five days or so, and that came with hours of reading daily.

Charging changed on the Signature Edition. The 2024 version supported Qi wireless pads, and Tom's Guide confirms the new one doesn't. Instead, pogo pins on the back connect to Amazon's $59.99 Charging Dock and the $79.99 Page-Turn Cover, which adds physical buttons without needing its own battery, per 9to5Toys. TechRadar's explainer still lists wireless charging, but Amazon's spec list says pogo-pin dock charging. A USB-C port remains.

## Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition: what the extra $30 buys

The $30 step up gets you 32GB instead of 16GB, an aluminum body, an auto-adjusting front light, double-tap page turns, the Fig color, and dock and Page-Turn Cover support. Tom's Guide notes the standard model comes with ads, while the Signature Edition has none. Storage matters more than it sounds: TechRadar estimates 16GB holds 30 to 50 graphic novels and 32GB holds 60 to 100.

The trade-offs are small. It weighs 25g more than the standard model, it loses Qi charging, and 9to5Toys wishes it had a glass screen at this price instead of plastic.

Our take: if you're buying a Colorsoft at all, buy the Signature Edition. Ten percent more money gets you double the storage for the image-heavy books that justify color in the first place, and no ads.

## Where reviewers disagree

- **How much the screen improved.** The Shortcut calls it a "categorical improvement on Amazon's first color e-reader," and Tom's Guide sees slightly more vibrant color. PCMag says the screen and hardware are the same as before, and 9to5Toys saw no meaningful color difference beyond the banding fix.
- **Overall score.** The Shortcut gave it 4.5 out of 5 and called it the best e-reader to gift this holiday. PCMag gave it 3.5 and names the Paperwhite the best pick for most readers. Tom's Guide calls the Signature Edition "arguably the best color e-reader right now," but expensive.
- **Battery.** The Times recharged after about five days. Tom's Guide expects another month or so after its first two weeks.
- **Wireless charging.** TechRadar lists it for the Signature Edition. Amazon and Tom's Guide say it is a pogo-pin dock.

## Who should buy it, and who should skip

Buy it if your Kindle library already includes comics, graphic novels, cookbooks, or travel guides, or if you study and want color-coded highlights. It also suits anyone locked into Amazon's store; Polanco says Goodreads and Kindle store integration make it hard for him to leave. For most buyers in this group, the Signature Edition is the one to get, and Tom's Guide expects Amazon sales to take a little off the price.

Skip it if you mostly read novels. The $199.99 Paperwhite is PCMag's Editors' Choice, and The Shortcut tells strict book readers to buy it instead. Skip it too if you own a 2024 Colorsoft without screen problems: Gear Diary, 9to5Toys, and PCMag all see little reason to upgrade. If you read lots of comics, a tablet shows them far better, and [our iPad mini vs iPad Air comparison](/blog/ipad-mini-2026-vs-ipad-air-desk-fit/) helps you pick a size. On a tight budget, the $179.99 Kobo Clara Colour does color for $110 less.

## Kindle Colorsoft 2026 review: the verdict

The 2026 Colorsoft is a nicer object than the original: thinner, with a flush front, a better power button, and, from 9to5Toys' testing, no yellow banding. The color itself is still soft and the page still darker than a Paperwhite, and PCMag argues the panel hasn't really changed. At $289.99, a $40 increase, it remains the best color e-reader for people already invested in Kindle books, but only if color is something you'll use every week. Comic, cookbook, and highlighter fans should buy the Signature Edition. Everyone reading plain novels should save $90 and buy the Paperwhite.
