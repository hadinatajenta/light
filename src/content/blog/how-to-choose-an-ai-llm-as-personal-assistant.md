Most people adopt an AI assistant by signing up for whatever their employer already pays for, then discover weeks later that it has been summarising the wrong emails or quietly drafting replies in a tone they would never use. An assistant sits between you and your most sensitive information, so it is worth deciding what you actually need before you hand over access to your inbox.

## Start with the work you do

A person drowning in email, a person managing a crowded calendar, and a person writing long documents need different things. The most capable assistants now connect to thousands of applications and can handle reservations, form-filling, and customer service interactions on your behalf. Others focus on professional workflows: monitoring customer feedback, writing code, and deploying bug fixes. Write down the two or three tasks that consume the most of your week and let them drive the shortlist. If you mostly need help drafting text, you do not need an agent with browser control.

## Understand what an always-on agent actually is

The current generation of assistants runs in the cloud, not on your device. Some operate 24/7 on cloud virtual machines and keep working even when your phone and laptop are off. Others work independently in the background to handle complex task sequences based on long-term goals, rather than responding to individual commands. This is a genuine shift from the chatbot model. The assistant is not waiting for you to type something; it is already working. Before you choose one, decide whether you want that. A tool that acts while you sleep is powerful and also harder to supervise.

## Memory and privacy are the real trade-off

The usefulness of an assistant comes directly from what it remembers about you. Persistent memory enables personalisation across sessions and makes proactive behaviour possible. But research on frontier models shows that these systems can leak sensitive information in inappropriate contexts, and that these violations accumulate unpredictably across tasks. When an agent links to external apps, the data in its memory can seep into shared pools, exposing not isolated data points but the entire mosaic of a person's life. A casual chat about dietary preferences could later influence health insurance options, or a search for accessible restaurants could leak into salary negotiations, all without the user's awareness. Read the data-sharing terms carefully and prefer assistants that offer local processing options or encrypted memory.

## Hallucination is a structural limitation, not a bug

Agents are still built on models that generate plausible text rather than verified facts. Hallucinations are a major limitation that affects agent operation in real-world settings, leading to risk and loss. One study found that high unsafe success rates in tool-using agents are primarily driven by integrity leaks, where models hallucinate user identifiers to bypass mandatory authentication. This means an assistant might confidently take an action based on information it invented. The practical implication is to keep a human in the loop for anything involving money, identity, or irreversible changes. Use approval gates where the product offers them, and treat the assistant's output as a draft, not a decision.

## Set standing orders and quiet hours

The difference between a frustrating assistant and a useful one is often configuration. Put recurring preferences into the agent's instructions or settings so you are not repeating yourself. Set quiet hours, for example requesting no routine updates from 23:00 to 07:00. Ask for a daily operations brief covering completed tasks, blockers, and next steps. Some assistants let users define goals and preferences that the agent proactively assigns tasks against. One executive told it to protect dinnertime with his family, and the agent now flags meeting conflicts and suggests rescheduling. That kind of standing instruction is where the real value accumulates.

## What to ignore

Extremely long context windows sound impressive but rarely change daily work if the model still loses track of priorities. A very large app integration count matters less than whether the three apps you actually use are supported well. Voice interfaces are convenient for briefings but poor for reviewing sensitive content. Do not pay for autonomy you are not prepared to supervise.

## A simple decision order

Task fit first, then memory and privacy controls, then approval-gate options, then integrations, then price. If two assistants are equal on those points, choose the one with the clearer audit trail of what it has done on your behalf. That order keeps the risk where you can see it, and the convenience where you will feel it every day.

Choosing an AI assistant is really an exercise in deciding what you are willing to delegate. Get that right and almost any capable model will save you time. Get it wrong and the most autonomous agent on the market will still be the wrong one.
