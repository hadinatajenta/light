## Quick answer

The table gives the five placement targets that decide most desk setups.

| What to set | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Tweeter height | 120-140 cm off the floor, at seated ear height |
| Speaker spacing | Left-right gap equal to each speaker's distance to your ears |
| Side wall clearance | At least 110 cm each side, symmetrical |
| Front wall distance | Just clear of the rear port, or 110 cm or more |
| Listening distance | Arm's length to 2.5 m |

Most desk audio complaints are geometry complaints. Bass that booms on one track and vanishes on another, a centre image that drifts when you lean sideways, a vocal that seems to come from under the screen: those usually trace back to where the speakers sit relative to your ears, the desk and the walls. Most of the corrections are free. The harder part is that a small desk in a small room cannot satisfy every rule at once, so it helps to know which ones bend.

## What does the equilateral triangle mean on a desk?

Sonarworks gives the starting geometry: the distance between the left and right speakers equals the distance from each speaker to the back of your head. What matters is that all three sides stay equal, not that they hit a particular number. If your ears end up 75 cm from the plane of the speakers, set the speakers about 75 cm apart.

Sonarworks cites Carl Tatz's figure of 171.5 cm between nearfield monitors (speakers built for close listening rather than filling a room), but that number assumes a listening distance to match it. On a 140 cm desk the equal-sides rule matters more than the figure. Dynaudio states most nearfield monitors work best from arm's length out to about 2.5 m. Sit closer and you hear the drivers; sit further back and you hear mostly the room.

One requirement is not negotiable: the distance from the left speaker to your left ear must equal the distance from the right speaker to your right ear. That is what keeps both channels in phase and holds the centre image in place.

## How high should desk speakers sit?

Tweeters belong at seated ear height, typically quoted as 47-55 in (120-140 cm) from the floor. The range is wide because it depends on your seat height and torso length, so measure your own ear height rather than aiming at the middle of it; [chair sizing and seat depth](/blog/chair-seat-depth-and-height-office-chair-sizing/) decides where your ears land as much as the desk does.

Dynaudio's rule is to line up the point just below the tweeter cone with your eyes and ears, which sit on roughly the same axis when you are seated. A speaker left on the desk surface points at your chest, and tilting it down to aim at your ears is a shaky fix that risks toppling it.

The usual conflict is a display sitting where the tweeters want to be. When the monitor's own stand holds the screen high, the speakers get pushed down with it. A monitor arm that lets the display sit lower frees the space the tweeters need, and [choosing a monitor arm for a heavy ultrawide](/blog/how-to-choose-a-monitor-arm-for-a-heavy-ultrawide-monitor/) covers the weight and reach limits first.

## How far from the wall behind the desk?

Treat the front wall as a binary choice: almost against it, or at least 110 cm away. Sonarworks puts the trouble between roughly 38 cm and 110 cm, where a low-frequency front-wall reflection causes uneven bass through a cancellation effect called speaker-boundary interference, or SBIR. Against the wall, the reflection returns in phase with the direct sound and reinforces the low end; further out, it arrives late enough to cancel part of it instead.

Many monitors ship with boundary compensation settings, and the right one depends on placement. The table shows what each position does to the low end and what compensation it calls for.

| Placement | Bass effect | Compensation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Free-standing, away from walls | Flat | None |
| Near the front wall | Up to 6 dB lift | Bass cut |
| Near a front corner | Larger lift | Larger bass cut |

Rear-ported cabinets add one constraint. A port is the hole that tunes the bass, and a rear-ported model needs at least the port's diameter of clearance from the wall, usually 5-10 cm.

Side walls count too: at least 110 cm (44 in) from the nearest one, with both speakers equidistant from their respective walls. Dynaudio ties clearance to listening distance, suggesting free space of roughly one quarter to one third of the distance from the speaker front to your ears, which is 50-66 cm at a 2 m listening distance.

For a subwoofer, keep the crossover (the frequency where the sub takes over from the main speakers) at or below 100 Hz. Above that, Sonarworks warns, your ears can locate the sub and the stereo image suffers.

## Where should the speakers point?

Toe-in means rotating the speakers inward so that their acoustic axes (the centre line of each speaker's output) cross just behind your head rather than inside it. How far behind is contested: a studio-design forum post argues the axes should meet several inches behind the head and aim slightly outboard of the ears rather than straight at them. That is one discussion's claim, not a measurement I can verify, so test it rather than apply it.

A free test: play a mono recording and move your head from side to side. If the centre image swings with you, the angle is too narrow; if it smears instead of holding, the toe-in is too steep.

## Do you need stands, pads or panels?

A speaker sitting directly on a desk sends early reflections off the desk surface into your ears a fraction of a millisecond after the direct sound. Dynaudio presents desktop stands as the fix, since they decouple the speaker from the furniture and let you set the height. Any solid stand does some of that for free.

Where the desk cannot move and the speakers cannot come far enough off the wall, absorption is the next lever. A broadband panel has to sit between the speakers and the wall to reduce the reflection behind SBIR, so the JBER sound-absorbing panels belong on the front wall, not the side.

First reflection points on the side walls are the other candidate: the spots where a mirror on the wall, seen from your seat, would show a speaker. Panels there tighten the image, and a decorative option such as the TroyStudio broadband absorbing panels matters when the room is also a living space.

## Buying checklist

- Measure seated ear height before shopping. The tweeter has to reach it, and a speaker too short for your desk needs a stand in the budget.
- Measure the distance from the speaker fronts to the wall behind them before you commit to a cabinet depth or a rear port.
- Check the port. A rear-ported cabinet needs its own clearance from the wall, which limits how close you can sit it.
- Check which boundary compensation settings a monitor offers, and confirm the listening distance you will actually use.
- Check side wall clearance and symmetry before buying a wider desk. One speaker jammed into a corner is worse than both sitting equally out from their walls.
- Decide early whether the room or the layout gives way. If neither can move, [a desk upgrade planned around what already works](/blog/planning-a-desk-upgrade-without-replacing-everything/) beats fighting the room.

Treat headphones as a different monitoring path rather than a substitute for a working stereo setup. But when the desk is fixed, the walls are off limits and your ears cannot sit at the apex of an equal-sided triangle, headphones take the room out of the chain in a way no amount of toe-in will.
