## Measure the Void Before You Shop

A cabinet that fits under a desk is a geometry problem first and a storage purchase second. The under-desk zone has no standard size, and it is rarely empty: modesty panels, support beams, cable trays, and the crossbars on sit-stand frames all reduce the clear space you actually have.

Take two measurements before you open a browser tab. First, the clear height from the floor to the lowest obstruction under the desktop, which is the underside of the cable tray or beam if one hangs below the surface. Second, the horizontal gap between the desk legs or pedestals, measured at floor level and again at the height where the cabinet top would sit, because splayed legs narrow the opening as it rises.

Cabinets are sold in nominal sizes. Your desk is a specific one.

## Read the Spec Sheet, Not the Marketing Bullet

Manufacturer documentation is the only dependable source for dimensions, and three numbers are routinely softened in listings.

Height must include handles or knobs on the top edge and the casters. A cabinet listed at 64 cm might measure 66.5 cm with a top-mounted pull and casters fitted. Against 68 cm of measured clearance that leaves about 1.5 cm, and the margin shrinks if the unit sits on carpet where casters sink into the pile.

Depth comes in two versions: the cabinet body, and the body plus an open drawer. Handle protrusion adds to the first. For the second you need the drawer extension distance from the spec sheet, because a full-extension drawer nearly doubles the footprint while it is open.

Width has to clear your narrowest leg gap with a few centimetres spare so you can slide the cabinet in and out. A unit you must lift over a desk stretcher every time you vacuum is one you will stop moving.

Listings claiming a cabinet "fits under any desk" are noise. No cabinet does, because desks share no clearance standard.

## Drawers, Slides, and Load Limits

Slide type determines how the cabinet feels and how much floor space it needs. Ball-bearing slides run more smoothly and generally last longer than roller slides. Full-extension slides let you reach the back of a drawer but demand more clear space in front. If the cabinet faces a wall, a chair, or a radiator, measure that path first: a drawer that cannot open fully turns the back third of the cabinet into a dead zone.

Weight capacity appears per drawer or as a total. Exceeding it damages the slides, and a heavy top drawer on a light cabinet is a tip-over risk. Estimate your load honestly. A drawer of hanging letter files weighs more than the same volume of loose paper, and one shelf of manuals can exceed a budget cabinet's per-drawer rating.

File size is the other compatibility check. Letter, legal, and A4 files need different drawer depths, and a cabinet built for letter files will not accept legal folders however the rails are set. Adjustable rails help if you mix sizes; confirm they exist in the documentation rather than assuming.

## Stability, Locks, and the Certifications Worth Asking About

Two published standards do most of the useful work here. ANSI/BIFMA X5.9-2019 covers storage units and includes tests for stability, strength, and durability, so a cabinet tested to it is less likely to fail under load or tip. Ask for the test report in writing. "Commercial grade" is not a regulated term.

Locks are murkier, because there is no universal security rating for filing cabinets. ANSI/BHMA A156.5 covers auxiliary locks, so a lock citing that standard, or one with a documented pick-resistant cylinder, gives you something concrete to compare.

Look for anti-tip design too. Interlocking drawers that permit only one open at a time are the simplest version, and some cabinets include a bracket for anchoring to the desk or wall. If heavy files live in the top drawer, anchoring is worth the ten minutes.

Construction details are checkable. Steel thickness is quoted in gauge, where a lower number means thicker steel. Powder coating resists scratching better than paint, welded joints are generally stronger than bolted ones, and rolled or hemmed edges add rigidity while removing sharp corners. None of that shows up in a thumbnail, so it belongs in your shortlist criteria rather than your final comparison. Check warranty length and parts availability as well: a cabinet whose slides, locks, and casters can be reordered years later beats one that becomes landfill when a single slide fails.

## Height-Adjustable Desks and the Knee-Space Trade-Off

With a sit-stand desk, the cabinet has to clear the underside at the lowest setting and stay out of the frame's travel as it rises. Crossbars are the usual culprit, sitting lower than the desktop and cutting several centimetres from your available height.

Then there is the human factor. Any cabinet in the knee space reduces where your chair and legs can go. Mark the intended footprint on the floor with tape or a cardboard box and work at the desk for an hour before buying. You will learn whether your chair base collides with the cabinet during normal movement, and you can weigh a narrower cabinet with a compact chair against a wider cabinet with a cramped seat. If you are replacing the chair at the same time, check its base diameter, which is listed in the manufacturer's documentation rather than in product photos, against the gap you measured at the start.

Mobility adds its own variables. Casters raise the cabinet body, need clearance to swivel, and change stability, particularly with a loaded drawer open. On carpet they may sink and reduce the clearance you thought you had. Check caster load ratings and whether they lock, since a cabinet that rolls when you pull a drawer gets old quickly.

## Claims That Collapse Under a Question

- "Fits under any desk": measure your own clearance instead.
- "Heavy-duty": ask for a per-drawer weight capacity.
- "Commercial grade": ask whether it was tested to ANSI/BIFMA X5.9-2019.
- "Secure lock": ask for ANSI/BHMA A156.5 compliance or the cylinder type.
- "Easy assembly": ask whether tools are required and whether it ships assembled.

## Buying Checklist

- Measure under-desk clear height to the lowest obstruction: beam, modesty panel, or cable tray.
- Measure the leg or pedestal gap at floor level and at cabinet-top height.
- Note the floor type and whether casters will sink or need to lock.
- Download the spec sheet; confirm height including handles and casters.
- Confirm depth, handle protrusion, and the drawer extension distance.
- Check per-drawer weight capacity against what you actually store.
- Match drawer interior size to letter, legal, or A4 files; check for adjustable rails.
- Confirm slide type, ball-bearing or roller, and full or partial extension.
- Look for interlocking drawers and an anchor bracket.
- Ask for ANSI/BIFMA X5.9-2019 testing and any lock standard such as ANSI/BHMA A156.5.
- Check steel gauge, powder coating, and welded versus bolted construction.
- Check warranty length and whether slides, locks, and casters can be reordered.
- Tape out the footprint on the floor and sit at the desk before buying.

## Sources and further reading

- ANSI/BIFMA X5.9-2019, the storage unit standard covering stability, strength, and durability.
- ANSI/BHMA A156.5, the standard covering auxiliary locks.
- Manufacturer specification sheets and installation manuals, for dimensions, weight capacities, slide types, and clearance requirements.
